# Chapter 1. A Glass of Water

Regina Arcadian lay on the treatment bed with her eyes closed and told me how I ought to live.

"You need to get married, Nikki, dear," she said while I applied the second layer of acid to her face. "While you still have something to offer."

"Please don't move your lips," I said. "It'll run into the corner of your mouth, and it'll sting."

"I'm not moving them. I'm talking. Those are two different things."

Mrs. Arcadian is forty-six. Her husband owns three gas stations and half the slope above the reservoir. Her skin is good, dense, with pores she calls "a catastrophe," and with one real problem, a pigment spot on her left cheekbone that she doesn't notice. I see it every week. Every week she pays for a peel that won't help the spot, and every week I suggest something that will, and every week she says no, because it's "not in style."

This is my fourth year at the White Orchid salon. Before that I spent four years as an OR nurse in surgery at County General. The difference is simple. In the OR, someone says "retractor," and you hold the retractor. In the salon, someone tells you how to live, and you hold a smile. The smile pays three times as much.

"Done," I said. "Now I'll neutralize it. This'll feel cool."

"Did you know your hands are like ice?"

"I know."

"That's poor circulation. You need to join a gym. And cut back on sweets."

I looked at my gloved hands. My hands really are cold, always, for as long as I can remember. In the OR it never bothered anyone: everybody's cold in there, and through two pairs of gloves you can't feel it. In a salon it's a liability. Clients flinch.

---

I count everything in facials.

It's how I stay sane. One facial at the White Orchid comes to what I make in an hour of work after the salon takes its cut. Rent on my one-room apartment on Riverside Drive: forty facials a month. The loan for the kitchen Kyle picked out before he left, leaving me the kitchen and the loan: another twelve. Food: fifteen, if I don't buy anything I like. A gym membership, which I use twice a month so I can feel guilty: four.

Mrs. Arcadian left me a tip of one facial. That's generous. She always leaves one facial, and she always says I need to get married. I suspect the two are connected: she's paying me for the right to say it out loud.

After her came a man with an ingrown toenail, who'd been sent to me because "Nikki's the medical one," then two college girls for waxing, then lunch.

For lunch I ate cottage cheese out of a plastic tub and watched Ollie scroll through her phone behind the front desk.

"Nikki," said Ollie, without looking up. "Come here. You have to see this."

Ollie Sinclair is my best friend and the chattiest person in Highridge. She's twenty-four, she weighs a hundred and eight pounds, she eats eclairs and doesn't gain an ounce, and I forgave her for it a long time ago, because she's the only person at work who doesn't tell me how to live. She just tells me how everybody else lives.

"What is it?"

"Some guy in Granton. Holding a glass."

I went over. On the screen was a video shot in somebody's kitchen. A middle-aged man in an apron was holding a plain drinking glass of water in both hands. A digital thermometer lay next to it. A voice off camera, a woman's, very serious, was counting: "Two minutes fifty. Three minutes. Measure."

The man took his hands away. The woman lowered the thermometer probe into the water and held the display up to the camera.

"So?" I said.

"The water got colder," said Ollie. "He held it in his hands for three minutes, and it got colder. It should've warmed up. Hands are warm."

"There's a fridge off camera."

"Nikki. It's that handbook. The one everybody was posting about the spring before last. A million people have downloaded it already. People bend iron with their bare hands."

I remembered. The spring before last my feed was full of it for a week, and then of something else. Some people from some town, some market built over a weekend, some manual somebody put up for free. After that it was in the news all the time, but the boring kind of news: workshops, amendments to laws, a guy in somebody's building who sharpens knives by looking at them. I scrolled past. Back then I decided it was a cult or an ad, and in a year and a half I hadn't changed my mind. It's always a cult or an ad.

"Did you download it?"

"I downloaded it," said Ollie. "I held a glass. Mine warmed up a whole degree. So I can't do it."

"What about the control?"

"What control?"

"A second glass. One you just hold."

"Nikki, I get a twenty-minute lunch," said Ollie. "It's a bummer, is what it is. I'd like to be able to bend iron."

"What do you need to bend iron for?"

"I don't know," Ollie said honestly. "But I'd bend it."

I went back to my cottage cheese. And when Ollie went off to the stockroom, I opened the search bar on my phone and typed: "Reshaping Matter by Hand download."

---

That evening I came home, took off my shoes, took off my jacket, took off my jeans and stood in front of the mirror in the hallway. I do this every evening. It's a bad habit, worse than smoking. I know that, and I do it anyway.

The mirror is full-length, the landlady's, with a gilt frame, heavy as a slab. It hangs by the front door, so the first thing I see when I come home is myself. All of me.

I'm twenty-nine. Five foot six. A hundred and thirty-nine pounds, which is nine more than before the divorce and thirteen more than at twenty. Dark blond hair down to my shoulder blades, with split ends, because I'm an esthetician and I never have time to do anything for myself. My face is good, really good, I'm not embarrassed to say so: high cheekbones, my mother's, and my father's gray eyes. Clear skin. That's my job, and I do it.

Below that begins what I don't discuss with myself.

I had on a beige bra with thick foam padding. Underwear that lies for me. Without it I have a chest that Mom, when I was sixteen, called "never mind, at least your back won't hurt," and that Kyle, after three years of marriage, called "neat." I can't stand the word "neat" anymore. It's a word for handwriting and lawns.

Under the bra, my stomach. Not fat. I'm medical, I know what fat is. The normal stomach of a woman who has lived on rice cakes twice and twice gained it all back with interest. Low down, under the navel, a soft fold that doesn't go away with any amount of crunches, because it isn't muscle, it's fat, and it sits there not by my decision but by the decision of my hormones and my grandmother. And the hips. I have my mother's hips. That's when everything's fine in jeans, and out of jeans you can see where the body stockpiles reserves against a famine that has never once happened in my life.

My panties were gray cotton with a stretched-out waistband. Under a beige bra. Nothing matched. Matching is for women somebody sees.

I stood there a while. Turned sideways. Sucked in my stomach. Let it out.

"Ignatius," I said. "Don't look."

Ignatius is my goldfish. He lives in a round bowl on the little cabinet in the hallway. A round bowl for a goldfish is like foam padding for a chest: pretty and bad for you. A rectangular tank won't fit in the hallway. He looks at me every evening with one round eye and says nothing. He's the best man in my life.

I went to the kitchen, put the kettle on and opened the file.

---

The manual didn't begin the way I'd expected.

I thought it would be "Unlock the power within." Or formulas. But on the first page, in big letters, it said:

"Why, and When Not To. Read this page all the way through before you try anything. If you teach others, read it to them out loud."

And then, points. Simple, like the safety rules at a factory.

Before every action, say out loud what you want and where the edge is. Account for the cost: hunger, cold fingers. Never draw current without ground-fault protection. Without a ring, don't draw it at all: a hand is not a thimble.

And Rule Six.

"Don't touch the living. Never on a person or an animal, not even with consent, until you understand what you're doing. We don't understand. The one who did it first didn't understand. Below is a record from twenty years ago: a yeast dough that stopped rising. Do not repeat."

I read Rule Six twice. I looked at the record about the dough. Yeast. Single-celled fungi. Twenty years ago somebody tried to do something to a dough, and the yeast died. Well, of course it died. If you go poking into a cell without knowing what's in there, it dies. I knew that without any manual. I'd seen it on the operating table, the time an intern got confused about what you can cauterize and what you can't.

"Until you understand what you're doing."

I understand what a cell is, I thought. Not in great depth. But better than a baker does.

Then I thought that this was ridiculous. I'm sitting in my kitchen in panties with a stretched-out waistband, seriously debating whether I understand how to reshape living tissue by hand. As if that were possible at all. As if the glass in that video hadn't been standing right next to a fridge.

The kettle clicked off. I went to get glasses.

---

The experiment took up half a page in the manual, and I liked that. People who come up with a half-page experiment are at least not trying to sell you a course.

Two identical glasses. The same water, at room temperature, two hundred grams each. A thermometer. First you hold the first glass for three minutes, just holding it, not wanting anything. That's the control: how much your hands warm the water on their own. Then you hold the second one, also for three minutes, and want the water to get colder. Not think it: want it. Then you compare how much one warmed up and how much the other did.

I have a kitchen scale, and I have a digital meat thermometer, a probe on a wire and a display with one digit after the decimal point. I bought it for a duck on New Year's Eve, and I never did cook the duck, because I saw the New Year in alone, and nobody eats a duck alone.

I always keep water on the table in a filter pitcher, poured the night before, so it was at room temperature. I weighed out two hundred grams, twice, into two identical glasses from the same set. Stirred with the probe. Twenty-one point four degrees Celsius. In both.

The first glass. The control.

I picked it up in both hands and looked at the clock on the microwave. Three minutes is a long time when you're holding a glass and trying not to want anything. I thought about Mrs. Arcadian. About the kitchen loan. About needing to buy fish food for Ignatius.

Three minutes. I stirred and put the probe in.

Twenty-two point one.

It had been twenty-one point four, and it was twenty-two point one. Plus seven tenths. Even my icy hands had warmed the water. That's normal, that's physics: a palm at thirty-something degrees against water at twenty-one. Heat flows from warm to cold.

The second glass.

I picked it up the same way. And tried to want.

It turned out that wanting water to get colder is very hard. I don't know how you want a thing like that. I'd like Kyle to pay back half the loan. I'd like to have a chest nobody calls neat. But water is water. I have no feelings about it.

Then I thought about work.

When you do a peel, you have to stop the acid on the skin in time. You apply the neutralizer, and you want the reaction to stop. You don't think about it, you want it with your fingertips, because if you're ten seconds late there'll be a burn, and the client will come back with a complaint. I want that with my hands twenty times a week. I know what it's like.

So I wanted it that way. For the water in the glass to settle down. To stop running around. Like skin after the neutralizer: quiet, cool, done.

My fingers went cold. Not right away. After about a minute and a half, as if I were holding not a glass but a can out of the fridge. I decided I was imagining it, and I didn't let go.

Three minutes. Stirred. The probe.

Twenty-one point two.

I looked at the display. Then I took out the probe, wiped it off and stuck it back in.

Twenty-one point two.

It had been twenty-one point four, and it was twenty-one point two. Minus two tenths. My hands hadn't warmed the water. It had cooled.

I sat down on the stool and started working it out on a scrap of paper, the way they taught us in nursing school. Control: plus seven tenths. With wanting: minus two tenths. Difference: nine tenths of a degree. My hands should have warmed the water by seven tenths, and instead it got colder by two. So the warmth of my hands hadn't gone into the water, and something more had gone out of the water besides. Where to, I had no idea.

The manual had a little table, "What counts as error." One tenth: error. Two to three tenths: "look closely." Five tenths or more: "repeat three times; if it repeats, you have the ability."

I repeated it three times.

Fresh water, fresh glasses, control and wanting, from scratch every time. Minus eight tenths against the control. Minus nine. Minus a whole degree.

After the third time my fingers were as icy as in the OR at three in the morning, and my stomach felt as empty as if I hadn't eaten since breakfast. Though I'd had the cottage cheese. Afterward I worked it out: nine tenths of a degree per glass is less than one calorie. And yet I was as hungry as after a night shift. Either the manual is lying about the cost, or I'm not paying for the water, I'm paying for the wanting.

I opened the fridge, took out the can of sweetened condensed milk I keep "for emergencies," and ate four spoonfuls standing up. Condensed milk is as sweet as a verdict: no appeal. Then I realized what I was doing, and I laughed.

The manual had this written down. The section called "Cost." "Energy does not come from nowhere. At first you will pay with yourself: hunger, cold fingers. Eat before practice. Sweets work fastest, but don't overdo it."

I stood in my kitchen in my panties, with a spoonful of condensed milk, and understood that minus a degree against the control wasn't a fridge off camera. It was me.

---

Ignatius swam a lap of his bowl and stopped against the glass, looking at me.

"I know," I said. "I'm scared too."

I wasn't scared. I was interested, the way I hadn't been interested in anything since nursing school. Since the day they first showed us a section of skin under the microscope: the epidermis in layers like a stack of paper, living cells at the bottom, dead ones on top, flat, flaking off. I sat at the eyepiece longer than anyone, and the instructor said I would have made a good histologist. What I made was an OR nurse, and then an esthetician. But I remembered that slide.

Further on, the manual said what to try. Nonliving things first. A paper clip. A knife. Glass. The leather on a shoe: it hasn't been alive in a long time. Fabric. Stone.

And then, in small print, in a footnote, one of the authors had written: "The nonliving on the living: hair, nails and the top layer of skin are dead cells, keratin. Formally, Rule Six doesn't cover them. But hair roots and the nail bed are alive. If you're not sure where the line is, don't touch."

I read the footnote again and went to the bathroom.

---

Hair is keratin. A protein. Long strands twisted into spirals, spirals into bundles, bundles into a hair. On the outside a hair is covered with the cuticle, flat scales that overlap like roof shingles. When a hair is healthy, the shingles lie tight and shine. When the tip is dry and old, the scales lift, the fibers inside come apart, and the tip splits into two, into three, into a little broom. Split ends.

You can't glue a split end back together with anything. Not with oil, not with keratin ampoules, not with hot scissors. That's all marketing, and I sell it to clients myself, at a facial and a half per treatment. All you can do is cut it off.

That much I knew. I sell it.

I sat on the edge of the tub, took one strand, the most frazzled one, by my left ear, and looked at the tip under the light over the mirror. A broom. Five or six split hairs, each with about a centimeter of fork.

The manual said: "Say out loud what you want and where the edge is."

I felt awkward talking out loud in an empty bathroom. I said it quietly:

"Hair. The tip. Bring the fork back together. Edge: where the hair is still whole. Don't touch the roots. The roots are far away."

And I took the tip between my thumb and forefinger.

At first there was nothing. Just hair between my fingers, dry, prickly. Then, the way it happens when your eyes adjust to the dark, I started to feel it. Not with my fingers. Some other way, from inside my fingers. The hair was long, like a tunnel. Inside it ran threads, dense, parallel, and at the end they came apart, frayed, with air between them. The scales on the outside stuck up like the fur on a cat that's been petted the wrong way.

I thought about skin after the neutralizer. Quiet, cool, everything in its place.

And I wanted the threads to remember that they were one.

It was slow. The threads came together reluctantly, as if they couldn't be bothered. In some places they lay down side by side, in some they didn't. I held on for maybe three minutes. My fingers went cold again. My stomach went empty again.

Then I let go and looked under the light.

Of the six hairs, four were whole. Not glued: whole. The tip smooth, fine, shiny, like a child's hair. The other two were still forks, only the fork was shorter now, as if somebody had started to zip up a zipper and quit halfway.

I sat on the edge of the tub, looking at four whole hairs, and my hands were shaking. Not from the cold. From the fact that it was impossible, and I'd done it.

Then I went to the kitchen and ate three more spoonfuls of condensed milk.

---

By one in the morning I'd done the whole left side of my head. Not strand by strand: that way I'd have been at it till dawn. Toward the end I figured out that I could take a whole handful of hair at once, in my palm, and think not about each single hair but about all the "shingles" together: lie down, all of you lie down, all of you remember you're whole. It worked worse than with a single strand, but faster. Some of the tips stayed forked. I kept notes on a scrap of paper: roughly how long a handful took, and how much condensed milk.

The whole left side took the rest of the can, half a package of cookies and two hours.

The manual had a rule: every time you join something that has come apart, you pay more than when you bend. Mending a hair is joining. That's expensive. Bending is cheaper. If I'd just been straightening my hair, it probably would have cost less.

I did the math. A can of condensed milk is about twelve hundred calories. Half a package of cookies, another four hundred. Sixteen hundred calories in two hours. That's like running a half marathon. I have never in my life run a half marathon.

Then I realized something else and went to the scale in the bathroom.

That morning, before work, I'd weighed myself. A hundred thirty-nine point one. Now: a hundred thirty-eight. A pound in one evening. I know your weight drifts by a couple of pounds over a day. But I'd eaten a can of condensed milk, half a package of cookies and the cottage cheese, and I weighed less than I had in the morning.

In the evening you're always heavier than in the morning. So almost two pounds had gone. It couldn't be fat: two pounds of fat is about seven thousand calories, six cans of condensed milk. More likely I'd burned through my glycogen, the sugar the liver and muscles keep in reserve. It holds on to water, three times its own weight, and the water left along with it. Fat, maybe a hundred grams. But even a hundred grams is nine hundred calories I never ate.

Heat always belongs to someone, the manual said. Energy always comes from somewhere.

From the glass. From my hands. From the condensed milk.

And when there isn't enough condensed milk, from me.

---

I turned off the bathroom light and went back to the hallway, to the mirror. It was one in the morning. The left half of my head gleamed under the bulb like a shampoo commercial. The right half was my usual, with its little brooms. I looked like a before-and-after ad shot in a single frame.

I took off the bra. The foam went with it, and everything got honest.

I looked at myself for a long time. At the chest somebody had called neat. At the stomach with the fold under the navel. At the hips. And I wasn't thinking the way I usually do. Usually I think: "Oh, whatever." Or: "Starting Monday." Or: "At least I've got the face."

This time I was thinking the way I did in the OR, when the field is open in front of you and you have to understand what lies where.

Fat is not the enemy. Fat is a warehouse. Droplets of triglycerides in cells that look like little bubbles. The body stores energy there for a rainy day. You can't make fat disappear into nowhere: mass is conserved, it's even in the manual, in the section on energy. You can burn fat, and then it leaves through your breath, as carbon dioxide and water; anyone who's read about weight loss past the headline knows that. Or you can move it. Surgeons do. Fat grafting: they take fat from the stomach with a cannula, process it and put it where there isn't enough. Into the face. The buttocks. The breasts.

Tonight warmth had gone out of my hands, and out of me had gone all my reserve sugar and maybe a hundred grams of fat. No cannula. No surgeon.

I put my palm on my stomach, on the fold under the navel. It was warm and soft. Under the skin, right under the dermis, lay the bubbles: small, dense lobules near the surface, and deeper, under a thin layer of fascia, big lazy ones. The very ones that don't go away with any amount of crunches. I could almost feel them. Almost. Like the hair, once your eyes adjust to the dark.

And I snatched my hand away.

The manual lay on the kitchen table, open on my phone. I went and reread Rule Six. For the third time that evening.

"Don't touch the living. Never on a person or an animal, not even with consent, until you understand what you're doing."

Not even with consent. What about my consent? Is my own body "on a person" too?

Until you understand what you're doing.

For four years I held retractors over open abdomens. I know where the blood vessels run. I know what a fat embolism is and why you wear compression garments after liposuction. I've seen what necrosis looks like, and I know how it smells. I understand the living better than the people who wrote about the dough.

But I don't understand how it's done by hand. Neither do they. That's the truth.

I turned off my phone. Got into bed. The left half of my hair felt like somebody else's, smooth, like something off a store display. The right half was mine. Sleep didn't come. What came was a feeling you get in the OR before the first incision: everything sterile, everything ready, and any second now someone will say, "Let's begin."

"Not tonight," I said into the dark.

Ignatius in his bowl didn't answer. But he didn't object, either.
