# Chapter 2. The Nonliving on the Living

In the morning I pulled my hair back into a ponytail, tight, the way I used to before a shift, and decided nobody would notice anything.

Ollie noticed in forty seconds.

"Stop," she said as I walked past the front desk. "Turn around."

"I have a client at nine."

"You have a client at nine fifteen. I booked her. Turn around."

I turned around. Ollie walked all the way around me, the way you walk around a car on a used-car lot, and took my ponytail in her hand.

"Nikki," she said quietly. "Your left side looks like you just had a gloss treatment. And your right side doesn't."

"I'm testing a new product."

"On half your head?"

"So I have something to compare it to. A control."

Ollie looked at me. She never went to nursing school, but she'd heard the word "control" from me just yesterday, over my cottage cheese, and I could see her putting two and two together. Ollie is chatty, but she isn't dumb. That's the most dangerous combination there is.

"What product?"

"I'll tell you later."

"Nikki."

"Later," I said, and went into my treatment room, where it smelled of antiseptic and was calm.

---

I finished the right side at lunch, in the staff bathroom, sitting on the closed lid of the toilet. It isn't the most sterile place for scientific work, but it's the only place in the salon where the door locks from the inside.

I already knew how. A handful of hair in my palm, eyes closed, wait for my fingers to start seeing, and want the scales to lie down and the threads to remember that they're one. Edge: halfway down the length. The roots are far away. Don't touch the roots.

The right side took forty minutes instead of two hours. Faster, because I knew what I was looking for. And cheaper: I ate the eclair Ollie had absentmindedly left in the staff fridge, and half of my own lunch. Sixteen hundred calories yesterday, four hundred or so today. Understanding, it turns out, saves money. The manual said exactly that, only about steel: "The better you understand, the cheaper it gets." Yesterday I'd decided it was a pretty phrase. It's bookkeeping.

When I came out of the bathroom, Ollie was standing by the door holding an empty eclair box.

"Did you eat this?"

"I did."

"You don't eat sweets. You haven't eaten sweets in three years. You say sugar is 'glycated proteins in the dermis, premature aging, Ollie, put it down.'"

"Today I'm eating them."

Ollie looked at my hair, smooth on both sides now. Then at the box. Then at me.

"Tonight," she said. "My place. With wine. You're telling me everything."

"Ollie…"

"Or I tell Laurie you're selling your own product at work, off the books."

Laurie Victor owns the White Orchid. Off the books is the one thing she fires you for on the spot.

"I'm not selling anything."

"Yet," said Ollie. And went back to the front desk, pleased as a cat.

---

After lunch, Mrs. Stepp came in.

Galina Stepp is sixty-two. She comes in for a pedicure once a month, because her daughter gave her a package of visits as a gift, and because, in her words, "I've got heels like a camel, Nikki, honey. I could walk on hot coals." That's no exaggeration. Mrs. Stepp has hyperkeratosis. That's when the skin on the heels thickens, coarsens, yellows and cracks. The cracks are deep, sometimes deep enough to bleed. She worked thirty years on her feet, in the cafeteria at the steel mill, and her feet are the kind of feet you get when you've stood for thirty years.

Usually I do it like this: a soak, then a file, then the electric file, then cream. That's an hour of work, and in two weeks it all comes back. Because I remove the result, not the cause. The cause is the load, the age, and the fact that the skin on a heel protects itself by growing armor.

The hardened layer is dead cells, the same keratin as in hair, only flat and thick. Per the footnote, fair game.

I set Mrs. Stepp's feet in the foot basin of warm water and looked at her heels.

"Mrs. Stepp," I said. "Would you mind if I tried something different today? By hand. No electric file."

"By hand, like a massage?"

"Almost."

"Do what you want," she said. "Just don't let it hurt."

Not letting it hurt is the main thing. Under the dead layer lies a living one. If I go past the edge, it won't hurt. It'll be bad. Living cells of the epidermis, and under them the dermis, with its blood vessels and nerves. Off-limits.

I took one foot out of the water, dried it, laid it on a towel across my knee and wrapped both palms around the heel.

"Heel," I said quietly, almost without a sound. "The dead layer. Soften it and lift it off. Edge: where the living starts. Don't touch the living."

Mrs. Stepp looked at me with interest.

"What, are you praying?"

"It's a technique," I said. "That's how it's done."

My fingers adjusted faster than they had with the hair. The heel was like an old shoe sole: dry, hard and layered on the outside, with cracks; softer further in; and beyond that began something else entirely. Warm. Alive. I felt the difference as clearly as if somebody had drawn a line between them with a marker. The dead was quiet. The living stirred, in tiny movements, all the time, like water in a pot just before it boils. The cells were moving upward, toward the dead layer, and somewhere very deep down they were dividing.

I got scared. It was the first time I'd felt the living from the inside. It was like laying my hand on someone else's heart.

I pulled my attention back from the living, the way you pull your hand back from something hot, and went to work on the dead.

The dead didn't resist. The sheets of keratin held on to each other with protein rivets, and between them was a fatty cement, like the mortar between bricks. Skin knows how to let go of those rivets on its own: that's how it flakes. On a heel, it had forgotten how. I wanted the rivets to let go. For the sheets to come apart like wet mica. Not all of them: only the top ones. Not down to the living. Leave a thin layer, so the heel wouldn't be left bare.

My fingers went cold. But not the way they had yesterday. I suddenly realized it wasn't only my fingers going cold. The water in the basin was going cold too, where Mrs. Stepp's other foot was soaking.

"Oh," said Mrs. Stepp. "The water's gone cold. Add a little hot, would you?"

I added some from the kettle. And kept holding, without letting go of the heel.

Here's what I understood in that minute. Heat always belongs to someone. Yesterday, with my hair, I'd been taking it from myself: there was nothing warm in the bathroom except me. Today there was a basin of warm water right there, and the power took from it. On its own, without asking me. The way water runs downhill, heat apparently runs to wherever is closest and easiest.

A pedicure basin. About three liters of water at forty degrees Celsius. To cool it by five degrees, you have to take out about sixty kilojoules. That's fifteen calories, one square of chocolate. Not my chocolate.

The heel went soft. I lifted the top sheets off with a towel, the way you lift off wet paper. Underneath was pinkish skin, smooth, thin, but not bare. The cracks closed, because their edges were dead, and dead can be brought back together, like the tips of a hair. The deep crack near the edge, the one that had bled: that one I didn't touch. There was living tissue there.

I did the second heel faster.

Mrs. Stepp looked at her feet the way you look at feet somebody has lent you.

"Nikki, honey," she said. "What is this?"

"A new technique."

"Like my daughter's," said Mrs. Stepp. "Like my daughter's when she was twenty. How much do I owe you?"

"It's on your package."

Mrs. Stepp took her wallet out of her purse, pulled out two bills, folded them in quarters and tucked them into the pocket of my smock.

"That's not for the package," she said. "That's for you. Don't argue with an old woman."

Two facials. On top.

I didn't argue. Afterward I stood in my treatment room and looked at those two bills the way Ollie had looked at my ponytail that morning. They hadn't gone through the register. Laurie didn't know about them. It was my money for my work, for work nobody in the salon knew how to do except me.

And I had never been so ashamed of money I'd earned honestly.

---

By Friday I had eight facials on top. It could have been eleven.

Two from Mrs. Stepp. Three from a woman who came in with brittle nails: the nail plate is dead keratin too, and you can make it denser, like the tip of a hair, as long as you don't go under it, into the nail bed, where it's alive. Two from a college girl whose curly bangs I straightened in five minutes without a flat iron, and she squealed. One from Mrs. Arcadian: she rushed in without an appointment to "freshen up" before her husband's milestone birthday. All I asked was "Do you mind if I do it by hand?" She said, "Do whatever you want, just be quick," and I smoothed away the fine, dry flakes on her cheekbones. She said the peel was "finally working." I didn't touch the spot on her cheekbone. The spot is melanin in living cells. Living.

And three that don't exist, from a man with nail fungus.

To be precise, he wanted to pay three. I said no.

Fungus is alive. They aren't human cells, but they're living cells, growing in the nail and under it, in the bed. To get rid of it, you have to destroy something living. Even if what's living is the fungus and not the man, I didn't know where one ended and the other began. I didn't know what would happen to the nail bed if I went in there. I sent him to a dermatologist.

"But you can do it," he said. "I was told you can."

"Who told you?"

"Galina Stepp. My neighbor. She says you do it by hand."

I thought: that's it, I'm done for. Mrs. Stepp knows her whole building. Her building knows the whole neighborhood. In a week Laurie would know.

"I don't work on the living," I said. "Only on the dead."

"What's the difference?"

I looked at him. An ordinary man of fifty, tired, in a good shirt. He honestly didn't know.

"The nail you can see is dead," I said. "What's under it is alive. The fungus is in both places. If I go in where it's alive, I don't know what will happen. Maybe I'll cure it. Or maybe I'll hit the root, and the nail will grow back crooked, or not grow back at all. I don't know. So I can't."

"You decided that?"

"It's Rule Six," I said. "You can read it. It's free."

He went to the dermatologist. Without three facials for me. And I was as pleased about it as if I'd earned six.

---

Friday evening I sat in Ollie's kitchen. Ollie lives with her mom in a two-bedroom on Ridge Street, and her mom goes up to the cabin on weekends, so on Fridays Ollie's kitchen is the best bar in Highridge. Boxed wine, cheese from the discount store, grapes Ollie washes one at a time.

I told her everything. About the glass. About my hair. About the heels. About the eight facials. About the basin that goes cold, and about the fungus.

Ollie listened with her mouth open. Every so often she closed it to sip her wine, then opened it again.

"Show me," she said when I was done.

"Show you what?"

"Something. On me."

I looked at her. Ollie has beautiful skin, twenty-four years old, not a single pimple, and she doesn't even know how lucky she is. Fine blond hair, dyed three times this year, with ends that look like straw.

"Give me a strand."

Ollie held out her hair to me the way you hold out your hand to be kissed.

I did the ends. Not all of them: one strand, in about two minutes. Ollie touched the strand and said nothing. For Ollie, that's a lot.

"Nikki," she said, very quietly. "You're a witch."

"I'm an esthetician."

"You're a witch esthetician. That's better." She touched the strand. "What else can you do?"

"Heels."

"I don't want heels." Ollie sipped her wine. Then she gave me a sidelong look. "What about boobs?"

I set down my glass.

"No."

"Why not?"

"That's living, Ollie."

"So? You're a nurse. You know what's where in there."

"I know what's where in there when it's open and lying on the table," I said. "When the surgeon cuts and I hold the retractor. By hand, from the inside, I don't know. Nobody knows. It says so right in the manual: 'We don't understand.'"

"And if you figure it out?"

I didn't answer.

Ollie reached across the table and poured me more wine. That was her way of saying she wasn't going to let it go.

"You know what I thought when you were telling me?" she said. "About the basin. That the heat comes from whatever's close by. What if what's close by is a person?"

I hadn't thought about that. I'd thought about the basin.

"Then the person gets cold," I said slowly. "Mrs. Stepp had her other foot in the water. If there hadn't been any water, I'd probably have been taking it from her feet. Or from my own hands. From whatever was closest and warmest."

"Is that dangerous?"

"A little, no. Your feet get cold, that's all. A lot, yes. Tissue hypothermia. The blood vessels constrict, circulation drops."

Ollie shivered.

"So always put the basin out."

"So always put the basin out," I agreed. And I thought that this was the first rule I'd come up with myself. Not from the manual. From Mrs. Stepp's heel and Ollie's wine.

---

At home, late that night, I got on the scale.

A hundred thirty-six point seven. Tuesday night it had been a hundred thirty-eight. Down more than a pound in four days, after wine and cheese. And I'd been eating everything. Condensed milk, cookies, the eclair, Ollie's cheese. I'd eaten more sweets than in the whole previous month, and I was losing weight.

I took off my T-shirt and stood in front of the mirror. At first I liked what I saw. My stomach had gotten a little smaller. Just barely, almost invisible to the eye, but I could see it, because I've looked at that stomach every evening since the divorce. The fold under my navel was softer.

And then I looked higher and realized my chest had gotten smaller too.

Not by much. A spoonful, maybe, on each side. But my beige bra with the thick foam padding, which had always fit loose, now stood away from my body at the edge of the cup, and between the foam and my skin there was a gap half a fingernail wide.

I took that off too. And I laughed. Loudly, in the empty apartment, so that Ignatius darted to the far side of his bowl.

It's a law every woman knows if she's been on a diet even once. You lose it from your chest first. The body doesn't burn fat where you want it to; it burns it where it's easiest to give up. The fat low on the stomach and on the hips has more "brakes": receptors that hold on to the reserve until the very end. That's fat for a rainy day and for pregnancy; the body gives it up last. But a small breast is almost half fat, and every spoonful shows. I hadn't chosen where to pay from. My body paid the best way it knew.

I stood in front of the mirror in nothing but my panties. Tonight they were black, decent ones, my "Friday" pair; I'd put them on for Ollie's, though for Ollie's anything would have done. Under a beige bra. Nothing matched, again. I stood there and thought.

When I work, I spend myself. Heat, sugar, fat. The fat burns wherever it happens to. I'm not in control of where it comes from.

Not yet.

Because if I can feel where the dead turns into the living on Mrs. Stepp's heel, then I can feel where the fat lies under my own skin. I can want it to burn not from my chest but from under my navel. For the payment to come not from my whole body, any old way, but only from the fold I hate.

That isn't reshaping the living. That's just… choosing where to pay from.

I caught myself thinking it and smirked. It was a very convenient thought. Too convenient. That's how everybody thinks who wants to do something and is looking for a reason it's allowed.

Fat is alive. Adipocytes are living cells. Each one with its own nucleus, its own mitochondria, a capillary right beside it. If I start choosing which of them to burn, I'm touching the living.

Rule Six.

I turned off the light and went to bed.

---

On Saturday I went to the workshop.

In Highridge the workshop meets on Saturdays in the reading room of the county library, from twelve to three. I'd seen the notice on the library door a hundred times and walked past it a hundred times. "RESHAPING MATTER BY HAND. OPEN WORKSHOP. FREE. BRING YOUR OWN GLASS."

I brought my own glass.

There were about twenty people in the reading room. Retired ladies with their knitting, two college students, a man in a work jacket from the mill, a mom with a teenage daughter who kept staring at her phone. On the long tables stood glasses, thermometers, paper clips, spoons, pieces of wire. It smelled of old books and tea bags.

The workshop was run by Leo Gantz. Sixty-eight, a retired physics teacher, gray-haired, small, in a knit vest, with a voice that carried to the back row without any effort at all. Forty years in a school gives you a voice like that.

"New?" he asked. "Brought a glass? Wonderful. Have you read the first page?"

"Three times."

"You'll read it a fourth time now, out loud," said Mr. Gantz. "That's our rule. Everybody new reads the first page out loud. The whole thing."

I read it. My voice shook on Rule Six. Nobody noticed except Mr. Gantz. He noticed and said nothing.

Then came the glasses. Same as at home, only with a logbook, where Mr. Gantz wrote everybody down: name, control, with wanting, difference. The retired ladies got a tenth, plus or minus. The man from the mill got minus three tenths and was very proud of it. The teenage daughter got zero and went right back to her phone.

I got minus nine tenths against the control. Then, a second time, minus one.

Mr. Gantz looked at the logbook, then at me over his glasses.

"In a whole year," he said, "our best result was minus six tenths. Walt Sanders's"—he nodded at the man from the mill—"on a good day. What do you do for a living?"

"I'm an esthetician."

"And before that?"

"An OR nurse."

Mr. Gantz took off his glasses and wiped them on the hem of his vest.

"I see," he said. "Hands that know what's under the skin." He put his glasses back on. "Young lady, I'm going to tell you one thing, and I want you to remember it. Rule Six in the manual wasn't written for you. It was written for me, for Walt, for these wonderful women with their knitting. We don't know what's under the skin, and so we're forbidden. But you do know. That's why you need it more than anyone."

"Why?"

"Because you'll want to," said Mr. Gantz. "They won't."

Then he showed us a ring.

He kept it in a matchbox, on a wad of cotton. Thin, silvery copper, dull as an old wedding band. Mr. Gantz took it out with two fingers, the way you take out a splinter with tweezers.

"Sixth generation," he said. "Grown from a fifth, the fifth from a fourth, and so on, all the way back to the first one, in Granton. Each one is weaker than the last. This one's weak. But with it, last Saturday, Walt sharpened a knife in ten minutes instead of half an hour. I sign it out only to people who've passed the test, and only here, in this room. It doesn't go home with anybody."

"Why not?"

"Because there are very few of them," said Mr. Gantz. "And a great many people who want one. And not everybody who wants one is Walt."

Then he carried the ring along the rows on his palm: "Don't take it. Just bring your finger close." I brought my finger almost up against it and felt it. Not the way I'd felt the hair or the heel. The ring was warm. Not to the touch: from the inside. As if something in it were humming quietly, like a transformer on the other side of a wall.

The test, Mr. Gantz said, was in a month. If I came every Saturday.

A month. Four Saturdays.

---

When I came out of the library, a guy was smoking on the front steps.

About thirty, in a leather jacket, with a short haircut and very white teeth. He hadn't been at the workshop. I'd have remembered. He stood there smoking and watching the door the way vendors at a market watch shoppers: sizing up what each one is worth.

"Minus nine tenths?" he said as I walked past.

I stopped.

"How do you know that?"

"The old man's logbook sits right there on the table," he said. "And the girl with the phone has a camera." He smiled. His teeth weren't his own; they were too even. "Good result. Very good. Shame to wait a month for a sixth-generation ring they let you hold for three hours on Saturdays."

"Who are you?"

"Vic," he said. "Just Vic."

He reached into the inside pocket of his jacket and took out a little velvet box. The kind engagement rings come in. He opened it.

Inside lay a ring. Thin, silvery copper. Like Mr. Gantz's, but different. It hummed louder. I could feel it the way you feel a space heater that's switched on when you hold your palm up to it.

"Fifth," said Vic. "Not sixth. One step closer to Granton. No test, no signing for it. Yours. Wear it at home, wear it at work, wear it wherever you want."

"How much?"

"Three hundred."

He named the sum. I converted it into facials. Three hundred facials. Seven and a half months of rent. Two years of the loan for the kitchen cabinets Kyle picked out.

"That's a lot," I said.

"That's nothing," said Vic. "In six months it'll be a thousand. They don't make these in a factory. They grow them. And nobody wants to grow them: it takes forever, and you go hungry."

"Where did you get it?"

Vic smiled even wider.

"From Granton," he said. "Where else?"

He closed the box and put it back in his inside pocket. Then he took a business card out of the same pocket. White, no logo. Just a name and a number.

"Think it over," he said. "You've got hands that know what's under the skin. And with this"—he patted his pocket—"they'll know even more."

He tossed his cigarette butt into the trash can, nodded to me and went down the steps to a black car at the curb.

I stood on the library steps with the card in my hand. The wind off the mountains was cold, an October wind. My fingers, as always, were like ice.

Mr. Gantz had said, "Because you'll want to."

He was right. I wanted to.

I didn't throw the card away.
