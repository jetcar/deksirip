# Chapter 1. Minus Forty-Seven Thousand

I count money in pierogi.

Not because I'm some great pierogi enthusiast, although that too. It's just that "forty-seven thousand" is an abstraction, a fog, something from the world where people wear blazers and say "let's circle back." Two hundred forty-eight bags of frozen pierogi, on the other hand, are concrete. You can picture them. They're a closet. They're about eight months of life, if you're not picky and you don't splurge on sour cream.

That's how much I owed that morning. Two hundred forty-eight bags. Plus interest, which grew faster than I could keep track of: another half a bag every single day, as if somebody invisible came by at night and ate my future pierogi.

The phone rang at 8:03, while I was rolling up the shutter on my booth. My shutter squeals so badly that in three years the local pigeons have learned to take off in advance. I looked at the screen. "Don't Answer." That's what I'd saved Bull's number as.

I answered. That was my strategy: if you don't pick up, Bull starts texting, and he texts worse than he talks. His voice, at least, sometimes gets tired.

"Timothy," Bull said. He has the low, even voice of a man who has told people unpleasant things many times in his life and long ago stopped enjoying it. "Good morning."

"Morning," I said. "So far."

"You promised Tuesday."

"Is it Tuesday?"

"Timothy."

"I know it's Tuesday. I mean philosophically. It's Tuesday, sure, but whether I personally get a Tuesday is still an open question."

Bull was quiet. I could hear a radio playing behind him and somebody arguing about football.

"Fifteen at least," he said. "By Friday. Otherwise I pass you along, and there'll be other people. You understand me? Not me."

I understood. Bull was bad news, but, oddly enough, familiar news. Bad news you know on sight is practically a friend.

"By Friday," I said. "Fifteen."

He hung up. I rolled the shutter the rest of the way, the pigeons took off, and the Granton market looked at me with its usual October-morning face: wet asphalt, crates of cabbage, the sunflower-seed lady who is always set up before everyone else, as if nobody actually takes her home at night, they just throw a tarp over her.

Fifteen thousand by Friday. Eighty bags.

I sat down on my stool and started thinking the way I always think when there's no money: in a column.

---

I should explain who I am here, so the rest of this makes sense, including why I'm the way I am.

My name is Timothy Nagel, I'm thirty-two, and I fix things. It's all I know how to do, but I do it well. Watches, phones, irons, shoes, although shoes, strictly speaking, are Ashot's territory, and we'll get to that. Door locks. Umbrellas. Once I fixed a woman's dentures, but that was one time, and I swore never again. The sign over my booth says "We Fix Everything," and it's not advertising. It's a diagnosis.

Once upon a time I was studying to be a materials engineer. Three years of it. Then my father got sick, somebody had to work, and I dropped out, telling myself I'd go back in a year. My father died two years later, I never went back, and my mom moved in with her sister out in the country, and now she calls me on Sundays to ask whether I'm eating soup. I tell her I am. Pierogi count as soup if you drink the water they were boiled in. I've looked into it. It's a philosophically defensible position.

My debt came about the way all debts come about for people like me: not from luxury, but from a hole. When my father was sick, we needed money, and banks look at a guy in a market booth the way they look at the weather: with interest, but without trust. The payday loan came through in twelve minutes. Then there was a second one, to pay off the first. Then a third. Loans breed like rabbits if you don't separate them in time. It was the fastest and most expensive decision of my life.

I've been paying ever since. Honestly paying, every week. The problem is that the interest grows as if it were fixing something too, only in reverse.

---

Here's what the column came out to.

Income: on a good day, six or seven bags, net. On a bad day, two. Averaged over a week, if I don't get sick and don't get distracted by other people's dentures, about thirty.

Expenses: rent on the booth, the apartment, electric, phone, bus fare, food. If nothing breaks, twenty-five. But something always breaks, and with me, a man who fixes things, it breaks with special enthusiasm. You know how the cobbler's children go barefoot? I'm the cobbler's children of everything at once.

Remainder: five bags a week. Toward the debt. With interest at half a bag a day.

The math is simple enough to explain to a fifth-grader: if you pour five cups a week into a bucket and three and a half leak out, the bucket fills. But very slowly. And if you remember that by Friday you need to scoop eighty cups out of that bucket, it doesn't work at all.

I sat there for a while. I have a habit: when the column doesn't add up, I start looking for where it can give. Raise income: take more jobs. But there are only as many jobs as there are people breaking things, and people break things at a steady, almost scientific rate. Cut expenses: I already don't buy anything but pierogi. Sell something: I have a soldering station, a good set of screwdrivers and my father's watch. The watch isn't for sale. The station I need for work. The screwdrivers… nobody pays what screwdrivers are actually worth.

That, by the way, is an interesting thought, and I had it that day for the first time, though I didn't know yet that it would matter to me: why is a thing I can't work without worth so little to everybody else? My screwdrivers are half my income. Without them I can't fix anything. And I could sell them for a bag and a half. So the value of a thing and its price are two different numbers, and something lives in the gap between them that I didn't have a name for.

I wrote it down on the back of a receipt. I write down a lot of things on the backs of receipts. Then I lose them.

---

At nine, Mr. Abrams, who owns my booth, showed up. He comes once a month, like the moon, and with a similar effect on the tides of my mood.

"Timothy," he said, looking my booth over as if he were seeing it for the first time and didn't care for it. "Starting the first, your rent goes up twenty percent."

"Twenty?"

"Everything's going up."

"The booth isn't going up. The booth is the same. I was in it yesterday. It's exactly the same."

"Land is going up," Mr. Abrams said, in the tone of a man personally responsible for land prices and weary of the burden. "Word is they're going to reshape the whole market. If you want, you can move out."

I didn't want to move out. My booth sits in a good spot, between the entrance and the meat aisle, and people carrying broken things have to walk right past it. Location is value too, value that isn't in the price. Or rather, it is, but the one paying for it isn't the one creating it. The people going for meat make my spot a good spot, and Mr. Abrams gets the money for it. That was my second thought of the morning, and I wrote it on the back of the same receipt, under the first.

"Twenty," I repeated.

"Starting on the first."

He left. I redid the column. What was left for the debt came to three and a half bags a week. Exactly the amount leaking out of the bucket.

The bucket had stopped filling.

---

At ten, Ashot stole a customer from me.

Ashot Sarkisian runs the shoe-repair booth across from me, and we have a complicated relationship, roughly that of two cats on one windowsill. Officially, we split the market: he does shoes, I do everything else. In practice the border runs along the line called "heel," and there are constant skirmishes on either side of it.

A woman came by with a handbag. The handle had come off: the metal bracket had popped out of the leather. That's my job. Metal, rivet, press. But the woman stopped at Ashot's first, because the bag was leather, and Ashot is a man who looks like he knows everything about leather, including its past lives.

"I'll do it," said Ashot. "By this evening."

Afterward I watched him come across the aisle to me, holding the bag with two fingers like a dead fish.

"Tim," he said. "Put a rivet in. It's a minute's work."

"How much did you charge her?"

"What's the difference?"

"Ashot."

"Two bags," he said grudgingly. Ashot and I speak the same language. I infected him with pierogi about five years ago.

"And me?"

"Half a bag."

"Ashot, that's my work. That's metal."

"She came to me. I found the customer."

And right there I wanted to say something cutting, but it suddenly occurred to me that, on the whole, he was right. The customer hadn't come to him by accident. Ashot looks reliable. Ashot talks to people in a way that makes them believe him. Ashot knows how to haggle, whereas when I name a price, I start apologizing before I get to the end of the number. He found the customer, and I know how to set a rivet. Those are two different skills, and, to be honest, his skill is one I'm completely unskilled at.

It stung, and then right away I got interested. That was my third thought of the morning: maybe Ashot isn't stealing from me? Maybe he does what I can't, and I do what he can't, and the only question is who gets how much?

Still, half a bag was highway robbery. I said so.

"One," said Ashot.

"One," I agreed.

I set the rivet in forty seconds. Ashot handed the bag back to the woman, got two bags out of her plus a sweeping gesture of gratitude, and I got one bag and the feeling that somewhere, somehow, I wasn't getting something. On the receipt I wrote: "Ashot sells. I make. Which one matters more?" And underlined it. Then I looked at the receipt and realized there was no room left on it for the receipt.

---

By noon I had three jobs, a bag and a half, and the distinct feeling that life is a bucket I'm trying to fill with a teaspoon.

I was just finishing a sandwich (bread, a thin layer of butter, philosophy) when an old woman came up to the booth with a lamp.

I knew her by sight. Everyone at the market knew her. Tall, straight-backed, in a dark coat older than I am and a hat with a little veil, as if she were on her way not to the market but to the funeral of some very distinguished man, and the market just happened to be on the way. People said she was a professor's widow. People said the professor had been a genius, or a madman, or both, the way professors usually are. The professor had died in the spring. I'd seen the obituary on the board outside the Poly: small, framed, with a photo of a man with bushy eyebrows and the expression of someone who's just noticed something interesting behind the photographer.

"Young man," said the old woman. "You repair everything?"

"Almost," I said. "Except broken hearts and dentures."

"I don't need the latter," she said. "I have my own teeth. And the former you can't fix anyway. Nobody can."

She set the lamp on the counter. It was an old desk lamp: a heavy base of dark bronze, a gooseneck, a shade shaped like a little metal bell. It looked like something from the sixties, but made the way they made things in the sixties only for museums and senators. No screws on the outside. The base was solid, like a stone.

"It won't light," said the old woman. "My husband built it himself. For thirty years it burned on his desk. When he was gone, it stopped."

"That happens," I said. "Things get attached to their owners."

She looked at me closely through the veil. Her eyes were pale and very sharp, like a bird's when it's deciding whether you're a worm or just a twig.

"You think so?"

"No," I said honestly. "Usually the contacts are just corroded. But it sounds nice."

She smiled suddenly: briefly, with one side of her face.

"Bella Stern," she said. "Take it apart very carefully. Don't rush. I'll come by on Friday."

"It'll cost…"

"I'll pay," she said. "Don't worry about the money."

It was the strangest sentence I'd heard all year. No one had ever said "don't worry about the money" to me. People said "how much?!", "can you do it cheaper?" and "I'll think about it," after which they went off to think and, as far as I could tell, were still thinking.

She left, straight as a pencil, and I was left with the lamp.

---

The lamp turned out to be heavier than it looked. I hefted it: eleven pounds, at least. That's a lot for bronze, even at that size. I turned it over. On the bottom: no screws, no cover cap, no maker's plate. Just a thin, almost invisible line around the edge of the base. A seam.

At first I figured the base unscrewed, like a jar lid. I tried. It didn't turn. I tried to pry it with a knife. The knife wouldn't go in. I shone a flashlight into the seam and saw that it wasn't a seam, it was threading, very fine, and as far as I could tell, left-hand.

A left-hand thread on a lamp. Somebody very much didn't want it opened by accident.

I turned it the other way. The base came loose smoothly, with an oily whisper, as if it had been greased yesterday and not thirty years ago.

There were no wires inside.

That is, there were wires: they ran up from the plug to the socket. But not in the base. The base was empty, smooth, polished on the inside like a jewelry box. And right in the center, in a little nest lined with faded velvet, lay a thimble.

An ordinary-looking thimble. A tailor's thimble, with dimples. A little bigger than a woman's, just right for a man's finger. Dark metal, gray, or maybe bronze, or maybe black. It changed when I moved my head, like an oil slick on a puddle.

I reached for it. And felt the warmth before I touched it.

You know how when you hold your palm near a stove that was turned off an hour ago, and it still remembers the fire? Like that. The thimble was warm, and the warmth didn't seem to come from the metal so much as from inside me, as if I weren't reaching toward it at all but toward some place in myself I'd never noticed before.

I picked it up.

It was heavy. Not eleven-pounds-of-lamp heavy, but strangely heavy for its size, as if there were lead inside, only denser. I turned it over in my fingers. No markings. No trace of welding or casting. As if it hadn't been made but grown, the way crystals grow, or seashells, or fingernails.

Here's what I thought. A materials-science professor kept this thing on his desk for thirty years. Inside a lamp. Behind a left-hand thread. The man knew something. Otherwise why hide it? And I also thought that the widow who'd said "take it apart very carefully" and "don't worry about the money" knew something too. Maybe the lamp not lighting wasn't the real reason she'd brought it to me.

And then I thought this was all nonsense, that I was tired, that I owed two hundred forty-eight bags, fifteen thousand of it due by Friday, and that I had no time for the mysteries of old professors.

And I put the thimble on the middle finger of my right hand.

---

At first nothing happened.

Then the world took a step.

I don't know how else to describe it. As if I'd spent my whole life looking at everything through a fogged-up window, and someone had wiped it clean. Only not with my eyes. My eyes saw the same things: the booth, the counter, the lamp, my philosophy sandwich. But under that, deeper, through my hand, I suddenly started to feel.

The counter was wooden, and I felt the wood. Not the surface: the whole of the wood. The fibers, long as hair, packed tight, separated here and there, with bubbles of air between them, with the dead tunnels of a beetle that had been gone for twenty years. I felt it the way you feel your teeth with your tongue: without looking, but exactly.

The lamp was bronze, and bronze felt different: dense, grainy, like wet sand, only packed so hard it rang. The grains sat tight together, and in between them, in the base, I could feel little voids, pockmarks from the casting.

I sat there holding my hand over the counter, and I don't think I was breathing.

Then, very slowly, I picked a half-inch wrench up off the bench. Ordinary, chrome-plated, my father's. Cold.

The wrench was steel, and steel felt like something orderly and strict, built of identical cells like a honeycomb, if bees were engineers. I could feel where the chrome was on the outside, a thin shell, and where the steel was inside. I could feel a tiny crack by the jaw that I'd never known about, though I'd used that wrench for ten years.

And I thought (just thought, without words, the way you think "scratch my nose") that the crack ought to be closed.

The cells under my fingers stirred.

As if steel weren't hard at all, just very patient. As if all this time it had been waiting for somebody to ask. The edges of the crack reached toward each other, the cells rearranged themselves, closed up, and the crack was gone.

I looked at the wrench with my eyes. A wrench like any wrench. Only by the jaw, where the crack had been, the metal shone a little brighter.

I put the wrench down on the counter. Took off the thimble. The world stepped back, the window fogged up again, and the wood went back to being just a counter.

I sat there and very calmly, very rationally, thought about the fact that I'd lost my mind.

---

I should say here that I'm a practical person. When something doesn't work, I don't panic. I start testing. First the obvious. Then the not-so-obvious. Then the thing you don't believe, but you have to check, because you've already checked everything else.

The obvious: I'd lost my mind. Test: I remembered that it was Tuesday, that I owed Bull fifteen by Friday, that Ashot had taken a customer for two bags and given me one. Crazy people, as far as I know, don't keep track of details like that; they have other things on their minds. So, probably not.

The not-so-obvious: the thimble was coated with something, some hallucinogen absorbed through the skin. Test: I looked at my finger. My finger was fine. My head was clear. Even the sandwich was still a sandwich. If this was a hallucination, it was a very modest and very precise one: the hallucination of a man who dreams of fixing wrenches.

Third. The thing you don't believe.

I put the thimble on again.

The world stepped. Wood, bronze, steel. I picked up the wrench and thought that I wanted its handle to bend, just a little. Not much. A couple of degrees.

The steel flowed.

Slowly, softly, like modeling clay in a warm hand. The cells rearranged, shifted, the handle bent a couple of degrees, and I stopped. The wrench lay in my palm, warm (much warmer than it had been) and bent.

I took off the thimble, put it in my shirt pocket and buttoned the flap.

And then I did what any reasonable person does upon discovering supernatural powers in himself.

I went and bought myself a hand pie.

---

The hand pie was cabbage. I ate it without tasting it, then went back and bought a second one, potato. Then a third. I was hungry as if I hadn't eaten in a week: a sucking, ringing emptiness under my ribs. The woman behind the counter looked at me with respect.

Halfway through the third hand pie, it hit me.

Steel isn't modeling clay. To bend steel, you need force. To shift those cells, you need energy. I remembered this from college, vaguely, the way you remember a song whose words you've forgotten: bonds, lattice, activation energy. To reshape something, you first have to break something, and breaking is always expensive.

Where had the energy come from? The wrench got warm, so some of it had gone into heat. But the rest?

I looked at the hand pie in my hand.

Well, of course. From me. I'd paid for that bent wrench with myself. With my lunch. With my breakfast, if I'm honest, and, it seemed, part of my dinner.

It struck me as funny and a little scary. Funny, because it was the most bean-counter magic I'd ever heard of. No flashes, no spells, no ancient voices. Just: you want the wrench to bend, you pay in hand pies. Scary, because I didn't know what something big would cost. If a wrench is three hand pies, what does, say, a door cost? A car? If I tried to do something big, would there be enough of me?

I wrote on the back of a new receipt, because the old one was full:

"One: it works.

Two: it costs food. A lot.

Three: need to figure out what costs what.

Four: tell no one."

I thought about it and added:

"Five: especially not Ashot."

---

For the rest of the day I worked as usual. Well, almost.

I didn't put the thimble on. It sat in my shirt pocket, over my heart, and I could feel its warmth the way you feel a phone in your pocket that's about to ring. I changed watch batteries, soldered a wire in somebody's hair dryer, put a new tip on an umbrella (umbrellas have tips that wear down, believe it or not), and the whole time I was thinking.

Here's what I was thinking about.

If this was real (and it was real: the bent wrench was lying on the counter, and every five minutes I touched it to make sure), then I had the most valuable thing in Granton in my pocket. Maybe in the world. Maybe the most valuable thing that had ever belonged to a man who counts money in pierogi.

And the first thing that comes to mind, naturally, is gold. If I can reshape metal, then I can make gold. Sell it. Pay off the debt. Two hundred forty-eight bags in one evening. Buy myself a workshop, an apartment, send my mom a decent stove instead of the one that smokes.

I turned the idea over, and I liked it, and then, as always when I like something, I started looking for where it breaks.

First, what would I make the gold out of? The wrench didn't get bigger when I bent it. It didn't get lighter. So I'm not adding metal, just rearranging what's there. A steel wrench turns into a steel wrench of a different shape. Turning steel into gold isn't rearranging, it's swapping. I vaguely remembered that this was a whole different story, not chemistry but something from the section with the reactors and the people in white suits. Maybe the thimble could do that too. Maybe not. And if it could, how many hand pies would one gram cost?

Second, who would I sell the gold to? Me, the guy from the booth, walking into a pawn shop with a lump of gold with no hallmark, no receipt, no history? You don't have to be a lawyer to know how that ends. With questions. And I didn't have any answers I could say out loud without ending up in a very quiet room with very soft walls.

I wrote: "Gold — test it. But carefully. Hard to sell."

And then I thought about something else entirely. About the wrench.

The wrench had a crack. In a year or two it would have snapped. I'd have bought a new one for a bag and a half. Now the crack was gone, and the wrench would probably last another twenty years. I'd saved a bag and a half. Not earned. Saved.

But that's the same thing, if you think about it. Money I didn't spend is no different from money I got. A bag of pierogi I didn't buy, because I don't have to buy it, is a bag of pierogi in my pocket.

Only I can't reshape pierogi for myself. Pierogi are dough, filling and water, and for now I had no idea whether you could touch food with the thimble, or what would come of it. But everything else…

I looked around. Half the things in my booth were broken. The stool wobbled. The light over the counter flickered. The shutter squealed. The kettle leaked. The zipper on my jacket had split. At home it was even worse: the faucet dripped, the door wouldn't shut, the fridge droned like an airplane and ate electricity like an airplane.

Every one of those things cost money. Every one would need replacing someday. Every one was a little debt I hadn't taken out yet but already owed.

And every one, maybe, I could fix myself. Not for bags of pierogi. For hand pies.

I wrote: "Don't buy what you can make. Do the math." And underlined it twice.

---

At six I closed the booth. The shutter squealed its evening song, the pigeons took off. I stood there looking at it and thought that if I put the thimble on and went at it, it might stop squealing.

Not now. Right now there was the market, there were people. Ashot was closing up his booth across the aisle and eyeing me with the suspicion of a man who has spent twenty years at the market and knows what a neighbor looks like when something has happened to him.

"What's with you?" he asked.

"What do you mean?"

"You look like somebody gave you money today."

"Today my rent went up," I said. "And you stole a customer from me."

"Ah," said Ashot, reassured. "Okay, then you're fine."

He left. I stood by my closed booth, holding a bag with Mrs. Stern's lamp in it, with the warm thimble in the pocket over my heart. Three days left until Friday. The debt was two hundred forty-eight bags, plus half a bag a day, and if you looked at the column, nothing had changed.

But the column was old. The column only counted what I earn and spend. It had no line for "things I no longer have to buy." No line for "things I can make myself." And, come to think of it, no line for "things I can do for other people, if nobody but me knows how."

I didn't know yet how to count that. I didn't even know if it could be counted.

But I knew that tonight I'd go home, shut the door (which doesn't shut; I'd have to wedge a chair under the knob), put on the thimble and start testing. First the obvious. Then the not-so-obvious. Then the thing you don't believe.

And that first I'd buy pierogi. Two bags.

Better make it three. Just in case.
