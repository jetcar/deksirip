# Chapter 9. The Collector

On Wednesday I'd been forty-three short. Now, on Friday evening, I had forty-three. I was twenty-nine short.

I counted them three times: in the kitchen, on my iron chair, with the money laid out on the table in stacks, like a game of solitaire. Saturday, Monday, Wednesday, Thursday: eight apiece. Five from Mrs. Stern. Six from the market today, because it started raining after lunch, and when it rains people don't bring their broken things in; they stay home and break new ones. Forty-three altogether. Minus nothing. I honestly hadn't touched that money, not even when I really wanted a fried meat pie.

Forty-three out of seventy-two. More than half. But less than I needed.

I sat over the stacks and thought about how a week ago I'd had eight, and I'd been ashamed handing them over. Now I had five times that, and I was still ashamed. It was some kind of law that wasn't in any of Professor Stern's notebooks. Debt is set up so that you're always ashamed, no matter how much you bring. Because what you're bringing isn't what you have. It's what you're short.

On Thursday evening I'd been at the chemistry department with Val. This time I'd gotten off the bus. I spent two hours fixing the analytical balance, the one that had sat under its glass cover for five years. The thin steel knife-edge that the beam rocks on was damaged. I spent about forty minutes straightening it, slowly, one face at a time. Val sat next to me and watched. The balance was on the house, for the crystal, but Val still spent an hour telling me about the iron-carbon phase diagram anyway, "as an advance, so you'll have something to check your work against," and for the first time in my life I understood why one steel snaps and another one bends. It was the second-best evening I'd had in years, after Wednesday. And that was even without the kissing. Well, almost without: there was one kiss, at the door, before the security guard started rattling his keys.

But the balance didn't earn me a single bag. I'd known that going in; that was our deal. And still, on Friday evening, sitting over forty-three bags, I thought that if I'd charged money for the balance, I'd have a couple more bags right now.

And then I thought that if I had, I wouldn't have the iron-carbon diagram. Or the kiss at the door.

Some things don't convert into pierogi. I was starting to get that.

---

At eight-thirty a car came rumbling into the yard.

Not rumbling: coughing. I recognized the cough right away, even though I'd heard it only once, in the market parking lot. A hollow cough with a wheeze in it, as if an old chain-smoker had moved in under the hood. The cough came closer, stopped under my window, jerked twice and died.

Then it was quiet. And then I heard somebody trying to start the car again. The starter cranked and cranked and cranked. Nothing.

I looked out the window.

The old blue sedan was parked under the streetlight by the front door of the building. Its hood was up. Bull was standing beside it, looking into the engine with the face of a man who understands exactly what's wrong and can't do a thing about it.

I put the money in an envelope and went downstairs.

---

"Timothy," said Bull, without turning around. He'd recognized my footsteps, or he'd just been waiting for me. "I was on my way to see you."

"I figured."

"Wanted to talk someplace other than the market. At the market there's people." He shut the hood, not all the way, and turned around. "It's Friday."

"I know."

I held out the envelope. He took it, opened it and counted, with the same flick of his thumb as a week ago. Forty-three. He looked at me.

"That's more than I expected," he said.

"It's less than I owe."

"It's less than you owe," Bull agreed. He tucked the envelope into the inside pocket of his windbreaker and zipped it shut. "Timothy. On Monday I've got to turn your file in. Turn it in means I pass you along. To the recovery department. The guys who don't do conversations. I told you."

"You told me."

"I held off as long as I could. A week ago I told my bosses you were paying, that we should wait. Today my bosses said no more waiting." He paused. "I came to tell you in person. Not over the phone. Over the phone it's kind of…" He hunted for the word. "Not right."

I looked at him. At the windbreaker, the gray temples, the hands with the black rim under the nails. At the sedan with its hood up, which wouldn't start.

"Thanks," I said.

"For what?"

"For coming in person."

Bull looked at me in surprise. In three years at this job, apparently, nobody had ever thanked him for anything.

"You're a strange one," he said, and turned back to the car.

---

I asked him what was wrong with it. Not to be polite. It was an occupational disease. When something in front of me doesn't work, I can't not ask what's wrong with it. It's like doctors who can't walk past somebody coughing on the street.

Bull answered without looking at me. He talked the way mechanics talk: short, precise, no wasted words. Zero compression in cylinder two. Burned valve, probably, and the seat too. Maybe the piston rings as well, but that was a problem for later. The car had been running on three cylinders for going on three months and burning oil, and today it had finally given up. He knew all that. He'd been a mechanic himself: fifteen years at a repair shop, then a garage of his own, then…

He didn't finish.

"Then what?" I asked.

Bull was quiet for a long time. Then he said, looking into the open engine bay:

"Then I took out a loan. For my own shop. A lift, a diagnostic bench, a compressor, tools. Good location, out on the highway where you come into town. I ran it for three years. And then they built a big service center next door, with free coffee and couches. And people went there. Even though my work was better. I know for a fact it was better." He paused. "But the loan stayed. And the interest. And they offered me a job. The same people who'd given me the loan. Collecting. From people like me. Half my paycheck goes right back to them. They call that 'working it off.'"

He said it in a perfectly even voice, as if he were talking about the valve.

I stood under the streetlight next to the man who called me every week from the number "Don't Answer" and said, "Timothy, you promised." And all of a sudden I understood that he and I were the same thing. He was just three years ahead of me. Or three years behind, depending on how you looked at it.

"What would the repair cost?" I asked. "The valve, the seat?"

"Head's got to come off," said Bull. "Lap the valves, new valve, maybe a new seat. Parts, labor. Thirty bags or so, if we're counting your way. If I do it myself, fifteen. But I don't have fifteen. And I don't have time to pull the head, either; tomorrow morning I've got to be on the other side of town. I'll take the bus. What else am I going to do."

Thirty bags. Almost exactly what I was short of seventy-two.

I thought about it. Thought fast, the way you do when the decision has already shown up and your head hasn't signed off on it yet.

"George," I said. "Upstairs in my apartment I've got wrenches, a socket set, everything we'd need. I've been fixing things for twelve years. Cars aren't my line, but I know how an engine works. Let's pull the head right here. Right now."

"It's nighttime."

"There's a streetlight."

"You're kidding."

"No."

Bull gave me a long look. Then he looked at his watch. Then at the car.

"What's in it for you?" he asked.

It was exactly the right question. And I didn't have an answer for it right away. Then I did.

"You can give me something I don't have," I said. "Not money. Time. You said it yourself: Monday you pass me along. But it doesn't have to be Monday. It could be a month from now. You could do that, couldn't you?"

Bull didn't say anything.

"That's not allowed," he said finally.

"But could you?"

"I could," Bull said slowly. "If I write that the debtor's back on a schedule. That he's paying in installments. That there's grounds to wait. My bosses will believe it if I say so. But if next time you don't bring it, that's on me. They take it out of my pay."

"I'll bring it."

"Everybody says that."

"I'm not everybody," I said. "I'm about to fix your car. Watch, and then decide whether I'm everybody or not."

---

We had the cylinder head off in an hour.

It was the most fun I've ever had working. The moment my wrenches were in Bull's hands, he turned into a different man. He stopped being a debt collector. He was a mechanic again: fast, precise, grumpy. He knew every bolt in that engine by feel. He told me which wrench; I handed it over. He loosened bolts; I held the flashlight. We barely talked, and it felt good.

About halfway through, Mrs. Mazur came out into the yard with a pot.

"Timothy," she said, looking down at us from the front stoop. "Who's that?"

"George," I said. "He's… a friend of mine."

"Debt collector," said Bull, without looking up. He never lied about himself. I figured that out later.

Mrs. Mazur was quiet for a moment. Then she came down off the stoop and set the pot on the fender, next to the open engine bay.

"Eat," she said. "Both of you. They're cabbage. It's cold out. And they're not free," she added, looking at Bull. "Timothy's going to take a look at my iron on Monday. Again."

And she left.

Bull looked at the pot, then at me.

"She doesn't know me," he said.

"She feeds everybody," I said. "Even debt collectors."

We each ate three hand pies, standing by the open hood under the streetlight. They were the best hand pies Bull had ever eaten in his life; he said so himself. I believed him.

Then Mikey came out of the building. Hoodie, hood up, hands in his pockets. He stood there a while and looked at the car, at us, at the pot.

"Tim," he said. "Want me to hold the light?"

"Go ahead."

Mikey took the flashlight from me and stood next to us. He held the light exactly where it was needed, without moving, as if he'd been doing it all his life. Bull grunted approvingly.

---

The cylinder head lay on a sheet of newspaper spread out on the asphalt. Heavy, aluminum, still warm. Bull flipped it over and showed me cylinder two.

The exhaust valve had burned through. The face of the valve, the round metal cap that closes off the port, was scorched along the edge, and a half-moon chunk had been eaten out of it, as if somebody had taken a bite. The seat underneath, the ring the valve settles onto, was eaten away too, pitted. That was where the compression had been leaking out.

"There," said Bull. "Valve's shot. Seat needs to be machined or replaced. That's a machine shop. That's three days minimum. That's thirty bags."

I looked at the valve. Then at Bull. Then at Mikey, holding the flashlight.

And I made the decision I'd probably made back upstairs, looking out the window.

"George," I said. "I'm about to do something. Afterward you can tell me whether I did it right. But first, please, don't say anything. Okay?"

Bull gave me a suspicious look.

"What, you've got a stolen valve stashed up there?"

"No. Just watch."

Next to me, Mikey suddenly said, very quietly:

"Tim. I won't tell anybody."

"I know," I said. And Old Pete, who happened to be looking out of his second-floor window at that very moment, said clearly from above, "I know." All three of us looked up. The window shut. A minute later it opened again.

"It's the gas they sell nowadays," Old Pete informed the darkness. "They do it on purpose."

The window shut for good.

---

I put on the thimble. It had been in my pocket; I slipped it onto my right middle finger where Bull couldn't see. Then I set the thimble finger right on the face of the valve, so the nail was touching the seat as well, and laid my palm flat on the aluminum beside it.

Aluminum under my palm. Warm. I understood aluminum: soft, light, obliging, with a lattice a lot like copper's. The valve seat wasn't aluminum. It was a steel ring pressed into a pocket in the aluminum. Steel, but a different kind, hard, with things added to it that I didn't know by name but could feel with my hands. Something tough, like chrome, and something stubborn, like the tungsten in old drill bits. The valve itself was steel too, heat-resistant and even more stubborn.

For the first time, I was touching metal that had been made for one purpose: so that nobody could change it. To stand up to fire, pressure and impact, a thousand times a minute. It didn't want to change. It had been built not to want to.

It was going to be expensive. I felt that the moment I tried.

And then I thought about heat. The engine was still warm. Bull had driven all the way across town to get to me, and cast iron holds heat for a long time. The block, the head, the oil in the pan: all of it was warm. It was the heat of Bull's car. His gas, his drive.

"George," I said, without turning around. "May I take the heat from your engine? You don't need it right now anyway."

Bull was quiet for a long time.

"Take it," he said at last. In a completely bewildered voice. That's probably how you'd answer if somebody asked to borrow your own shadow.

I put my other hand, the left one, on the engine block (solid cast iron, warm, heavy) and took. Slowly. Holding the valve in my head: the professor's kind, not the engine's. Holding an exact picture in my head: the valve, round and true, with a smooth forty-five-degree face, like in the reference book. The seat: a ring, even, no pits, matching the valve like a lid on a jar.

There wasn't enough metal. What had burned away was gone; it had gone out the tailpipe, and there was no getting it back. I took a little steel from the valve's thick stem, where there was more than it needed, and moved it to the edge of the face. The stem got a little thinner. The face was whole. I meant to do the same thing with the seat: take steel from its outer edge, since that was hidden in the aluminum anyway.

"Leave the outside alone," Bull said suddenly, even though I'd asked him not to talk. "That's what holds the fit. If it drops out while you're driving, the engine's scrap. Take it off the bottom end; there's extra there."

I took it off the bottom end and moved it up to the working face.

The block under my left hand was cooling. I could feel the heat leaving the cast iron, leaving the oil. Under my fingers the iron beaded with moisture, like a bottle straight out of the fridge. Somewhere in the engine bay, cooling metal gave a sharp tick.

It took about forty minutes. Forty minutes that Bull and Mikey stood next to me, barely moving. All I could hear was their breathing and the traffic far off on the avenue.

When I took my hands away, the cylinder head was as cold as if the car had sat out all night in a hard freeze. And the valve and the seat were whole. Shiny. True.

I took off the thimble and put it in my pocket. I was shaking. The engine's heat had run out somewhere around the halfway point, and after that the power had been drawing on me; I'd felt it, the way you feel your wallet getting down to the last few bills. I finished every hand pie left in the pot and knew that at home I'd eat everything I could find. Heat-resistant steel. It resisted.

---

Bull bent over the cylinder head. He looked at the valve. He touched it with a finger. He turned the head toward the light, toward Mikey. He looked again. Then he took a little caliper out of his pocket (mechanics always have a caliper in a pocket somewhere) and measured the valve stem where he could get at it.

"Stem's thinner," he said. "Eight thousandths. It'll wobble in the guide. Burn oil."

"It already burns oil," I said. "You said so yourself."

Bull opened his mouth, closed it and looked at the face of the valve again.

"Yeah."

"And the face is whole."

"Yeah."

"That's…" He raised his eyes to me. "Timothy. What is this?"

I thought about what to say. And I decided not to lie. Not because I'm such an honest guy, but because Bull didn't lie. He'd told Mrs. Mazur "debt collector" when he could have said "a friend." He'd told me about his shop when he didn't have to. Lying to a man like that just seemed wrong. Same as with Mikey.

"I can move metal around," I said. "From one place to another. By hand. Don't ask me how; I don't really know myself yet. But I can."

Bull looked at me. Then at the valve. Then at me again. Then he sat down right on the curb, next to the cylinder head, and covered his face with his hands. I was afraid he was crying.

He wasn't crying. He was laughing.

He laughed quietly, without a sound, his shoulders shaking. Then he lifted his face, and I saw that his eyes were wet, but from laughing.

"Eighteen years," he said. "Eighteen years I lapped these valves by hand. With compound. Two hours a valve. I thought that's what skill was. And you… by hand…"

"I don't know engines the way you do," I said. "I'd never have figured out it was the valve. I couldn't have pulled the head in an hour. I wouldn't have known what angle the face was supposed to be if it weren't for the reference book. You know what to do. I know how. Those are two different things."

Bull gave me a very careful look.

"Two different things," he repeated slowly.

Next to us, Mikey switched the flashlight off and on again. Checking whether he was dreaming, I guess.

---

"Ought to have a new head gasket," said Bull, holding the old one up to the light. "Can't put this one back like this."

The gasket was steel, multilayer, and that I understood a lot better than the valve. I ran a finger along it and smoothed out the dents the old bolts had left, and Bull looked at me as if I'd just turned that newspaper into money right in front of him.

We had the head back on by eleven. Bull tightened the bolts in the proper sequence, crisscross, with a torque wrench out of his trunk, the way you're supposed to. Then he topped off the coolant from a jug, also out of his trunk. And the whole time he grumbled about my wrenches, which in his opinion were made of "soft no-name steel, good for nothing but opening cans." I didn't argue. I liked listening to him grumble.

Then he got behind the wheel and turned the key.

The sedan started on the first try. No cough. The engine ran smooth and quiet, as if it were five years old instead of twenty. Bull sat behind the wheel and listened. I could see his face through the windshield: he was listening to the engine the way Mrs. Stern had listened to the watch.

He shut off the engine and got out.

"How much?" he asked.

"A month," I said.

Bull looked at me.

"Timothy," he said. "You understand you just did fifteen bags' worth of work for me? If we're being honest, thirty. And for that you want a month. A month of my signature."

"Yes."

"That's not a fair trade. You're giving me more."

"No," I said. "You're giving me what I need more. I can make fifteen bags in two days. I can't make a month for any amount of money. A month means I stop being scared every Friday and start thinking."

He was quiet. So was I.

I remembered what I'd written in my notebook after Val: how can both people come out ahead? Today was the same thing. A valve for a month. Chemistry for repairs. Hand pies for an iron. I still didn't know why it worked. But this was the second time in a week I'd seen that it did.

Bull looked at me for a long time. Then he held out his hand.

"A month," he said. "I'll write that you're back on a schedule. A month from now: the twenty-nine you came up short, plus a month's interest. And after that, every week, no skipping. Can you do that?"

"I can."

"If you can't, it's on me."

"I know."

He shook my hand. His hand was hard and rough and covered in grease; we were both covered in grease.

"And listen, Timothy," he said, still holding on to my hand. "Be… careful. With this thing of yours. I've been in this system three years, on both sides of it. I know how it works. If anybody finds out you can do something like that, you won't be paying off a debt. You'll be the payment. You understand?"

"I understand."

"No, you don't," said Bull. "But you will." He let go of my hand and looked at Mikey. "And you, kid. You didn't see anything."

"I didn't see anything," said Mikey. "I was holding the light."

"Good man," said Bull.

He got into the car, started it on the first try (you could tell from his face how much he loved the way it started) and drove away. No cough.

---

I stood by the front door under the streetlight. Mikey stood next to me. Mrs. Mazur's pot sat empty on the curb.

"Tim," said Mikey. "Will you teach me?"

I looked at him.

"Teach you what?"

"You know… that. The valve thing."

I didn't know what to say. I didn't know myself whether it could be taught. Mrs. Stern couldn't do it. Val couldn't do it. Professor Stern wrote that it's like an ear for music. Some people have it, some don't.

"I don't know, Mikey," I said honestly. "I don't know if it can be taught."

"And if it can?"

"If it can, I'll teach you."

Mikey nodded the way people nod when they've gotten a promise and have no intention of forgetting it. Then he picked up Mrs. Mazur's empty pot and carried it into the building.

"I'll take it back to her," he said over his shoulder. "She'll like that."

---

Upstairs, I sat down at the table and stared for a long time at the empty spot where the stacks of money had been lying a few hours earlier.

Forty-three bags had gone to Bull. Until the next deadline: a month, twenty-nine bags, plus interest. If you thought about it, nothing had changed. The debt was the same. A little bigger, even: the interest.

But everything had changed.

Today I'd understood something I'd been feeling for a long time but couldn't put into words. I needed money a lot less often than I'd thought. Today I'd bought a month without paying a single bag for it. What money was actually for, then, I couldn't say yet. Rent, interest, Bull's bosses: they don't take payment in valves. But where the line was, I didn't know.

But the rest of the world ran on money, and I made money when I did something for other people. So here was the question: what could I do for other people that there'd be a lot of call for? Not fixing things one at a time, but making something everybody needed?

I opened my notebook to a clean page and wrote at the top: "What everybody needs."

And underneath it, nothing. For twelve years I'd been watching what people brought in, and it turned out I'd never once asked what they needed. That's not the same thing. I wrote just one word: "Knives?" And I realized that tomorrow I shouldn't be fixing things. I should be walking around asking.

Lugnut came over, sat down on the table and looked at my pen. Then he stretched out a paw toward its shiny cap.

"That's mine," I said.

Lugnut thought it over. He took his paw back.

He was learning too, it seemed.

At the bottom of the page I wrote one last line and underlined it twice:

"What if I fixed things so they didn't break again?"

Then I looked at it and realized that was a whole different job. Not repair. Something bigger. I didn't know yet what it was called.

But tomorrow was Saturday, and there would be people at the market. I could start.
