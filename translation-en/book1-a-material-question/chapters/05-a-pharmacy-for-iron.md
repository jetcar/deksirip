# Chapter 5. A Pharmacy for Iron

Bull showed up on Friday at 10:02. I know exactly, because I'd been watching the clock since eight.

I'd pictured him from his voice many times, and of course I'd pictured him wrong. On the phone Bull was huge and bald, in a leather jacket, with a chain around his neck and a habit of cracking his knuckles. In real life he turned out to be medium height, in an old windbreaker, with neatly trimmed graying temples and the tired face of an accountant whose quarterly report won't balance. The only thing I'd gotten right was the hands. Bull's hands were big and heavy, and under his nails was a black rim that no soap ever gets out. You only see that on people who work on cars.

"Timothy," he said, coming up to the counter.

"George," I said.

It was the first time we'd laid eyes on each other. It was a strange feeling, like meeting someone you've been writing to for a year and finding out he has a different voice. Only the other way around: I knew the voice, not the man.

I put an envelope on the counter. In the envelope were eight bags: everything I'd made on Wednesday, minus food, minus food again.

Bull opened the envelope and counted without taking the money out, with one flick of his thumb, the way people count who have counted a great deal of other people's money in their lives. Then he looked at me.

"What's this?"

"That's eight," I said. "Out of eighty."

"I can see it's eight."

"Wednesday was a good day. Thursday I wasn't in."

"And where were you Thursday?"

I thought about what to say. The truth went like this: "On Thursday I knocked out the power to my whole stairwell, got six grams of gold out of old military-surplus boards, couldn't sell it, and escaped from a scrap dealer through the wall of a garage." It was a very good truth, but Bull didn't look like a man who appreciates a good truth.

"I was sick," I said.

Bull gave me a long look. Then he looked at my counter. Then at me again.

"You weren't sick," he said. "You've got the face of a guy who hasn't slept. That's different."

"Being sick'll do that."

"Sure," Bull agreed. "A different kind of sick." He was quiet for a moment. "All right. A week. Next Friday. Eighty minus eight. If it's not there, I pass you along. I told you who to."

"You told me."

"Timothy," Bull said, and his voice went very quiet and not scary at all. "I don't like passing people along. Those guys don't do conversations. You understand me?"

"I understand."

He stuck the envelope in his pocket, turned around and left. I watched him walk down the aisle between the stalls, and I noticed that when he passed the meat counter he stopped for a second and looked at the price tag, the way people look who haven't bought anything for themselves in a long time.

Then he went out to the parking lot and got into an old blue sedan, and the sedan started on the third try, with a sound like somebody coughing inside it.

On a receipt I wrote: "Bull. One week. Mechanic's hands. Car coughs."

I don't know why I wrote that down. Habit, probably. When something coughs, a repairman makes a note of it.

---

Mrs. Stern came at eleven.

I'd fixed the lamp first thing that morning, before Bull, and it was, to be honest, the first repair in three days I'd done in a completely ordinary way, without any thimble. I wanted it like that. For some reason it seemed wrong to use that thing to fix the very object it had lived in.

The problem turned out to be nothing, just like I'd told her the first day: the contacts in the socket had corroded down to a green crust. I sanded them clean, bent the spring back up, checked the switch and the cord. The lamp came on with a warm yellow light, and I sat in the booth and just looked at that light for a few minutes. It was a very old kind of light. An incandescent bulb gives off something like candlelight, only more honest.

I screwed the base back on, of course. Left-hand thread. The little nest with the faded velvet inside was empty.

Mrs. Stern walked up to the booth, straight-backed, in her dark coat and the hat with the little veil. She looked at the lamp. I flicked the switch. The lamp came on.

"It works," said Mrs. Stern.

It didn't sound like a question. I can't explain it, but it sounded as if she wasn't talking about the lamp. Or not only about the lamp.

"The contacts," I said. "They'd corroded. I cleaned them up."

"Yes," she said. "The contacts."

She took her wallet out of her purse (old, leather, with a metal clasp) and put money on the counter. I looked at it and froze. It was five bags. For cleaning some contacts. I usually charged half a bag for that.

"Mrs. Stern, that's too much," I said. "It was ten minutes of work."

"But you took it apart carefully?" she asked.

I looked at her. She was looking at me through the veil with those pale, sharp bird's eyes.

"Carefully," I said.

"Well, there you are," she said. "Care is worth more than ten minutes."

The thimble was in my shirt pocket. I could feel its warmth. I knew that this was exactly the right moment. To say: "Mrs. Stern, there was something inside it. Here. Take it." To give it back. To go back to my own life, to the debt, the pierogi, Ashot, and the shutter that squeals.

I opened my mouth to say it.

"My husband," Mrs. Stern said suddenly, "always used to say one thing. He used to say: a thing should work, not sit around. He taught for forty years, and for thirty of them he told his students that, and they thought he was talking about lab equipment." She picked up the lamp in both hands and held it against her like a baby. "Thank you, Timothy."

And she left.

I stood there with my mouth still open. Ashot, across the aisle, had seen the whole thing and was looking at me like a man who had just sat through a very strange play and hadn't understood a single line.

"Five bags for a lamp?" he called across the aisle.

"Care," I said, "is worth more than ten minutes."

Ashot thought that over. You could see he liked it and was planning to use it on customers.

And I thought about something else. If Mrs. Stern had come an hour earlier, Bull would have gotten thirteen. But she came at eleven. That left seventy-two by next Friday, minus these five: sixty-seven. It sounded almost like a plan.

---

After lunch I closed the booth and took the bus to the Poly.

I had three reasons. The first was books. I needed to understand at least something about how the things I was touching were put together. Not with my hands; with my head. I needed reference books on metals, on crystals, on polymers, which I knew only looked like spaghetti. The second was Professor Arkin. If he was still teaching, maybe I could ask him a couple of questions. No details. Just casually, in passing.

The third reason was in my backpack, in a coffee can, wrapped in three plastic bags and a wool sock.

The lead ball.

I'd been thinking about it all night. About how it had glowed in the dark. About "something flew out." I remembered this vaguely, the way you remember a horror movie from when you were a kid. When atomic nuclei change, things can fly out of them. You can't catch it and you can't see it, but you can get it in your lungs or your bones. I didn't know whether any of that applied to my ball. But I did know that a cat lived in my apartment, and that out on the balcony, three feet from the ball, my socks were drying.

The chemistry department had to have a Geiger counter. Chemists always have a Geiger counter. At least one, at least an old one, at least in a cabinet somewhere.

---

The Poly was right where I'd left it twelve years ago, and it looked as if it had spent all twelve years waiting for me to come back and had gotten its feelings hurt. Peeling columns at the entrance. Steps worn down into hollows. A bulletin board with the same notices about rooms for rent, only the phone numbers were different. Professor Stern's obituary was still up on the board by the door: small, framed. I stopped in front of it.

"Ephraim B. Stern. Professor, Department of Solid-State Physics. Forty years of service to science." Bushy eyebrows. The expression of someone who's just noticed something interesting behind the photographer.

I looked at the photo, and it seemed to me he was looking straight at my shirt pocket.

"Well," I told him silently, "I didn't steal it. I was given it. I think."

Professor Stern didn't answer. Obituaries rarely do.

---

The Poly library was probably the cheapest store in Granton. I figured that out about ten minutes after I walked in.

Everything I needed was here. Whole shelves of it. A three-volume reference on physical metallurgy. *Crystal Physics*. *The Chemistry of High-Molecular-Weight Compounds*, which, as I worked out from the table of contents, was plastic: my spaghetti. *The Structure of Matter*. *The Properties of Wood*: that one I grabbed right away, in memory of the chair that was standing on three legs at my place now.

I walked along the shelves like a sick man in a pharmacy: for rust, volume three; for brittleness, *The Structure of Matter*; for cracks, the chapter on dislocations. A pharmacy for iron, except you write your own prescriptions.

And all of it cost zero.

The librarian, a middle-aged woman in a knit cardigan, made me a library card in five minutes, after I showed her my driver's license and my old student ID, which for some reason I still carried around in my backpack. She looked at the ID, at the last semester sticker, at the date, and raised her eyebrows.

"Twelve years?" she said. "Decided to come back?"

"Decided to read," I said.

"That's good," she said seriously. "Lots of people decide to come back. Hardly anybody decides to read."

I sat at a long table in the reading room, walled in by books, and thought about this. These books held knowledge people had paid for with years of their lives. Somebody had sat in a lab, measured, gotten it wrong, measured again, written it down. Hundreds of people, thousands of years of work. And I walked in off the street, showed an old student ID and got all of it for free.

And on top of that, when I finished reading a book, it wouldn't get any thinner. The next person could read it. And the one after that. Knowledge, unlike pierogi, doesn't run out when you use it. It doesn't even wear out, unlike my screwdrivers.

On a receipt I wrote: "Books: free. They don't run out. Why isn't anybody taking them?"

And I looked around. Besides me, there were four students in the reading room, and three of them were asleep.

I opened the metallurgy reference to the chapter on crystal lattices, and the next three hours went by like ten minutes.

---

It was strange. Twelve years ago I'd sat through these same lectures and understood nothing. Formulas, lattices, dislocations: it all sounded like a foreign language you're being forced to learn. And now I read, and every page answered back in my fingertips.

"Body-centered cubic lattice": I knew what that felt like. It was that strict army I'd felt in the steel spoon. "Face-centered": copper, silver, gold. Softer, more yielding; the cells are packed tighter, which is exactly why they bend more willingly. "Vacancy": an empty spot in the lattice, like a gap in the ranks where somebody didn't show up for formation. That was what I'd felt in the lead that night, around the speck. And a "dislocation" was a row that had slipped out of line: I'd felt those in every bent nail, I just hadn't known what they were called.

I read and thought: so that's what it's called. I'd been touching this with my hands without knowing the names. And now that I knew the names, I understood why it behaved the way it did. And once I understood why…

I put on the thimble. Right there in the reading room, under the table. I took a paper clip from the tray of office supplies and squeezed it between my fingers.

Steel. Body-centered. The lattice in the paper clip wasn't perfect: the wire had been drawn through a die, and the cells were stretched out lengthwise, like fibers. Before, I would have just felt that as "tight." Now I knew it was work hardening: the wire gets drawn, the grains stretch out, tangle up and hold each other in place. And I asked them to stretch out even more and lock together tighter, like in the spring wire from the reference book.

It took about three seconds. Hunger: almost none.

I bent the paper clip. It didn't bend. I let go, and it sprang back like a spring. Then, to check, I asked for the opposite: for the grains to even out and relax. The paper clip went limp as a cooked noodle. I knew ahead of time what would happen, and that's exactly what happened.

Before, something like that would have cost me a minute and a hand pie. Now that I understood what I was doing: three seconds and almost nothing.

I wrote it down, and my hand was shaking a little: "When you understand it, it's cheaper. Knowledge equals savings." Then I looked at the word "equals," crossed it out and wrote "is more than" above it, because "equals" was a lie. Knowledge didn't equal savings. It was more.

---

Professor Arkin wasn't in the solid-state physics department. The department secretary said he'd been retired for three years now, lived somewhere out of town and occasionally wrote papers nobody read. I got his address from her, "just in case," and went looking for a Geiger counter.

The chemistry department was on the third floor, at the end of a long hallway that smelled the way every chemistry hallway in the world smells: a little like vinegar, a little like rotten eggs, a little like something sweet and dangerous. The sign on the door read: INORGANIC CHEMISTRY LAB. NO UNAUTHORIZED ENTRY. Underneath, somebody had added in marker: "Authorized entry also discouraged."

I knocked.

"It's open!" a woman's voice called. "If you're here about a grade, I don't have it. If you're here for reagents, don't have those either. If you're here for the Geiger counter, I have it, but you can't have it."

I opened the door.

The lab was a long room with tall windows, fume hoods along one wall and lab benches along the other. On the benches were flasks, ring stands, burners and assorted instruments, many of which, in my professional opinion, hadn't worked in ten years. On the windowsill, a sickly cactus in an old reagent jar. In the corner, a broken analytical balance under a dusty glass dome, like a museum piece.

At the nearest bench sat a young woman writing something in a thick logbook. She was probably pushing thirty. A white lab coat with two small burn holes on the sleeve. Dark hair gathered at the back of her head and skewered with a pencil instead of a clip. Purple stains on the fingers of her right hand, from ink or from something worse. Thin-framed glasses had slid down to the tip of her nose, and she looked at me over them, the way teachers look at students who come in late.

"You're not a student," she said. "You're too old for a student, and too scared."

"I need a Geiger counter," I said.

"I just told you: you can't have it."

"What if I really need it?"

"Everybody really needs it. Day before yesterday a freshman came in to test his phone, because he read online that phones irradiate your brain. I tested it. The phone was fine. The brain wasn't, but this thing doesn't pick that up." She took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "What've you got?"

I took off my backpack, took out the coffee can, took the sock out of the can, the bags out of the sock, the lead ball out of the bags. I put it on the bench.

She looked at the ball, then at the sock, then at me.

"That's lead," she said.

"Yes."

"Lead isn't radioactive. Well, barely. You've brought me a lead fishing sinker in a sock."

"I think there's something in it."

"Like what?"

I didn't say anything for a moment.

"I don't know. It glowed. At night. Just a tiny bit."

She narrowed her eyes. You could see she'd been about to say something cutting, but she didn't. Instead she got up, went to a cabinet by the wall, unlocked it with a key on a long string she wore around her neck and took out a yellow instrument with a long probe on a cord. Old, clearly from the last century, but, judging by the way she held it, in working order.

She switched it on. It started to crackle: quietly, sparsely, a click every second or two.

"That's background," she said. "Cosmic rays, the walls, the granite in the foundation. Same as always."

She held the probe up to the ball.

The crackling sped up. Not by much: the clicks came faster, about twice as fast. She looked at the needle, then at me, then at the needle again.

"Well, now," she said.

"Is that bad?"

"It's not dangerous," she said slowly. "About like an old watch with glow-in-the-dark hands. I wouldn't carry it around in my pocket, but there's no need to faint, either." She pulled the probe away and brought it back. "But lead doesn't read like that. Where's it from?"

"My father's tackle box."

"Did your father fish in the cooling pond of a nuclear plant?"

"No."

"Then where's it from?"

I looked at her. She had a very smart face. Not in the sense of pretty, though it was pretty too, but in the sense that something behind it was running all the time. Like a booth with the soldering iron left plugged in. She looked at me, at the ball, at the sock, and I could see theories clicking through her head one after another and getting thrown out one after another.

"I don't know," I said.

"You're lying," she said, without any anger. Just as a fact.

"I'm lying," I agreed.

She smiled suddenly. Briefly, with one corner of her mouth, and it looked nothing like Mrs. Stern's smile, but for some reason it reminded me of it.

"Okay," she said. "I'll keep the ball here. In the lead safe. Not because it's dangerous, but because it's interesting, and you, judging by the sock, have nowhere to keep it. In a week I'll measure it again and we'll see whether it cools down. If it cools down fast, that means one thing. If it's slow, something else." She picked up the ball with tweezers and put it in a small, heavy container. "Valerie."

"What?"

"My name is Valerie. Val's fine. Lab tech. Local queen of test tubes and cacti. And you?"

"Timothy. Tim's fine. We Fix Everything."

"We Fix Everything," she repeated. "Is that a profession or a philosophy?"

"It's a sign. You hang it up when there's nothing else you know how to do."

She laughed. A real laugh, loud, with her head thrown back. The pencil in her hair wobbled.

---

And then I did something stupid.

Val turned away to put the container in the safe, and with nothing better to do, I looked at her bench. Next to the logbook stood a beaker of blue liquid. A thread hung over the rim of the beaker, tied to a pencil, and on the thread, down in the liquid, dangled a tiny blue crystal, about the size of my pinkie nail.

Copper sulfate. I recognized it right away. In sixth grade, every kid in the country grows copper sulfate on a string. I grew some too, on the windowsill, for two weeks, and I ended up with a crystal the size of a pea, while Kevin Sommers got one the size of a fist, because Kevin added something secret to his and never told anyone. I was terribly jealous of him back then.

I remembered how copper sulfate grows. It was probably the only thing in chemistry I remembered with my hands. A solution holding more salt than it can hold on to. The salt wants to come out. All it needs is a place to come out onto: a seed, a little crystal on a string. And then the molecules, one by one, fall into line on it, layer after layer, and the crystal grows. Slowly. Over weeks.

The salt wants to come out. That was the main thing. It doesn't have to break anything. It just has to be allowed.

The thimble was on my finger: I'd never taken it off after the reading room. I reached out and, I don't know why (out of the same childhood jealousy of Kevin Sommers, probably), dipped the rim of the thimble into the solution, right where the thread hung.

The solution. Warm, supersaturated, full of blue molecules jostling each other and wanting to fall into line. And the little crystal on the thread, small, lopsided, with one crooked face.

I thought: "Go on. You know you want to."

And they wanted to.

It was nothing like metal, where I had to pull and ask. It was like opening a floodgate. The molecules rushed at the little crystal all at once, like a crowd at a bus stop when the last bus pulls in. The crystal grew (I could see it with my own eyes), grew in faces, flat, shining, deep blue, with sharp edges. The solution got paler right in front of me. In one second, maybe two, there was a crystal on the thread the size of a big cherry. Not Kevin Sommers's fist. But in a second, not two weeks. Perfect, slightly slanted, the way copper sulfate is supposed to be, clear as blue glass.

Hunger: almost none, as if I'd eaten one sunflower seed. Out of habit, the thimble pulled some extra, but there was almost nothing to pull: the solution was doing the work itself. If anything, the solution around the thimble had gotten warm. I wasn't heating it; it was doing that on its own. As the crystal grew, it gave off heat. All at once I understood: this had cost me almost nothing because it wanted to happen anyway. I hadn't broken anything. I'd just allowed it.

I pulled the thimble out wet, with a blue drop on the rim.

Val shut the safe and turned around.

She looked at the beaker.

I pulled my hand back. Too late.

She came over. Bent down. Looked at the crystal from one side. From the other. Touched the thread. Looked at the solution: it had gone noticeably paler, from deep blue to sky blue. Then she looked at me.

I'd never in my life seen an expression like the one on her face. It was a mix of fury, delight, disbelief and hunger. Not the kind of hunger I get after welding: another kind. The hunger of a person who has waited her whole life to see something impossible, and has just seen it.

"I've been growing that for three weeks," she said quietly.

"Sorry," I said.

"It was the size of a fingernail."

"I know."

"How?"

"I don't know."

"You're lying."

"I'm lying."

She straightened up. Took off her glasses. Set them on the bench very carefully, the way you set down something you don't want to break by accident right now.

"Listen, Mr. Fix-Everything," she said. "I don't know how you did that. Swapped the beaker while my back was turned? Sleight of hand? I've been in chemistry ten years; I've seen a lot of tricks. Students swap test tubes during lab exams so smoothly any magician would be jealous. But I'll tell you this." She jabbed a purple-stained finger at me. "The solution got paler. The copper went into the crystal. That's not a swap: I made this solution myself, I know its color. And copper sulfate doesn't grow that fast. It just doesn't grow like that. Ever. Under any conditions. So either I've lost my mind, or you're about to do that again."

"I can't do it again," I said. "This solution doesn't want to anymore. Everything extra is already in the crystal."

"I'll make a new one," she said. "Make it, filter it, cool it, grow a seed crystal: three days or so. Wednesday, at six, when the department's gone home. You'll come and do it again, in front of me, with my solution, my beaker, on my camera. And if it's a trick, I'm turning you over to the circus. And if it isn't…"

She didn't finish.

"And if it isn't?" I asked.

She looked at me for a long time. Her eyes were gray and very attentive, and right now there was something in them I'd seen in Mrs. Stern, in Ashot, in Mikey. Suspicion. Only in Val's there was something else mixed in. Something that didn't look like suspicion at all and looked a lot like interest. Not in the crystal. In me.

"Then I'm turning you over to science," she said. "That's worse than the circus. At least the circus feeds you."

"What if I don't come?"

"You'll come," said Val. "I've got your lead ball in my safe."

---

It was already getting dark when I left the Poly. In my backpack were four books: the metallurgy reference, *Crystal Physics*, *The Properties of Wood* and *The Chemistry of High-Molecular-Weight Compounds*. An empty coffee can and a sock.

No ball. The ball had stayed behind in a lead safe, with a woman who wore a pencil in her hair.

I walked to the bus stop and thought.

I thought that today, for the first time, I'd done what I'd promised myself I wouldn't: I'd shown it. Not on purpose, but I'd shown it. To someone I'd met for the first time. And that this person was the first one who, seeing the impossible, hadn't gotten scared and hadn't wanted to sell it. Suspicious, sure, very much so. But not to catch me; to understand. The dearest thing about that copper sulfate wasn't measured in pierogi. Val had seen it.

I thought that the copper sulfate had cost me almost nothing. Because it wanted to grow on its own. Whereas I bend steel for hand pies, because steel doesn't want to. So the cheapest thing of all is to help things do what they already want to do. That was a new thought, and I wrote it down right there at the bus stop, on the back of my library card, because I'd run out of receipts: "What wants to happen anyway: nearly free. What doesn't: expensive. Look for what the thing wants. And don't get greedy: the solution only wants to give up the extra."

Then I thought about it and added: "Copper sulfate. Eat your heart out, Kevin Sommers."

Then I thought some more and added something else entirely: "Val. Gray eyes. Pencil."

I looked at that line. It clearly didn't belong on a library card, where you're only supposed to write your name and card number. It had nothing to do with the thimble, or pierogi, or the debt.

I didn't cross it out.

The bus came, and I took a window seat. Next to me sat a woman with two bags that smelled of fried fish. Suddenly I was terribly hungry: not from magic, just hungry, the way everybody gets hungry on a Friday evening. It was almost pleasant. Ordinary human hunger, the kind you don't have to pay for with any miracle.

Lugnut was waiting for me at home. He was sitting on the windowsill, looking at me reproachfully, as if he knew I'd spent the whole day with another woman.

"It was work," I told him.

Lugnut turned away.

I put on a pot of pierogi to boil, opened *Crystal Physics* to the first page and started reading from the very beginning. From the preface. I had never in my life read a preface.

The preface said: "This book is intended for those who want to understand, not merely memorize."

I thought that meant me. For the first time in twelve years, something meant me.

The pierogi boiled over, by the way. But by then that didn't surprise anybody.
