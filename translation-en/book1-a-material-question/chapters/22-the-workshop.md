# Chapter 22. The Workshop

By the twentieth of February there were four rings.

The first was Grandma's, on my middle finger, under the thimble. The other three had grown next to it, on the neighboring fingers. For ten days I went around with a hand like a retired jeweler's: the thimble, the ring under it, another blank each on my ring finger and my little finger, and one on my index finger. Three blanks warming up at once, and the warmth was mine: six extra hand pies a day. Mrs. Mazur grumbled that I ate like a whole work crew and worked like one man.

Ashot looked at my hand and said:

"Now I'm asking. How many rings before the wedding? I'm going to be marrying off Arsen one of these days. I need to know the going rate."

I told him it wasn't for a wedding. Ashot said that in that case it was worse, and went back to his heel tip.

The silver for the blanks came from Mrs. Mazur. The six silver-plated cupronickel spoons were no good. Cupronickel is a quarter nickel, we didn't need that much, and pulling it out means tearing the alloy apart, which costs more than the spoon. And the silver in the plating wouldn't make a single earring. But the seventh one, the real silver one from her graduation, was just right. Mrs. Mazur brought it down herself, wrapped in a napkin, and laid it on the table.

"Fifty years it's been lying in the china cabinet," she said. "That's enough lying around. A spoon is for eating. If it won't eat, let it teach."

After three rings, half the handle was all that was left of the spoon. I put it in the case of hooks, next to what was left of the little silver bar.

The rings didn't all come out the same. The one that sat closest to the thimble turned out almost as good as the first. The one on my little finger was half as strong. As if the thimble had taught the first ring, and the first ring had taught its neighbors, and each one after that was hearing not the teacher but somebody's retelling. Val wrote that down and said, "Like gossip going up the stairwell. The farther from the source, the less truth in it." Old Pete took offense on behalf of the stairwell.

Four rings. Two weeks to the auction. A thousand joints in the frame that I couldn't bend by myself. And forty people at the market who wanted to know whether they'd have a booth.

It was time to teach.

---

We set up the workshop in Hank's garage. There was nowhere else. Hank said Sundays from ten to six, and Saturdays in the evening, after the market. And clean up after yourselves. Saturday afternoons the market was open, and none of us were going to leave our booths: not Ashot, not me, not Mikey with his order notebook. Mikey spent his Saturdays at the market working off Hank's welder, and Hank remembered that better than anybody.

Hank moved his car out by the doors, and in its place Mikey set up three workbenches knocked together out of old pallets. I'd taken care of the heat in advance. The oil-filled heater was eating through Hank's bill so fast that every morning he looked at the meter like it was the enemy. So a wood stove went into the corner. I made it out of two old propane tanks. First I made the tanks safe, and took my time about it: an "empty" tank always has some gas left in it. I spent two nights growing the seam between them shut, for a bag of pierogi: joining is expensive. I ran the stovepipe out through the wall above the doors, not through the transom, and took it up higher than the roof. Otherwise you get no draw, and the fumes stay inside with you. The firewood was what was left of Mikey's pallets.

That mattered. The heat the students would be taking was going to come from the wood. Not from each other.

On the doors Mikey hung a sign he'd printed out: "THE WORKSHOP. WE TEACH IN THE OPEN. WHOEVER WANTS TO LEARN, WE TEACH. UNDER 18: ONLY WITH YOUR PARENTS' PERMISSION."

He'd added the last line himself. I hadn't asked him to.

---

On the first Sunday, the twenty-second, five people came.

Ashot, with a hammer, a cobbler's last and a sack of old shoes, "for experiments; customers' shoes I'm not giving you."

Mrs. Mazur, with a pot of hand pies, a jar of cherry jam and some dough wrapped in a dish towel.

Mikey, with his notebook, his laptop and Lana. Lana had come straight from her night shift, smelling of warm bread. She sat for half an hour, watched, and didn't say anything. At the doors she stopped.

"You have my permission," she told me. "Under rule four. And you answer for him. Under rule three."

And she went home to sleep.

Bull.

I wasn't expecting him. Bull pulled up in his old blue sedan, which these days started on the first try, got out, looked around and said:

"I saw what you did with my valve, Timothy. Four months now I've been pretending I didn't. Every Friday I count your money, and I think: I'd like to be able to do that." He paused. "It's unprofessional, envying a debtor. Ashot said at the market you teach people here. Will you let me in?"

"We will," I said.

"For free?"

"For free. That's the rule."

Bull thought it over.

"Then I'd better chip in something, so I'm not freeloading," he said, and took a jerry can of coolant and a torque wrench out of his trunk. "This is for Hank. I heard they cleaned him out."

Hank took the wrench, turned it over in his hands, said "Nice," and went off to his corner with his thermos. He had no intention of learning anything. But he didn't leave, either.

And the fifth was Ms. Pratt.

Old Pete brought her. They came arm in arm, both in hats, him with his ledger, her with a folder. I saw them at the doors and went cold. The Revenue Department. In a garage. And forty pages of barter in Old Pete's ledger.

Ms. Pratt came in, looked around, saw the wood stove and said, before anything else:

"Where's the fire extinguisher?"

There wasn't one. Old Pete wrote in the ledger: "Fire extinguisher: absent." Hank got up without a word and brought his, out of the car. Ms. Pratt checked the inspection tag and nodded.

"Mr. Nagel," she said then. "Mr. Palmer tells me you're conducting instruction here."

"Free of charge," I said.

"I know," said Ms. Pratt. "I checked. Instruction without payment is not a business. A private individual with no employees doesn't need a license. The hand pies are a gift from a private individual, and gifts are not taxable." She paused. Then she took off her glasses, the ones that didn't fog up anymore, and wiped them, though there was nothing to wipe. "I came to learn."

I opened my mouth. Closed it.

"In the evenings I do crosswords," said Ms. Pratt. "This one is more interesting. And I don't like it when an answer doesn't come out right. Yours hasn't come out right since October."

Behind her, Old Pete nodded. Proudly. Like a man who'd brought the Revenue Department into a garage and hadn't died of shame.

---

First, the rules. I switched off the breaker in the panel, in front of everybody, and said out loud, the way we always do:

"Nobody touches the panel. Nobody goes near the wires with a ring on. We don't know whether a ring behaves like the thimble. It might not be your ground. We're not testing that on ourselves."

Ms. Pratt wrote it down. Then the water glasses. Val insisted. "Without a control, it's not a school, it's a cult." Eight glasses, two hundred grams of water each, three minutes, the control and the "wanting." We figured it the same way as in December: temperature before and after, the difference in each glass, and then how much less the "wanting" glass had warmed up than the control. That last number was the one Val called out.

Ashot: minus two tenths against the control. Borderline.

"Water isn't his material," said Val. "We'll see how he does with leather."

Bull: minus six tenths. He surprised himself.

Ms. Pratt: minus one tenth. Then again: minus one tenth. Then one tenth again.

"One tenth is within the margin of error," said Val. "But three identical in a row isn't. I've never seen reproducibility like that."

"I'm always like that," said Ms. Pratt. "At work they used to tell me, 'Pratt, even your mistakes are identical.'"

Mrs. Mazur: minus a degree and a half, without a ring, same as always. Mikey: minus three tenths, same as in December.

Everybody had something. Five out of five.

Val wrote it down. Then she looked for a long time at her own line from the week before: two tenths, with the ring. Then she closed the notebook and said, to nobody in particular:

"So I'm the only one who gets zero without a ring. I'm a rarity. That's a result too."

Nobody laughed. Mrs. Mazur silently slid a hand pie over to her.

---

Then I handed out the rings.

There were four rings and five students. Mrs. Mazur turned hers down right away: "My hands are plenty for me. Give it to the boy." The first one, Grandma's, the strongest, I gave to Ms. Pratt. I don't know why. It seemed to me that what she needed most was confidence. To Ashot, the one that had grown on my index finger. It wouldn't go over his finger, Ashot's fingers are broad from the hammer, so I opened it up to fit him. Bending is cheap: half a hand pie. To Bull, the one from my ring finger. To Mikey, the one from my little finger, the weakest. He's got plenty of his own as it is.

"At six o'clock, all the rings go back on my hand," I said. "They've got more growing to do. And the next ones to teach."

And that's when it turned out I didn't know how to teach.

Not at all. I stood in the middle of the garage, between three workbenches and the wood stove, and didn't know what to say. Five people were looking at me. Ashot, with an old shoe. Mrs. Mazur, with her dough. Bull, with an old tie rod he'd taken off back in the fall, "I'm never putting it back on the car, but for experiments it's just right." Ms. Pratt, with her folder, because she hadn't brought anything else. Mikey, with a nail.

"So," I said. "You… uh. Feel it?"

Silence.

"It's like water in a bathtub," I said. "Warm, cold. With sand in it."

"What water?" said Bull. "It's a tie rod."

I realized I was explaining it the way I felt it myself. And they weren't me. Ashot doesn't know what steel feels like. But he knows what leather feels like: where it's thin, where it's thick, where it's dried out, where it'll stretch. He's known for twenty years. Mrs. Mazur doesn't know copper. But she knows dough. Bull doesn't know silicon. But he can hear an engine.

They didn't need to learn to feel things the way I do. They needed to understand that what they already felt with their hands was it. Just quieter.

---

"Ashot," I said. "Don't think about the ring. Think about the sole. Where's it worn down?"

"Of course I know," Ashot said, offended. "Right here, on the heel, on the outside. The man walks pigeon-toed. I can spot them by their heel tips."

"Now want it to be stronger there. Don't do it. Want it. The way you want it when you're putting on a heel tip."

Ashot closed his eyes.

For a minute, nothing happened. Then the shoe creaked. Then it went hard. All of it. Not the sole: the whole shoe. The leather went as stiff as plywood. The upper straightened up and froze. Only the laces hung soft, the way they'd been. Ashot hadn't been holding the laces in his head.

Ashot opened his eyes. He set the shoe on the floor. The shoe stood there. Level. Like a stool.

"Sit on it," said Bull.

Ashot carefully sat down. The shoe held.

You could hear the wood crackling in the stove.

"I wanted the sole," said Ashot. "Just the sole. On the heel."

"You wanted it stronger," I said. "And the power doesn't know where a sole ends. It knows what you mean."

"What I meant," Ashot said slowly, "was for that man never to come to me with that heel again. I'm sick of him."

Without looking up from her notebook, Val said:

"Leather is collagen. Long chains. Tanning links them together here and there. You linked them everywhere. That's why it's plywood." She looked up. "It's not a mistake, Ashot. It's a very sturdy stool. It's just not what you wanted."

"I don't need a stool," said Ashot. "I have a stool."

He asked for tea. Afterward he couldn't button his jacket: his fingers wouldn't cooperate, and they were white, like Mikey's that day at Hank's garage. Leather costs a lot to work. The heat he'd used was his own, plus a little from the stove.

Then he looked at the shoe one more time and said, in a different voice:

"Although, for the new booth… Something to sit on."

A week later the upper of that shoe cracked. Too many links, and a thing gets brittle. Ashot said it's the same with people.

---

Mrs. Mazur had dough and cherry jam. She wanted to learn to make hand pies fast. So she could feed forty people at the construction site. And not take forty minutes for every ten.

"Not faster, Mrs. Mazur," I said. "Better first. Do you want the filling not to leak out?"

"I do," said Mrs. Mazur. "It's been leaking out on me my whole life. I've tried cornstarch, I've tried cream of wheat. Still runs."

She laid a raw hand pie with jam on her palm, covered it with her other palm, and wanted.

The pie went cold. I could feel it through the workbench: Mrs. Mazur was pushing the water out of the jam and into the dough, and that was what she was paying for with cold. The sugar inside lined itself up on its own. It had been wanting to for a long time. That part was nearly free.

Mrs. Mazur broke the pie open.

The dough had gone wet and sticky. And inside, where the jam had been, lay a slab of candy. Dark cherry red, clear, hard. And in the very middle, a pinkish crystal with faces, as small as a fingernail.

Mrs. Mazur licked the dark part.

"A lollipop," she said. "Like at the county fair when I was little."

"A lollipop is glass," said Val. "Like Professor Arkin's bottle. Sugar that didn't get the chance to become a crystal. But this, in the middle, is a crystal. Pure sugar. You got both."

"I wanted it not to run," said Mrs. Mazur. "And it doesn't."

Her hands were as cold as if she'd just come up from a root cellar. She warmed them on a mug and didn't complain.

We chipped the candy off a piece at a time with Ashot's hammer, the one that won't break. There was enough for everybody.

---

Bull started with the tie rod. Old, taken off the car, with a bend in the middle.

The tie rod straightened out. And then it went soft as wire.

"Took the temper out of it," Bull said himself, before I could open my mouth. "Like your Mikey did with that spring. Only the whole thing." He's a mechanic. He got it faster than I did. "It needs hardening."

He stuck the tie rod into the stove himself, got it red-hot, and dunked it in a bucket of oil. The oil hissed. From his corner, Hank grunted approvingly.

"And the fender?" asked Bull.

The front fender of the sedan had had a dent in it since the fall. "Hit a pole on a collection run. Embarrassing to admit."

"A fender's thin," I said. "Thin is easier. But it's also easier to ruin. And it's freezing out. Where's the heat coming from?"

Bull thought it over. Then he started the sedan and ran the engine for ten minutes or so, until the radiator was hot.

"My own," he said. "My car's. Don't need anybody's permission."

"You don't," I said. "But you know the rule."

"I know. Heat always belongs to somebody." He laid his left palm on the hot side of the engine and his right one on the dent. And wanted.

I stood next to him and felt through the thimble how the metal of the fender stirred. As if it remembered being smooth. And then as if it remembered too much. The steel went into a wave. The dent popped out, and around it the metal rose. More. And more. The fender bulged outward, ran in folds, curled over at the edge, stretched toward the headlight.

Bull jerked his hands away.

On the front fender of the blue sedan there was a sculpture. A wave of steel, arched like the crest of a breaker, with a curl at the end like a seahorse's tail. Bare steel gleamed where the paint had cracked and flaked off. And all around the crest, the fender had gone as thin as a tin can: the metal had gone into the wave.

"What's that?" asked Hank from the doorway.

"I wanted it to be beautiful," Bull said slowly. "Not smooth. Beautiful. I used to draw, back in the army. A long time ago. I forgot."

"It's beautiful," said Hank. "Only you can't drive around with that. The first cop who sees it'll pull you over."

Bull was looking at the fender. He had a strange look on his face. Like a man who'd found a letter in the pocket of an old jacket, one he'd written to himself a long time ago.

"Show me how to put it back," he said to me. "Smooth. And then I'll make it again. On something I don't have to drive past the cops."

Putting it back took an hour. The two of us. Him with his hands and his ring, me next to him, telling him where the steel wanted to go and where it was digging in its heels. By the end of the hour, the fender was smooth. To the touch, anyway. Not to the eye. It was covered in bald patches with no paint.

"I'll paint it," said Bull. "Otherwise it'll start rusting by April." He ate four of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies in a row. His hands were shaking. "This is better than any shop I ever worked in."

"Why?"

"Because here they teach you," said Bull. "There, all they do is make demands."

---

Mikey, with the weakest ring of all, wanted to go straight for something big. Of course.

"Tim," he said. "Can I do the hinge on the doors? I promised Hank I'd grease it, but the grease went thick in the cold. I'd rather just do the hinge itself. So it never needs greasing at all."

He'd already put his hand on the hinge. I could see him getting ready to want.

And then Ms. Pratt said, without looking up from her folder:

"Michael. Out loud. Exactly what you want. And where the edge is."

Mikey froze. Then he said slowly:

"I want the hinge… not to rust." He thought about it. "No. I don't know how to do that. I want it to get smooth inside. Only inside, where it rubs. The edge is the hinge itself. Not the doors. Not the post."

He stood there a moment. Then he took his hand away.

"No," he said. "I don't understand what's in there. First I'll watch how you guys do it. Otherwise I'll end up with a stool."

Ashot, sitting on his shoe, said, "Good call."

We never did touch the hinge that day. But Mikey straightened a nail, saying its length out loud first. And the nail came out straight: exactly from the head to the point, not "roughly."

---

Ms. Pratt sat over her folder and didn't do anything. For about two hours. Everybody else tried things, made mistakes, laughed, ate hand pies. She watched and took notes.

I went over to her.

"Ms. Pratt. What about you?"

"I'm observing," she said. "First I study the instructions. Then I do it."

"There aren't any instructions."

"Then I'll write them," said Ms. Pratt. And she showed me the folder.

There were three sheets in it. Neat, ruled, in her handwriting, even and small, like a payroll ledger:

"Observations. Day One.

"One. Each student works within his own area of knowledge: leather, dough, auto body metal. Outside his own area, the result is worse.

"Two. The error is not in the power but in the wanting. The power does what the student means, not what he says. Sarkisian: irritation. Mazur: taste. Bykowski: beauty.

"Three. Every action is accompanied by an expense: hunger, cold fingers. The expense is not being recorded. Recommendation: keep records.

"Four. The instructor, Nagel, cannot explain, but can demonstrate. Recommendation: demonstrate more, explain less.

"Five. Danger: a student may want the wrong thing. Recommendation: before every action, state out loud exactly what you want. And where the edge is."

I read it and felt myself turning red. Point four was about me. Point two I'd known since the very first night, with the faucet sealed shut. Point five I'd written down myself in January, after Hank's garage. I'd known all of it. Only I'd known it with my hands. She had written it down so you could hand it to somebody else.

"Ms. Pratt," I said. "You could give this to anybody. Just as it is."

"I didn't make it up," she said. "It's what I saw. People all make the same mistake on their tax returns: they don't write down what happened, they write down what they wish had happened. It's the same thing here."

Then she put on the ring. She took a paper clip out of the folder, bent it open, laid it on her palm and said out loud, distinctly, the way you read a report into the record:

"I want the paper clip to become straight. Only the paper clip. Only straight. The edge is the length of the paper clip."

The paper clip straightened. Perfectly. As if she'd used a ruler. On the first try.

Ms. Pratt looked at it. Then, for the first time all day, she took a hand pie. And a second one.

"Expense recorded," she said.

Old Pete wrote in the ledger: "Pratt, Nina. Experiment one. Paper clip. No remarks." And underlined it twice. Then he thought for a moment and added: "With honors."

---

The next Sunday, the first of March, things were different.

Ashot said out loud, "Just the heel. Three-quarters of an inch. On the outside." And did it. The heel got tough, and the shoe stayed a shoe. He set it on the floor, looked at it and said, "Too bad. Nothing to sit on."

Mrs. Mazur said, "So it doesn't run. But so it stays jam." And the jam stayed jam, only thick, as if it had been cooked an hour longer. She took a bite and said, "Now that's a hand pie."

Bull took the dent out of the door of Hank's car, smooth, on the first try, and didn't touch the paint.

And then Bull taught me. He started the sedan and said, "Listen." And I listened to his third cylinder knocking. Just barely, at idle. I'd never once heard that, for all the engines I'd fixed. Bull said, "You see steel with your hands. An engine, you see with your ears. Those are two different things. Your ears need teaching." And I realized that for the first time since we'd started the workshop, I was learning something, and not from the notebooks, and not from Val. From a student.

New people came. Greg the butcher, with a knife. Stefan the baker, "to have a look at the stove." Roxanne, with a pot of carnations, asking whether flowers were allowed. They were. Let them watch, for now. There weren't enough rings for everybody, so the new people started with the glasses. Greg got minus four tenths. He said that was because he'd spent thirty years with cold meat.

Hank stood in his corner with his thermos. In January, at the meeting on the stairs, he'd said, "You can't teach him." Now, toward the end of the day, he came over to the table with the water glasses, sat down, picked up a glass and held it for three minutes. Without a word. Minus one tenth. He looked at the thermometer, then at me, and said:

"Well. Guess you can't not teach."

And went back to his corner.

---

That evening, when everybody had gone and the rings were back on my hand, warm, as if after a long conversation, I sat by the stove and thought.

I don't know how to teach. I'd figured that out better in two Sundays than in my whole life. When I do something, I don't think about how I do it. But when you teach, you have to take what you do apart into pieces, and hand over each piece so that another person can pick it up with his own hands. And everybody's hands are different. Ashot: through leather. Mrs. Mazur: through dough. Bull: through an engine. Ms. Pratt: through instructions. Mikey: through "out loud, and where the edge is."

Teaching is harder than doing. I was more tired than I'd ever been at the market, even on the longest days. And I hadn't eaten a single hand pie: Mrs. Mazur had given them all to the people working with their hands. I'd been working with my head. That's a different kind of hunger: not in your stomach, but somewhere behind your eyes.

And one more thing. I like it.

A thing you've fixed is your work. A person you've taught isn't yours anymore. He'll go off and do something you can't. A stool out of a shoe. A seahorse on a fender. Instructions. And one day he'll start up an engine and tell you, "Listen."

I wrote in my notebook:

"Five out of five. Then eight out of eight. Everybody has their own."

Below that:

"Teaching: not through my hands. Through theirs."

Below that:

"Ms. Pratt's instructions: five points. Into the handbook. All of it."

And the last thing, after a pause:

"I like this. Why didn't I know that before?"

Lugnut, whom Mikey had hauled over to the garage "for atmosphere," was asleep on Ashot's shoe-stool with the cracked upper. He'd found it first. He's always the first to find anything that's turned into something else.

Five days to the auction. Four weeks to the end of the lease. Four rings. Eight students. Next Sunday there'd be more.

I tossed a log into the stove and thought that I needed to grow rings faster. Or grow them on somebody besides me. Maybe a ring left on Ms. Pratt's hand could teach somebody too. More slowly. But in a chain.

We teach in the open. Whoever wants to learn, we teach.
