# Chapter 21. The Copy

The first week, starting January eighteenth, we read.

Thirty-one notebooks. I'd read them before, but the way you read the manual for a washing machine: you open it when something isn't working, find the page you need, close it. Now Val said, "From page one. All of them. In order. With a pencil." And we read.

We sat in my kitchen in the evenings, after the market, with two lamps and two mugs. Val read the even-numbered volumes, I read the odd ones. Then we switched. Val copied into her oilcloth notebook everything that had to do with how the object was built. I copied out everything that had to do with how it behaved. We ended up with two columns. The left one was long and hard to follow. The right one was short and easy to follow. Between them was a hole.

Professor Stern had studied the thimble for thirty years. He knew the composition, roughly. Copper, silver, iron, a little nickel, traces of something he couldn't identify. Nothing magical. Nothing you won't find in an old pot and Grandma's spoon. Only one thing didn't add up: the thimble was heavier than a mix like that should allow. Professor Stern noted it and didn't explain it. He knew how the thimble behaved: it takes energy, converts it, amplifies it, gets warm next to the second one. He knew it didn't answer to everybody: to him, yes; to his wife, no; to Glen, yes.

He didn't know why.

"It's all in the structure," said Val on the sixth evening, closing the twenty-fourth volume. "Not in the composition. The composition is ordinary. If it came down to the composition, somebody would have copied it long ago. So it comes down to how the atoms are laid out. Like cotton and rayon. The same cellulose, and different properties. Because it's laid out differently."

"And how are they laid out?"

"I don't know," said Val. "And he didn't know. He didn't have an instrument that could show it." She looked at me. "Then again, he didn't have you."

---

I put the thimble on and tried to feel it from the inside.

That turned out to be strange. Everything I touch, I feel through the thimble. Steel, copper, silicon, dough through a tabletop. But the thimble itself I had never felt. The way an eye can't see itself. I touched it with the fingers of my other hand: no use. Metal like any metal. Warm. Smooth. Dimpled.

Then I took it off, set it on the table and touched it with the little silver bar, holding the bar in my bare hand. Nothing. Of course nothing: without the thimble, I can't feel anything. That's what I thought.

"A closed loop," said Val. "To feel the instrument, you need the instrument."

"You need a second instrument," I said.

We looked at each other. The second instrument was in a pine forest behind a tall fence, in a house where the lights are never on.

"No," we said at the same time.

And then I remembered Mikey. Mikey's fingers, without any thimble, feeling the little spring inside somebody else's padlock. Faintly. Through the body of the lock. But feeling it.

I picked the thimble up off the table, closed my hand around it and shut my eyes.

I don't know how long I sat like that. Val said afterward it was forty minutes. I don't remember that. All I remember is that at first it was quiet, like after a concert, when the ringing in your ears has finally died down, and then, very far off, as if through three walls, something started to come through. Not metal. A pattern. Rough, blurry, like a drawing on a fogged-up window.

The dimples on the thimble weren't decoration.

Under each dimple, something twisted inward. Not rows, the way it is in ordinary metal. Funnels. Like the spiral of chains in a cotton fiber that Val had drawn for me. There were a lot of them; each one started under a dimple and ran toward the center, and there they all met. Like whirlpools in a river, all pulling toward one point. That was all I could make out. Just that.

I opened my eyes. My hands were shaking. I was so hungry I could have eaten the thimble.

"Well?" said Val.

"The dimples," I said. "They're not dimples. They're entrances."

Val wasn't looking at the thimble. She was looking at me.

"You just did that without it," she said slowly. "The thimble was lying in your palm. Not on your finger. Contaminated sample, point one."

I wanted to argue and couldn't. She wrote it down. Underlined it. Then she said, "Later. Separately. Right now: the structure."

---

That's when the failures started. Three weeks, all told, counting the week of reading.

I won't tell you about every attempt. There isn't a notebook or a bag of pierogi big enough for all of them. I'll tell you about the main ones.

We didn't do this at home. Home was out of the question: Corey the electrician, the aluminum splices, three apartments on one line. If I started pulling current for a copy of the thimble, the neighbors wouldn't get dim lights, they'd get a fire.

Hank helped.

After the hinge and the padlock, Hank had changed toward me. He hadn't gotten any warmer: Hank doesn't get warmer. But he'd started saying hello. And when he heard from Mikey (Mikey tells Hank everything; the two of them tinker with the car together now) that I needed a place with good power, he came over himself.

"I've got heavy-duty power in the garage," he said. "Ran it for the welder. Separate meter, separate breaker, its own line straight from the transformer. You could run a steel mill in there. The electricity's yours, you pay for it yourself." He paused. "I sleep at home at night now. I'm giving it a try. Since somebody needs the place. Just don't touch the welder." He thought about it. "Not that there's a welder. Somebody walked off with it."

It was like a gift. A separate line meant no neighbors on the splices. Its own breaker meant that if anything went wrong, it would trip, not the whole stairwell. I came the very first night, in my father's rubber boots, with a rubber mat, a thermos and my copper cord, and I repeated the rule about the ground to myself three times before I touched anything. A garage floor is concrete, frozen through, and to current, concrete is still the ground. I only ever used one ordinary circuit, never the full welding hookup. The full hookup has enough voltage between two wires to kill you, and my fingers don't go between wires. Ever.

Attempt number one. I took copper, silver and iron in the proportions Professor Stern had found thirty years ago and made a thimble out of them. The same shape. With the same dimples. Identical, like a key cut from an impression.

It was dead. Just a thimble. A tailor's thimble. Sew all you like.

Val tested it on me: I put on the copy and tried to straighten a nail. The nail didn't budge. I tried to take the heat out of a mug. The mug stayed hot. The copy was silent, the way the original had been silent on Val's finger the evening my buttons fell off.

"You copied the key," said Val. "What you need to make is the thing inside the door."

Attempt number two. I made the spirals. Under each dimple, a funnel: atoms going around in a circle, toward the center. That was expensive. I assembled each spiral atom by atom, the way I'd laid out the chains of cellulose, only a hundred times smaller and a thousand times more precise. I drew power off Hank's line all night. By morning I had a thimble with spirals.

Dead too.

In one night the meter in Hank's garage ran up as much as mine at home did in a month. I did the math. One ordinary circuit, all night, running close to its limit. A bag and change. For one night. For a dead thimble. And atoms of the same metal, just rearranged, cost almost nothing, which meant almost all of it had gone off as heat. By morning the garage was like a drying room. The snow on the roof had melted and slid off. Val had taken off Hank's quilted work jacket and was sitting in just her sweater.

Attempts three, five, eight. I made the spirals tighter. Looser. Longer. I changed the proportions. I added nickel, took the nickel out. Val sat next to me on an upturned crate and wrote it all down. Every attempt. Every pattern. Every bag that went into the meter.

Two weeks in, by the end of January, we had eleven dead thimbles and an electric bill that made Hank whistle and say, "What are you doing in there, melting gold?"

"I've already melted gold," I said. "Gold, at least, you don't have to sell. These you can't even wear."

Goldie called in between attempts. The auction for the lot had been set for March sixth. Six weeks off, and the end of March right behind it. I wrote that on a napkin and hung it over the workbench.

---

On the fifteenth night I gave up.

Well, not completely. I just sat down on Hank's couch (it really did exist: in the corner, sagging, smelling of motor oil) and said out loud that I didn't understand. That I was doing everything right and it wasn't working. That in thirty years Professor Stern hadn't understood, and I wasn't going to understand in three weeks, and Glen had been right, and I should have learned from him and not from the notebooks.

Val didn't say anything. She closed her notebook, looked at me and said:

"Let's go see Mrs. Stern."

"Now? It's two in the morning."

"Her lamp is on around the clock," said Val. "You told me so yourself."

---

We took a taxi from Factory Street to Garden Street, for half a bag. The lamp on the second floor of the yellow building with the columns was on. Mrs. Stern opened the door in a bathrobe, a book in her hand, and wasn't the least bit surprised.

"I knew you'd come," she said. "Sooner or later." She looked at Val. "And this is?"

"Val. Finch. Chemistry. I got a B from Professor Stern," said Val.

"He gave B's to the ones he remembered," said Mrs. Stern. "Come in. Ephraim used to come home at two in the morning too. When things weren't working."

She put the kettle on. We sat down at the desk in the study, under that same lamp, the one the thimble had once lain inside. Professor Stern's watch was ticking on the desk. I'd fixed it in the fall. It was still running.

I told her everything. About the dimples. About the spirals. About the eleven dead copies.

Mrs. Stern listened without a word. Then she said:

"He never wrote about that."

"About what?"

"About how the object is made. He didn't write about it, because he didn't know himself. But he talked about it. To me. Once." She paused. "Have you gotten as far as the ninth volume? There's the evening he held both of them. They got warm. He was frightened, and he put the second one away. And afterward he came home, right here, sat down where you're sitting now and said, 'Bella, they weren't made. They were grown.'"

Val and I froze.

"Like a pearl," said Mrs. Stern. "That's what he said. 'Like a pearl. You can't make a pearl. You can put a grain of sand inside a shell, and the shell will build it up, layer after layer. For years. The object isn't a thing. The object is what grew around another object.'"

She looked at me.

"He thought the second one grew out of the first. Or the first out of the second. That at some point somebody put a piece of metal next to an object, and left it there. For a long time. And the metal…" She searched for the word. "Learned."

"That's why they get warm next to each other," Val said quietly. "It isn't heat. It's… a conversation."

"He didn't know," said Mrs. Stern. "He only thought so. And he was afraid to test it. Because to test it, you'd have to keep them side by side for a long time. And he couldn't keep them side by side for even a minute." She paused. "Glen… have you seen him?"

"I have," I said. "He asked how you were."

Mrs. Stern didn't say anything. She only reached over and straightened the watch on the desk.

I looked at the lamp. At its empty bronze base, where the thimble had lain for twenty years.

Val was looking at the same spot. Then she opened her oilcloth notebook and leafed back through it.

"The eleventh volume," she said. "The year Glen left. I copied it out and didn't understand it. 'Socket: bronze. Velvet. Let it lie quietly. I'll look in ten years.' And nothing else. He wanted to see what would happen to the bronze. And he never looked." She raised her eyes. "Tim. He set up the experiment himself. Twenty years ago. And he never got to take the reading."

"He built the lamp in the seventies," Mrs. Stern said slowly. "But the socket he turned on the lathe when Glen left. He said bronze was the calmest metal there is. That the object would have peace and quiet in it."

I reached out, put on the thimble and touched the socket in the base of the lamp. The velvet in there had worn through to the threads, and in one spot, on the side, the thimble had been touching bare bronze for all of those twenty years. In October I hadn't noticed. Back then I'd been looking for a screw, not a pattern.

The bronze answered.

Not the way ordinary bronze answers: heavily, indifferently. It answered the way a string does when somebody plays the same note nearby. Faintly. Barely. But where the thimble had touched it for twenty years, there were funnels in the bronze. Tiny ones. Unfinished. Like the first layer of mother-of-pearl on a grain of sand.

For twenty years the thimble had lain in the bronze. And the bronze had started to learn.

"Twenty years, and almost nothing," I said.

"Bronze is the wrong metal," said Val. "And the object in the lamp was asleep. Nobody was working with it. But what if you took the same composition it has, and kept it next to a thimble that works every day? Through you?" She paused. "Not copy it. Put it next to it and wait. The way he wanted."

---

What came after wasn't fast. But it came.

We didn't build anything atom by atom anymore. Enough. Eleven dead thimbles is lesson enough. We did it the way Val had thought up. The way a shell does it.

I took half of Grandma's little silver bar. The one that had been lying in the case of hooks ever since I'd pinched a crumb off it for the solar panel. I took copper from an old wire, the same one I'd sat with over the silicon for nights on end. Iron from a nail out of the same jar that gave me the nail for the balcony slide bolt, the one that keeps Lugnut out. I fused them in the proportions Professor Stern had found. Joining is expensive: two hand pies and a thermos. I made a plain ring. No dimples. No funnels. Just a little ring, like a grain of sand.

And I put it on the same finger as the thimble. Below it. Snug against it.

And I started wearing it.

I wore it for nine days, into the middle of February. Without taking it off. At the market, where Mikey was writing down people instead of orders. In Hank's garage, where I wasn't building anything anymore, just sitting and reading Professor Stern's volumes, resting the hand with the thimble and the ring on my knee. At home, in the kitchen. In my sleep.

The ring was warm. All the time. Not very: like a hot-water bottle that's cooling off. And the warmth was mine. For those nine days I ate two hand pies a day more than usual; Val wrote it down. The thimble was drawing on me a little at a time and passing it to the ring. And why the ring didn't just get warm but changed: that was the very same "Where did she put it?" we'd written down in December, in Mrs. Mazur's kitchen, and still have no answer to. Val said, "It isn't heat. It's a conversation." A hypothesis. With a price tag of two hand pies.

And on the ninth day, first I took off both the thimble and the ring and tried to straighten a nail with my bare hand. Nothing. Val wrote down: zero. Then I put on just the ring.

The nail straightened.

Not the way it does with the thimble. Slowly. With effort. With hunger, as if I'd run two miles. But it straightened. And it wasn't crooked: "roughly" straight, with a slight wave in it, like Mikey's paper clip. But straight.

Val was sitting across from me. She saw it.

"Again," she said. Her voice was the one she'd used for "It's not a story, it's a curve" that night in the lab.

I did it again. With a different nail. It worked.

"It's a student one," I said. "Weak. Ten times weaker, or so. But alive."

"It's the first man-made object in history," said Val. "Do you understand? Not found. Not stolen. Grown. On Grandma's silver."

At the market, Ashot saw the ring on my finger next to the thimble and said, "I'm not asking. But congratulations." I never did figure out what for.

---

Then we tested it on other people.

First Mrs. Mazur just held a glass, plain, same as in December: minus a degree and a half. Then she put the ring on her ring finger (it wouldn't go over her middle one) and held a second glass. Minus four degrees Celsius. She looked at the thermometer, then at the ring, then at me, and said, "Get this hunk of metal off me, it's making my hands cold." And didn't take it off. She held another one. And another.

Mikey (he came with Lana; I always asked now) without the ring gave minus three tenths on the glass, and with the ring, minus eight tenths. And with the ring he straightened a nail in ten seconds, then ate two of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, and his fingertips were white. Lana watched with her lips pressed together. Then she asked, "Is it dangerous?" I told her honestly, "I don't know. So far, no. We'll check." She nodded.

Val put it on last.

I could see how scared of it she was. She held the ring between two fingers, the way you hold a test tube of something caustic, and didn't put it on. Then she put it on. Picked up the glass. Closed her eyes.

Today's control: plus eight tenths again. In December, without the ring, her wanting had given plus nine tenths.

With the ring: plus six.

Two tenths. Right at the margin of error. No more than Mikey with nothing at all.

Val looked at the thermometer for a very long time. Then she took off the ring, set it on the table and said:

"That isn't data."

And she went out onto the landing.

I didn't go after her. I understood that right now I shouldn't. Mrs. Mazur looked at me and didn't say anything either. She just slid a hand pie over to me.

Ten minutes later Val came back. Her eyes were dry.

"Two tenths," she said. "That isn't data. But it isn't zero either. I'll repeat it. Twenty times. Then we'll see."

---

The fight happened the next evening. In Hank's garage. Over the paper.

We were sitting on the couch. Val was showing me her outline. Three pages, in small handwriting, with headings: "Introduction," "Materials and Methods," "Results," "Discussion."

"I'm going to send it to a journal," she said. "Not now. In a month. When there are twenty repeats, when there are photographs…"

"What photographs?" I said. "Nobody can see the funnels but me. From the inside. With my hands."

"Then there'll be descriptions," said Val. "There'll be a grown object. There'll be a method. There'll be the fact that lots of people have the ability, and it can be amplified. This is the discovery of the century, Tim. Not of my career. Of the century."

"And what happens when you publish it?"

Val stopped.

"There'll be a paper."

"There'll be Glen," I said. "There'll be Cochran. There'll be Lenny Kurgan, who back in October offered to 'work together.' There'll be people we don't know. There'll be Officer Sommers, who typed 'with my hands' and didn't understand. And then somebody will come along who does understand. And he'll come here, to this garage. And take the ring. And the thimble. And maybe us."

"So we hide it?" said Val. And her voice became the one I was afraid of. Even. Laboratory. "Like Glen? You said yourself you don't want to be like Glen."

"I'm not saying hide it. I'm saying not now."

"Then when?" Val got up off the couch. "When, Tim? When we've built the market? When Cochran stops being Cochran? When Glen dies? Professor Stern said 'not now' too. For thirty years. And then he died. And everything he knew sat in a safe, thirty-one notebooks, until you came along."

I didn't say anything.

"And one more thing," said Val. More quietly. "I've been writing for Dr. Andrews for six years. I told you. In the acknowledgments, in small print. I thought at least this would be mine. My water glasses. My twenty repeats. My threads. My bronze in the eleventh volume. And now you're saying 'not now.' And I realize that if it's not now, it'll be yours. Like always. You made the ring. You feel the spirals. And I'm the lab tech who takes notes again."

"That isn't true."

"It is true," said Val. "It's just that you don't want it to be. But that's how it'll turn out. Because that's how things are set up. You're the one with the power."

She picked up her three pages and walked to the doors. The garage doors creaked (in the cold the grease had gone thick; Mikey had promised to redo them), and she went out into the snow.

I sat on Hank's couch and looked at the ring on the table. Small, silver and copper, warm. Grandma's spoon was in it. The wire from the silicon. The nail from the balcony slide bolt.

I thought: she's right, though. Not that I want to take it from her. But that this is how it'll turn out if I don't do anything. If I just wait for the day when it's "all right."

And I thought: Glen said knowledge is something you have only as long as other people don't. And Professor Stern wrote in the ninth volume: if there are ever two of them, and they are in different hands, let those hands know each other.

The ring grew next to the thimble. Not out of it: next to it. For twenty years the bronze learned next to the thimble in the lamp. For nine days the ring learned on my finger.

Knowledge grows next to something too. Not out of one person. Next to him.

---

I caught up with her at the bus stop. She was standing under the streetlight with no hat (she'd left her hat on Hank's couch), watching the bus not come.

"Val," I said. "Coauthors."

She didn't turn around.

"You and me," I said. "You do the writing. You answer the reviewers. They're your water glasses, your bronze, your 'put it next to it and wait.' I'm the one who feels the funnels. And Mrs. Stern goes in the acknowledgments, in big print. She's the one who told us about the pearl."

Val turned around.

"And when?"

"When we've built the market," I said. "Not because 'not now.' Because by then there won't be two of us, there'll be forty. Forty people who can do it. Who've seen it. Who can't all be carried out of a garage. Let them come when there are forty of us. And after that, we'll see how much to show."

Val looked at me. Snow was falling on her glasses, and she didn't wipe it off.

"Finch, Nagel," she said. Then she was quiet for a moment. "No. Stern first. It's his thirty years. And his experiment with the bronze, the one he never got to take the reading on. I don't want to be Dr. Andrews, putting himself in front of the person who did the work."

"Stern, Finch, Nagel," I said.

"Stern, Finch, Nagel," Val agreed. "He wouldn't fit in my acknowledgments anyway."

She took my hand. The one with the thimble and the ring. The ring was warm. Her fingers were cold.

"Let's go back," she said. "I forgot my hat. And I want you to know: I'm not giving up on two tenths. I'll repeat it twenty times. And if it comes out zero, I'll publish the zero. Somebody has to print the zeros too."

---

We got back to the garage after midnight. Hank was sleeping at home, trying it out, the way he'd promised. At night the garage was ours.

Val found her hat on the couch and didn't put it on. She sat down next to me, took off her glasses, set them on the workbench and said:

"We had a fight and we made up. That calls for a celebration. I've read that's what people do. Not in journals. In novels."

"It's cold in here," I said.

"You know how to get heat," said Val. "Just not from me. And not from Hank. Hank's been through enough."

I laughed. I unhooked the leads, pulled the plug, saying each step out loud, like a rule. And I plugged in Hank's old oil-filled space heater, the one I'd fixed that very first night. Its heat on my own money, through Hank's meter. Val said it was the most honest heat of her life. Half an hour later, it was warm enough in the garage to take off a sweater.

Val took off her sweater.

This time it wasn't like the back room in the department. Without the laughing at first, and without the cot. Quieter. Slower. I took off the ring and set the thimble on the workbench, next to her glasses. The valve was shut. All the way. I didn't want any power at all.

She sat on Hank's sagging couch, on his quilted work jacket, in just the plaid shirt she wore under her sweater: a man's shirt, sleeves rolled up, two sizes too big. Under it, when she undid the top buttons, was that same gray bra with the stretched-out strap. No bow today. "I didn't know we'd make up," she said, and laughed. I looked at her: at her ears, red from the cold, at the glasses on the workbench, at the way she tucked her cold feet in their wool socks up under her, and I thought that I'd almost lost all of this over the words "not now." She whispered, "Don't rush. I want to remember this." And I didn't rush. The rest was only ours.

And afterward, her laughter. Laughter at last. When the couch under us made a sound that even the cot in the department had never made.

"Hank's going to find out," she said.

"Hank knows everything there is to know about this couch," I said. "He's been sleeping on it for six years. He just doesn't say anything."

Val fell asleep on my shoulder. The heater hummed. On the workbench, side by side, lay the thimble, the ring and her glasses. The thimble and the ring were warm. Both of them. Quietly. As if they were talking.

I looked at them and thought that the ring would need years more to become a real object. Or maybe not years. Maybe it just needs to lie next to somebody. And maybe the next ring would grow next to this one, more slowly, but in a chain. Mrs. Mazur had promised the silver for it: in her china cabinet she has six silver-plated cupronickel spoons and one real silver one, from her graduation.

Like a shell. Like the bronze in the lamp. Like us.

---

In the morning Hank opened up the garage, looked at the meter, at the humming heater, at the couch, at the two of us, sleepy, sharing one quilted jacket, and said:

"You're paying for the electricity yourselves."

"We are," I said.

"And for the couch," said Hank. "Six years it never squeaked on me. All right. Don't pay. Let it squeak. At least something in this garage is alive." He looked at the workbench, at the thimble and the ring, at Professor Stern's notebooks in the box under the workbench, where they lived now, and said: "I'm not asking, same as Ashot. I can just see I won't be needing the welder anymore. But when do I get my vise?"

"This week," I said. "Out of scrap. Like I promised."

He went over to his car. Val buried her face in my shoulder and laughed, without a sound, so hard the couch shook.

And I wrote in my notebook, right there, on my knee:

"An object isn't made. It's grown. Next to another object. For a long time."

And below that:

"Ring number one. Half of Grandma's silver. Working. Ten times weaker. Electric bill: a bag and change a night, fifteen nights, twenty bags or so. Best money I ever spent."

And further down, after a pause:

"Coauthors: Stern, Finch, Nagel. When there are forty of us. Auction: March sixth."
