# Chapter 28. A Material Question

A year ago, in October, I had minus forty-seven thousand, a broken fridge, a cat and a booth at the Central Market.

Now I have zero. Exactly. No minus sign.

The fridge is there. The cat's still around. The booth is at the new market, under the plastic roof, with Ashot across the aisle and Greg next door. And I also have a house. A small one. Twenty feet by twenty. I built it myself. Well, not myself. By myself I'd have been building it till spring. Assuming I hadn't used myself up first.

I'll start with the debt, because that's where it all started.

---

I brought Bull the last payment on Friday, August seventh.

Two hundred forty-eight bags since October, plus interest, minus what Bull had quietly paid in for me in February out of his own pocket, "for the valve." I only found out about that in August, when I went over the numbers with Old Pete. Old Pete reconciled everything down to the last hand pie, looked at the total, took off his hat and said:

"It balances. Without any property management company."

I took the money to Bull's office, the first time I'd been there all year: third floor over a pawn shop, instant coffee and old linoleum. Bull was sitting at his desk, no tie, in his work jacket, and lying in front of him wasn't paperwork but a camshaft.

"The last one?" he asked.

"The last one."

Bull counted it. Slowly, the way he always does. He wrote out a receipt: "Received in full. No further claims." Signed it. Then he took my file out of the drawer (thick, gray, going back to October) and laid the receipt on top.

"You know what I'm going to do now?" he asked.

"Tear it up?"

"No," said Bull. "You can't tear it up, it's a document. It goes in the archive. By the book. And then I'll put in my notice." He took out a second sheet, already written. "'I hereby resign, of my own free will.' Signed it this morning. I finished working off my own debt to them in July. Yours was the last one. I was waiting for you."

"George…"

"That's it," he said. "Now I'm nobody to you."

He wasn't nobody to me. But I understood what he meant.

Two weeks later a sign went up on the rusty shell of the old truck depot, next to the new market. Tin; Ashot cut it out, and I straightened it: BULL. AUTO REPAIR. I LISTEN TO ENGINES.

Half of Bull's jobs are ordinary. Oil, brake pads, belts. The other half are "reshaping." That's how he writes it in his log: "Valve. Reshaping. Half a bag." In September his books came up for a scheduled audit. Ms. Pratt recused herself as a coauthor, and afterward a colleague of hers called and asked what "reshaping" meant. Ms. Pratt said, "Not provided for. But not prohibited either." And left it at that.

Bull has a ring of his own now: a new one, grown in the spring from his old one, the third. After the manual came out, Ms. Pratt rewrote point six of her workshop instructions: "A ring stays with whoever has passed the test. The test is given by Ms. Pratt." Bull passed on the first try. He never takes the ring off. He says that without it he hears an engine worse, and with it he hears it "like a wife."

Bull doesn't have a wife. But he says that with a shop like this, now he will.

---

I built the house in the summer.

Mrs. Mazur found the lot. An abandoned one, out at the Appleseed garden plots, over the fence from her vegetable garden. A seventh of an acre of weeds; nobody had taken it in two years, because it was a long walk from the bus stop and there was no water. The owner had moved in with her daughter, and she sold it to me for two hundred bags, "just so somebody mows the grass." Goldie did the paperwork in a week. She said it was the smallest deal of her life and the most pleasant.

Two hundred bags. That was all the money I had left after the debt. Ashot and Mrs. Mazur lent me a little more, with no interest and no IOU. I'd paid them back by October: Ashot in money, Mrs. Mazur in pies for the housewarming, as agreed. That's why I'm at zero now and not in the minus.

And then I built. And not alone.

The foundation: concrete blocks from the truck depot, straightened. The frame: pipe from the dump, crimped, the way we did it at the market; Ms. Pratt said every joint out loud again. The walls: polyethylene panels, sorted the way Val taught us, woven by me, double, with air in between, like a double-pane window. Behind the stove, a wall of Hank's glass and brick from the depot: Ms. Pratt wouldn't sign off on the roof without it. The windows: Hank's glass, cloudy and thick, like in a bathhouse. Light gets in, and you can't see the neighbors. Roxanne said it was ideal for seedlings. Bull and Hank dug the well. The pump came out of "the shelter."

The roof went up on a Saturday. Everybody who'd held joints in March came. And another thirty or so people from the workshop. In one day. Without a single creak. In July I turned thirty-three; Mrs. Mazur baked a napoleon with two hundred fifty-six layers.

On the roof there are four panels. Mine, made from river sand. Not the size of my hand like the first one: three feet by five, each. Silicon, boron, phosphorus, a silver mesh. I made them in Hank's garage, one night per panel, on Corey's line: about two bags apiece on the meter. In December one hand-sized square had cost me four nights. Understanding is cheaper. For the silver mesh I used the half of Grandma's little bar that had come back to me, signed for, along with the pea. The last of it. Mrs. Mazur's half handle I gave back to Mrs. Mazur. The pea I sold to the pawn shop, with the paperwork and the analysis: "It has papers, so it's a thing." It paid for the roofing nails. Of Grandma's spoon I had nothing left by then except the ring on my finger, the first one, the one all the others had grown from, and the crumb in the old wafer out on the balcony. Or so I thought at the time.

During the day the panels charge two batteries from Double-A; in the evening they run the lights and the fridge. Nothing more than that. For anything more there's a line from the garden association, through a meter and Corey's panel with the protection. Corey came out to install it himself. He looked at the panels, checked them with his multimeter and, without a word, redid all my grounding. He said, "'Electricity' section, point one. I wrote it, I answer for it." He comes out twice a month. He says it isn't friendship, it's a coauthor's responsibility.

I don't have much money. The booth, knives, repairs. The workshop on Sundays: free. But I hardly need money. Half the lot is Mrs. Mazur's vegetable garden, and she says it belongs to both of us now. Heat: firewood and a wood stove, Hank's design, model two. Light: the panels. Food: the hand pies everybody brings to the workshop, and potatoes from the garden. The fridge: secondhand, out of "the shelter"; I rebuilt the compressor in an evening. The landlady's fridge, the quiet one, stayed with the landlady. She sent me a smiley face.

Old Pete kept the books for my house too. The last entry for September read:

"Nagel, T. Monthly expenditure: thirty bags. Was: a hundred-plus. Reason: learned how."

---

Val lives in two places.

At her place, in her room on the other side of town, there are copper sulfate crystals, a periodic table and the bed she sleeps in when she's writing. At mine there's everything else. Her toothbrush. Her old lab coat with the burn holes, on the hook by the door: the department issued her a new one, without holes. Notebooks. Her stool, which she brought over "because your chairs may have four legs, but they've got personalities."

The paper was accepted in September.

The journal she'd sent it to at two-twenty in the morning first wrote back with exactly what she'd been afraid of: "The method has previously been made publicly available, which conflicts with the journal's policy." Val read the letter, took off her glasses and said, "Zeros get printed too. Just not here." And went to wash the dishes, although there weren't any dishes.

A week later a second letter came. From the editor in chief. He wrote that he'd read the manual, repeated the glass test in his own department, gotten minus three tenths for one student and zero for everybody else, and that if this was true, then "the journal's policy" could wait. They took the paper as a "report of a reproducible effect." There were four reviewers. Three of them repeated the glasses. The fourth wrote that it was fraud and demanded the raw records. Keyes has been coming on Sundays since April (his glass is still a zero), and he keeps the records, as carefully as an interview transcript, with signatures and dates. Val sent the reviewer her series and Keyes's records. The reviewer didn't write again.

The paper comes out in December. Stern, Finch, Nagel, Rusk. And Mrs. Stern in the acknowledgments. I checked the galley proofs myself.

Dr. Andrews heard about the paper from the dean. He came to the lab, stood by Val's desk for a long time and finally said that "the department is proud," and that on the next report he'd put her name first.

"Don't," said Val. "Put your own. I've got places to put mine."

He left, and according to Aunt Tess at the library, he then sat in his office for half the day with the door shut, taking valerian drops.

---

Workshops have sprung up all over the country. I don't know how many there are. Nobody knows. Mikey tried to count them; he made a map on the manual's website, and by October the map had a hundred forty-two dots. But Mikey says those are only the ones who checked in. How many people are just reading it and trying the glasses in their kitchens, nobody knows.

Mikey himself runs glass-of-water tests for sixth graders on Saturdays, only with Ms. Pratt's form. Lana signs every one. He's sixteen, and he says "where the edge is" before he says "how."

Ms. Pratt and Old Pete now have consultations on Tuesdays and Thursdays. Old Pete calls it reconciling the accounts. Mrs. Mazur calls it a date. Ms. Pratt calls it "formally permissible."

Where the Central Market used to be, there's been a foundation pit since April. There's water in it, and ducks. In May the district attorney's office had Granton Structural Survey's assessment voided. No tenants turned up for the shopping center: everybody had moved out past the bypass. Goldie left her father's company; she has a sign at the new market now, LEGAL SERVICES. COCHRAN. These days she writes her last name. Her father called her in July. They talked for an hour. What about, she didn't say. But that Saturday she took him a pie from Mrs. Mazur.

The manual came out in a second edition. Ms. Pratt was the editor in chief. She added a ninth point to the instructions: "An instructor teaching a class first reads the first page out loud. All of it." That was because of the school a couple of counties over. The chemistry teacher wrote to us herself afterward, apologized and asked for a permission form. Ms. Pratt drew up the form. It's up on the website. It's been downloaded eleven thousand times.

The second edition has a new section. It's called "How to Hold the Knot." The author signed it with two initials.

The section came in June, not by mail: again, a paper sack hanging on my door handle. Twenty pages in small, even handwriting. Diagrams. Tables. Costs in kilowatt-hours. And one sentence at the end, which Val didn't touch:

"Holding is cheaper than easing off. I didn't know that for twenty years. Until March twenty-second, I had never once tried."

Glen never did come to the workshop. But in September he came to the market.

---

It was a Saturday, at eleven. I was in my booth, sharpening a knife for Greg. Across the aisle, Ashot was tapping. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

And suddenly he stopped.

I looked out. Glen was standing at Ashot's counter. In a long coat, no hat, white gloves. Thin, stooped, gray-haired. Only his face wasn't as gray as it used to be. So he'd gone out in the sun this summer after all. Into his yard, at least.

Ashot looked at him without a word. Then he took a box out from under the counter.

"Boots," said Ashot. "Size eleven and a half. As ordered. Box calf, leather soles, cut from an old pattern. Your driver took the measurements; I don't like doing it that way, but fine. You want to try them on?"

"I do," said Glen.

He sat down on Ashot's shoe-stool. Took off his shoes: very expensive, very clean, with soles that didn't have a single scratch on them. Put on the boots. Stood up. Walked. Three steps there, three steps back, on the concrete floor of the new market.

"Good," he said.

"I know they're good," said Ashot. "I've been making them this way for thirty years. For ten years nobody ordered any. You're the first."

Glen paid with a large bill and said, "Keep the change." Ashot counted out the change anyway. Glen looked at it the way he'd once looked at the hand pies, and took it.

Then he turned around. And saw me.

We stood facing each other across the aisle, the way we once had at the fence behind the truck depot. Only without the rail.

"Hello, Timothy."

"Hello, Glen."

"How are the boots?" he asked suddenly. Not me. Himself.

"Ashot does good work," I said.

"Ashot Sarkisian," said Glen, "makes boots better than I reshape. He knows leather by hand. I know leather by formula. Those are different things."

He was quiet for a moment.

"Did the section reach you?"

"It did. It's in the second edition. There's one sentence Val didn't touch."

"And the rest, she edited?"

"And the rest, she edited."

Glen nodded. He looked over at Mrs. Mazur's kitchen by the entrance. Mrs. Mazur was standing there in her apron, with a sheet pan of hand pies in front of her and a line of six people.

He stood there awhile. Then he walked over to the kitchen. Got in line. Seventh. Waited his turn. Mrs. Mazur saw him when there were two people left ahead of him, and didn't say anything; she only wiped her hands on her apron.

"One," said Glen. "Cabbage."

"Cabbage is all gone," said Mrs. Mazur. "I've got potato."

Glen thought about it.

"Potato."

Mrs. Mazur gave him a hand pie. He paid. He stepped over to a counter, took off his right glove and ate the hand pie. Bare-handed. Standing up. In the middle of the market.

Then he put the glove back on, picked up the box with his old shoes and left.

He didn't come to the workshop, not in September and not in October.

But Ashot says Glen has ordered another pair of boots. Winter ones. Fur-lined.

When Mrs. Stern heard about it, all she said was:

"There you are. The third time has already happened. Now he'll keep coming."

---

Mom came on the last Sunday in October.

A year ago, in October, she used to call me on Sundays at eleven and ask whether I was eating soup. Now she came herself. On the bus from Aunt Wanda's, four hours, with her carryall, and in the carryall a jar of jam, a jar of pickles, and the socks she's knitted me every winter since Dad died.

I met her at the bus stop. We walked through the garden plots, past Mrs. Mazur's vegetable garden, and Mom kept looking around the whole way, as if she were checking whether I'd lied to her on the phone.

Then she saw the house.

She stopped at the gate. She looked for a long time. At Hank's cloudy glass. At the white walls made of panels. At the roof with four dark rectangles on it. At the wood-stove chimney, with a little smoke coming out of it. At Lugnut, who was sitting on the porch looking at Mom with the expression of a homeowner whose guests have turned up uninvited.

"You did this?" asked Mom.

"We did," I said. "Me and forty-odd other people."

Mom went inside. She looked at the stove. At the table made out of Mikey's pallets. At the shelves of notebooks: fifty-two of them in a row, Stern's and Glen's. At Val's lab coat on the hook. She didn't say anything about the lab coat. She only looked at me. With that look that had everything in it: "I knew it," and "just like your father," and "well, finally."

Val came out around lunchtime, with a pie from Mrs. Mazur. Mom and Val shook hands, looked each other over, and five minutes later they were already talking about me in the third person, as if I weren't in the room. I went out on the porch, to Lugnut. It was calmer out there.

---

And on Monday morning, the thimble was gone.

Since the spring I'd been taking it off at night. Setting it on the windowsill. That had been the custom since the night we published: take it off, don't hide it. Lugnut had never once touched it. Half a year. The thimble lay on the windowsill, and Lugnut walked past it the way he'd walk past a glass of water. Doesn't shine: not interesting.

And on Monday the windowsill was empty.

I searched the house. Under the stove, behind the shelves, under Val's stool, in Mom's carryall. Mom was offended, then she helped look. Val searched methodically, square by square, like in the lab.

What I found in the end was a stash. A new one. Under the porch, in the gap between the foundation and the first step. Lugnut sat beside me and looked at me the way you look at someone who has finally caught on.

In the stash were: three of Val's paper clips. A nut. A button off my work jacket. A silver candy-bar wrapper. The cap off one of Mikey's markers.

And a ring.

Thin, simple, a smooth band. Gone black. Grandma's. The very one I'd made the first night, a year ago, out of the handle of the spoon, for Mom, and that Lugnut had made off with while I was putting the kettle on.

I squatted by the porch, holding it in my palm.

So he hadn't lost it. Hadn't dropped it on some garage roof. He'd kept it. For a year. He'd brought it from the apartment to the house when we moved, and I don't know how. In his teeth, or in a box of notebooks, or in my jacket pocket. A year.

But the thimble wasn't in the stash.

---

I looked for it for three more days. Mikey came out with a metal detector he'd built out of an old music player. The metal detector found two nails, a jar lid and an old coin that had probably been lost by the lot's last owner. It didn't find the thimble.

Val said, "It's gone." She said it calmly, scientifically, without any panic. The way you state the result of an experiment.

I'd thought I would panic. A year ago I would have. A year ago I'd have dug up the whole lot, the garden plots and half the town.

But I didn't panic.

I sat on the porch with the ring in my palm. The ring was black. Silver tarnishes from sulfur, which is in the air, in rubber, in eggs. The black is silver sulfide; Val had explained it to me last fall, about Roxanne's pruners. You can clean it off with toothpaste. You can do it with baking soda and foil in hot water. Or you can…

I took off my own ring and gave it to Val: a contaminated sample has to be free of impurities. Then I laid Grandma's ring on my bare palm. No thimble. No ring of my own. Just on my palm.

And I listened.

At first, nothing. Then, like that time in January, when I held the thimble in my palm and felt the funnels. Faint. From far off. But there. Silver. Soft, warm, like butter. And on top of it, a thin film, a foreign one, like dust on glass. Sulfur. Only the tiniest bit: a film thinner than a soap bubble.

I asked. Not to hand it over: to let it go. For the sulfur to leave. Anywhere. Into the air.

The sulfur didn't leave right away. Without the thimble there's no click and done. Without the thimble it was like pushing a car with your shoulder: slow, heavy, an inch at a time.

I sat there a long time. Lugnut sat beside me. Val stood in the doorway and didn't interfere.

Then the ring got lighter. Not shiny: lighter. Gray, like an overcast sky. Along the edge of the band a white stripe came through. Pure silver. Thin as a thread. But real.

It cost me two of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, my lunch, and ice-cold fingers that I spent half an hour afterward warming on a mug.

And there was a smell of rotten egg. Just a little. The sulfur had left with the moisture (from my palm, from the damp air) as hydrogen sulfide. Same as with the soda and the foil: that smells too.

"You did that without it," said Val from the doorway.

"A little."

"A little," Val agreed. "Contaminated sample. Point two."

She took a notebook out of the pocket of her lab coat and wrote it down.

---

The rest I cleaned off with toothpaste. By hand. Like everybody else.

Mom left on Wednesday evening. I walked her to the bus stop. We stood under the streetlight; the bus was late, and Mom was holding her carryall, which now had Mrs. Mazur's hand pies in it instead of pickles.

I took out the ring.

"Mom," I said. "This is for you. I made it a year ago. Out of Grandma's spoon. You always said you can't wear a spoon."

Mom took the ring. Looked at it. Recognized not the ring, but something in it. Maybe Grandma's spoon. Maybe me.

"You made it?" she asked.

"I did. A year ago. Then Lugnut stole it. And now he's given it back. And I cleaned it myself. Without…" I didn't know how to put it. "Without the tool."

Mom put the ring on. On the ring finger of her left hand, where her wedding ring used to be, the one she'd sold during the hard years along with the spoons. The ring was a little too big. She closed her fingers so it wouldn't slip off.

"Your father always said you'd have made a good scientist," said Mom. "I told you that."

"You told me."

"He wasn't wrong," said Mom. She paused. "He just didn't know a scientist could fix things. And be a good man, too."

The bus pulled up. Mom climbed onto the step, turned around and said from the doorway, hurriedly, the way people say something they haven't been able to say for a long time:

"I'm proud of you, Tim, sweetheart. Eat your soup."

The doors closed. The bus drove off.

I stood at the bus stop under the streetlight and thought that I was thirty-three years old, and that this was the first time Mom had said it out loud.

---

The thimble never did turn up.

Val says Lugnut might have dropped it into the gap under the foundation, and that someday, a hundred years from now, somebody will be digging in the garden and find it. Mikey says Lugnut might have carried it off to the neighbors, and that we should go over the whole garden association with the metal detector. Old Pete says they did it on purpose. When Mrs. Stern heard, she was quiet for a long time, and then she said:

"Before he died, Ephraim told me: 'Give it to someone who fixes things. Not to someone who sells them.' Maybe the cat knows who fixes things better than we do."

I think she's right.

It's lying somewhere. Dark one day, light the next, worn like an old coin. Warm with somebody else's warmth, the warmth of whoever puts it on. Maybe deep in somebody's vegetable garden. Maybe in a neighbor's shed. Maybe on somebody's windowsill, in another town, in another booth, belonging to somebody who's at minus forty-seven thousand with a broken fridge.

Let it lie there a while. Let someone who fixes things find it. And if someone who sells things finds it? Then nine hundred ninety-nine others will hold. We've verified it.

Rings grow from rings now. More slowly, "like a retelling." But they grow.

And I don't really need it anymore. I realized that on the porch, with the black ring in my palm. Not because I've gotten stronger. Without it I'm weak. Without it I'm like Mrs. Mazur without a ring: just a little: forty minutes for one ring.

But there are a lot of us.

And that, I think, was the material question.

---

On Sunday, November first, there were thirty-eight people at the workshop. I came without the thimble. I said I'd lost it. Ashot shook his head. Bull said, "Fine by me." Ms. Pratt said, "Point ten: an instructor may work without the tool." And wrote it down.

Mrs. Mazur gave me a hand pie. Cabbage.

And that evening Old Pete closed his ledger. The old one. The first one. The one he'd started last fall, when he wrote on the first page: "Iron repair. Twelve hand pies." And a line below that: "Balance: Nagel minus thirty-one."

On the last page he wrote:

"November first. Totals for the year. Nagel, T.: was minus thirty-one. Now: doesn't fit in the column. Debts: none."

He thought it over. Then he added at the bottom, in small letters:

"The material question: closed. The others: open."

And he opened a new book.
