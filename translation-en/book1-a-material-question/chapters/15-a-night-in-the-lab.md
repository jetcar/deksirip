# Chapter 15. A Night in the Lab

On Saturday I got there an hour early.

That was a mistake too, just with the opposite sign. That Wednesday, back at the very beginning, I hadn't gotten off the bus at all. This time I got off a stop before the Poly so I wouldn't get there too early, and I still got there too early. Then I spent forty minutes walking around the building in the snow, like a man who's planning to rob it. I had a tote under my arm. In the tote were three thermoses, a paper sack of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, a coil of copper wire and an old bedsheet.

The sheet was Val's idea. "Cotton. Old. One you won't miss." Mine was exactly that kind. It was thirty years old or so, it had come with the rented apartment, and it had a faded bear printed on it from some long-ago celebration. Strictly speaking, whether to miss it or not was the landlady's call. But the landlady lived in another town, and I doubt she remembered the sheet. The bear had stopped smiling a long time ago. He gazed up off the sheet wearily, like someone who's seen a lot.

I didn't know what she wanted the sheet for. I'd thought about it the whole way there. I had two theories, and I liked them both. That made it hard to concentrate.

At five to seven I climbed the front steps.

---

The security guard sat on the first floor, in a glass booth by the turnstile. He was an older man in a blue uniform jacket over a sweater, with a crossword and a thermos even older than mine. On the desk in front of him lay the visitor log, a telephone and the alarm panel. On the panel, a single green light blinked.

"Here for Finch?" he asked without looking up.

"How did you know?"

"She told me you'd be coming." He turned the log around and jabbed a finger at a line. "Fume hood man. Sign here. Gus."

"You're Gus?"

"I'm Gus," he agreed. "And you sign."

I signed. In the "Purpose of visit" column, in Val's handwriting, it said: "Ventilation repair. Long job." The word "Long" was underlined.

Gus looked at my tote. A corner of the sheet with the bear on it was sticking out.

"For the fume hood?" he asked.

"Rags," I said.

"Rags," he repeated, in a tone that suggested he was entering the word in a separate, special column. "I do my rounds at midnight. Third floor last. Turn off the kettle."

And he went back to his crossword. I headed for the stairs and heard him say to himself, "Ten letters, instrument for measuring humidity…" I wanted to help him out, but I didn't know.

---

The inorganic chemistry lab was different at night. In the daytime it was a long room full of cabinets, benches and students. At night every sound had left it except the hum of the reagent fridge and the ticking of the clock over the door. Only one light was on, over a single bench. Val was standing under it.

She wasn't wearing her lab coat. That was the first thing I noticed, and for a long while afterward I couldn't stop noticing it. A blue sweater with a wide neck, jeans, hair pinned up, the pencil in its place. Glasses. The fume hood against the wall was already humming: Val had turned it on ahead of time. The lab coat hung on a hook by the door, with its little burn holes, as always.

"You're on time," she said. "I'm shocked. I figured it'd be another Wednesday."

"I got off a stop early," I said. "And then I walked around for forty minutes."

"I saw," said Val. "The window faces that way. You walked past the spruce three times. Gus probably thought you were casing the place."

"He thinks I'm fixing the fume hood."

"Then that's what you're doing," said Val. "It says so in the log. And the log is an official document."

I'd actually fixed the fume hood back in November. It was one of the patients from Val's graveyard: the fan had sat dead for five years because the motor's winding had burned out, and nobody had ever bought the department a new motor. I rewound it in one evening. I drew the copper wire myself and put the varnish on by hand: I pulled the wire through a wad of cotton soaked in varnish from Val's cabinet, dried it over the hot plate and only then wound it. You can't wind the coils in bare copper; they'll short. Varnish is a polymer, and I don't understand polymers. That one evening paid for an hour of chemistry. Now the hood hummed like a good-natured vacuum cleaner. It pulled air so hard you could have fed it the cigarette smoke of the whole building, if anybody in the building still smoked.

"Did you bring the sheet?" asked Val.

I took out the sheet. The bear looked at Val. Val looked at the bear.

"Perfect," she said. "I almost feel sorry for him."

"Me too. What's it for?"

Val took a box off the shelf and set it down in front of me. Inside the box was a skein of thread. Shiny, smooth, the color of heavy cream. I touched a strand. It slid between my fingers like water.

"Silk," I said.

"Not silk," said Val. "Rayon. They also call it artificial silk. Do you know what it's made of?"

"Silkworms that quit smoking?"

"Cotton," said Val. "Or wood. The same stuff your bear is made of. Cellulose. Long chains of glucose, one after another, like railroad cars."

She took the edge of the sheet and held it up to the light for me.

"This is cotton thread. It's spun from short fibers, two or three centimeters each. Cotton, by the way, is stronger than rayon. But it's fuzzy: the ends of the fibers stick out in every direction, and the light scatters off them. That's why it's dull. And this," she held up the skein of rayon, "is the same cellulose, only they dissolved it and then squeezed it out through a tiny hole into one long continuous strand. A smooth one. That's why it shines. Light bounces off something smooth evenly. The catch is that when you dissolve it, the chains break into pieces, so the thread comes out weaker than cotton."

"And what do we need?"

"What we need is smooth like rayon and stronger than cotton," said Val. "Without dissolving it. You're not breaking anything. You're rearranging."

"So we're going to make silk out of a bear."

"We're going to run an experiment," said Val. "A real one. With a notebook."

She opened a thick notebook with an oilcloth cover. At the top of the first page it said: "Series One. Precision and Understanding." Below that was a table, ruled with a straightedge.

---

Here's what she'd come up with.

For thirty years, Professor Stern had written that precision equals understanding. I'd been seeing it for myself since the very first night. The chair whose leg turned into charcoal. The latch on Mikey's phone that crumbled after an hour. The knives that got cheaper once Val drew me a diagram. But those were all cases. Stories. And Val needed numbers.

"First you make a thread knowing nothing," she said. "Just 'make me a shiny thread out of this.' I measure: how much you ate, how much the thread can hold, how long you fussed with it. Then I explain one level to you. What cellulose is. You make it again. I measure. Then the second level: how the chains are laid down and why. Again. Then the third: hydrogen bonds between the chains, the thing that holds them together. And we watch how the cost and the strength change."

"And if nothing changes?"

"Then Professor Stern was wrong, and that's a result too." She pushed up her glasses. "In science, 'nothing changed' is an answer. It's just that nobody wants to publish it."

I looked at the table. At the columns: "Eaten, in hand pies," "Strength, in grams," "Time, in minutes," "Notes." The last column was the widest.

For each thread, Val cut an identical square out of the sheet, about the size of my palm, and weighed it on the same analytical balance I'd fixed back then. To the ten-thousandth of a gram. If a square came out heavier, she plucked a thread out of it with tweezers. I said at that rate she could be weighing till morning. Val said that's what science looks like, and everything else is cooking.

"I brought the hand pies," I said.

"I know," said Val. "Mrs. Mazur posted in the group: 'Twelve for Timothy at the lab, so he doesn't embarrass himself.' Pete logged it in the ledger. Your balance after the evening orders is plus forty-something. But he's opened a new account for you. It's called 'Science.' Minus twelve."

"That's science for you," I said. "Science is always in the red."

---

I made the first thread "knowing nothing."

I laid my palm, with the thimble, on the first square and asked for long, shiny, smooth. The cotton stirred under my fingers. It felt like trying to comb out badly tangled hair without being able to see it. The fibers stretched, snapped, snagged on each other. A thread crept out of the square, thin as sewing thread, fuzzy and gray, thirty feet or so of it; I wound it onto a pencil. It looked like a very sad piece of string for very small packages.

I ate two hand pies while I made it. Sweat broke out on my temples. Half a thermos went.

Val tied the thread to a ring stand and hung an unbent paper clip from the bottom of it as a hook. On the hook she hung a plastic flask and poured water into it from a graduated cylinder. "A hundred milliliters is a hundred grams, plus the flask, which is forty," she said. "I'm not hanging the weights from the analytical balance on a thread. Those are for tweezers." Forty grams, the empty flask. Fifty. A hundred. At a hundred and twenty, the thread quietly came apart in the middle, as if it were tired.

"A hundred and twenty grams," Val wrote down. "Two hand pies and half a thermos. Six minutes. Note: 'Looks like a loofah from a bathhouse where people go to scrub off despair.'"

"That's not a scientific note."

"It's an observation," said Val. "Observation is the foundation of science."

Then she explained the first level to me.

She drew on the blackboard in chalk. A hexagon with corners: that's glucose. A hexagon hooked onto the next hexagon through an oxygen: that's cellulose. A chain thousands of links long. I looked at the board, and suddenly I could feel it in the sheet. Not see it: feel it. As if the cotton under my hand had stopped being soft mush and become something that had a structure. Railroad cars. Couplings.

The second thread came out lighter and more even. From the same size square, it was thinner and longer. A hand pie and a half. It held two hundred and sixty grams.

Val wrote it down. Her eyes were shining.

The second level. Inside each fiber, the chains don't lie any which way: they run in a spiral, at an angle to its length, in layers. That's how the plant laid them down, so the fiber would bend instead of breaking. In flax that angle is smaller, which is why linen is stronger. And if you straighten out the spiral and lay the chains lengthwise, along the whole length of the thread, it'll be stronger than linen. Val took a bundle of matches and rolled it between her palms into a twist: that's cotton. Then she straightened them back into a neat bundle: that's what we need.

I made the third thread thinking about matches. I didn't pull on the fibers. I unwound their spiral and laid each chain lengthwise, all pointing one way, like matches in a box. It was slower. But it was easier. As if the chains themselves wanted to lie straight and had only been waiting for somebody to show them which way.

The thread came out thin and smooth, without any fibers sticking out. And it shone. Not like the store-bought rayon. But under the lamp, light ran along it.

One hand pie. Four hundred and ten grams.

"You see?" said Val. Her voice was shaking. "Do you see? More than three times as strong, at half the cost. That's a curve, Tim. It's not a story, it's a curve."

The third level was the hardest. Hydrogen bonds. Weak attractions between neighboring chains, and there are more of them in every thread than there are stars in the sky. Each one on its own means nothing, but all of them together hold the fiber together. Val explained it for about twenty minutes, and for about fifteen of them I honestly didn't get it. Then she said:

"Picture the market."

"Picturing it."

"Each booth by itself is nothing. One gust of wind and it's down. But they stand right up against each other, shoulder to shoulder, and hold each other up. Ashot holds you, you hold Greg, Greg holds Lottie. One bond is nothing. A thousand is an aisle."

And I got it. Right away. The way you get things not with your head but with your hands.

I made the fourth thread in about ten minutes, and with almost no hunger at all. I laid the chains lengthwise and fitted them together so tightly that each one held on to its neighbor. Right up against each other. Booth to booth. The thread flowed out of the square, smooth and cool, and lay down on the bench in a thin, shiny little snake. I'd cut that square from right next to the bear's head, and the bear lost an ear. I felt sorry for him. A tiny bit.

Val poured in water. A hundred. Two hundred. Five hundred. Seven hundred. At nine hundred grams, the flask dropped. The thread hadn't snapped: the paper-clip hook had straightened out. The flask landed softly on the towel she'd put underneath.

We both stared at the straightened paper clip. Then at each other.

"Half a hand pie," Val said very quietly, and wrote it down. "Over nine hundred grams. Ten minutes. Note…"

She thought about it.

"Note: 'The department's paper clip proved weaker than understanding.'"

Then she closed the notebook and laid both hands on top of it. The way you lay your hands on something warm.

"That's a paper," she said. "Not the silicon, where I don't know how to explain where it came from. This. About how precision depends on what you know. If I'm ever able to tell anybody about this…"

She didn't finish. I didn't finish it for her either. "If I'm ever" hung between us like a thread nobody had tested for strength yet.

The clock over the door said twenty to eleven.

---

"It's time," said Val.

"Time for what?"

"Series Two," she said.

I looked at her. She was standing by the bench with her notebook, with the pencil in her hair, and she was looking at me nothing like the way she'd looked at the flask of water. I felt the keys stir in my pocket.

"Hold the valve," said Val.

"I'm holding it."

"Don't," she said. "I've changed my mind. Hold only the most important part. Don't use it on me. Don't use it on yourself. Us: hands only. And everything else…"

She swept her hand around the lab.

"Anything that isn't alive, on me or around us: you may. Buttons, you may. The zipper, you may. The sheet, all the more so; it's already missing an ear. Department furniture, you may, only put it back afterward; it's on the inventory. I'm giving you permission. In advance. Out loud. In front of a witness."

"What witness?"

She pointed to the bear on the sheet. The bear looked weary.

"Are you sure?" I asked. My voice had gone hoarse again, like on the balcony.

"Tim," said Val. "I invited you to an empty building on a Saturday night, asked you to bring a bedsheet and wrote you into the log with the word 'Long.' Underlined. I'm sure. Are you?"

I said yes. I didn't say it very clearly. But she understood.

We turned off the lamp over the bench. The fume hood went on humming in the dark.

---

The back room behind the lab was a small room with no window. It held a set of shelves full of boxes, a sink, a cast-iron radiator as hot as a stove, an electric hot plate the lab techs used to make coffee during long syntheses, and a cot. The cot was old: aluminum, on springs, with canvas faded to the color of wet sand, the kind built back when things were built to outlast the people sleeping on them. A tin tag with an inventory number was nailed to the side of the frame. Val told me three generations of lab techs had slept on that cot, waiting for something of theirs to precipitate.

"It squeaks," Val warned me.

"I noticed," I said, because it had squeaked when she sat down on it.

I spread out what was left of the sheet. The bear ended up down at the foot.

Val took off her glasses and set them on the shelf, next to the coffee can. It was strange. Without her glasses she looked younger and somehow more defenseless. She looked at me nearsightedly and laughed.

"I can hardly see you," she said. "Just outlines. Come closer. For the sake of precision."

I came closer.

I don't know how other people tell this kind of thing. Beautifully, probably. I remember it all in pieces, the way you remember something very important. Her sweater, which smelled of reagents and a little bit of apple. The way she pulled it off over her head herself, and her hair came down, and the pencil flew off somewhere under the shelves, and we both said "Oops." Her shoulders, covered in goose bumps, because it was cool in the back room. Her hands on my shirt. It was a work shirt with metal snaps: after that night with the buttons, I'd stopped wearing plastic ones.

"Snaps," she said. "Go ahead."

I thought about the snaps. And the little spring rings inside them let go, quietly, one after another, top to bottom: click, click, click. Bending is cheap. The snaps didn't crumble. They didn't turn to powder. They just let go, and stayed where they were.

Val watched this with her eyebrows raised.

"Progress," she said. "Last time your buttons turned to powder, and you had to sew mine back on by hand afterward. I'm writing it down: 'Series Two. Experiment One. Snaps intact.'"

"Are you really going to write that down?"

"Don't interrupt."

I didn't interrupt.

Her skin was warm, and where a vein beat under it, hot. I touched her only with my hands. That was hard, because the power wanted more. It wanted to understand Val the way I understood the sheet and the silicon: from the inside, down to the chain. I could feel the thimble reaching for her, for her skin, for her warmth, and I held it back. I held it the way I'd never held it before. My rule. Hands only. Only what a hand can do. Val seemed to understand what was going on with me, because she took my hand, the one with the thimble, and laid it on her waist, on the waistband of her jeans. On the metal button.

"Here," she said. "This one, you may."

The shank of the button on her jeans bent open a little and let go of the buttonhole. And the zipper… The pull tab stayed right where it was. Instead, the teeth under it started coming apart by themselves, one at a time, top to bottom, slowly, with a soft sound, like someone tearing a very long strip of paper. Val laughed, low, and tipped her head back.

"You're doing it this slow on purpose," she said.

"No," I said honestly. "That's how I understand zippers. One tooth at a time."

"Learn faster."

She took her jeans off herself. She stepped out of them as if they were a puddle and stood there in front of me in her underwear, barefoot on the cold linoleum, curling her toes.

Her underwear wasn't the kind you see in the movies. For some reason that's what I remember best. The bra was plain, gray, cotton, with one slightly stretched-out strap that kept slipping off her left shoulder, and she kept fixing it without noticing. And the panties were black lace, with a tiny bow in front, from a completely different set, from a different life. As if she'd gotten dressed that morning as a lab tech and then changed her mind halfway.

Val followed my gaze and blushed. For the first time all evening.

"Don't look at me like that," she said. "It's… I put the top on for work. The bottom, later. At home. On my lunch break. I stood over the drawer for forty minutes wondering if I was an idiot." She paused. "I decided I was. And I put them on anyway."

"You're not an idiot," I said. My voice had gone hoarse again.

"That I know," said Val. "I meant about the bow."

I looked at her and couldn't look enough. In the back room there was one bulb burning, over the sink, and it laid a warm yellow light on Val's skin, and the shadows were blue. She was thin, all lines: sharp collarbones, slender wrists with purple reagent stains, a narrow waist, with a red mark under her ribs left by the waistband of her jeans. Goose bumps on her shoulders. A mole on her left shoulder blade, shaped like a comma: I saw it when she turned to toss her sweater onto the shelf. Her hair, which she usually keeps up with a pencil, lay on her shoulders, dark and heavy, and she kept pushing it off her face with the same gesture she uses to push up her glasses, when there aren't any glasses.

She was beautiful. Not like a picture. Like something you're seeing for the first time and know you're going to remember for the rest of your life, a piece at a time.

And I stood there, afraid.

Not of her. Of myself. The thimble was reaching for her again, for the warmth of her skin, for the steady beat under her collarbone, the same way it had reached for the sheet and the silicon. It wanted to understand her from the inside, down to the chain. And I held it back, harder than I'd ever held it. My rule. Don't touch the living. Her least of all. Now least of all. It suddenly hit me that if the power did even one thing wrong right now, I would never forgive myself, and my hands went cold.

Val noticed. She always notices everything.

"Tim," she said quietly. "Are you holding it?"

"I'm holding it."

"I can tell. Your fingers are white." She took my hand, the one with the thimble, and pressed it to her cheek. Her cheek was hot. "Hold me, not with that. And hold that separately. I trust you. I wouldn't have worn the bow if I didn't trust you."

And then it let go of me. Not the power: the fear.

I'm not going to tell the rest. Not because I'm ashamed. It's just that the rest was only ours. I'll only say that I'd never in my life felt so clumsy and so needed at the same time. That Val laughed when she was happy, and it was the best sound I'd ever heard in my life, better than the hum of a fridge I'd fixed and better than the rustle of Bull counting money. And that at some point she stopped joking, completely, and looked straight at me, without her glasses, as if she could finally see me very clearly.

And I thought: so this is how it can be. Twelve years of the booth, pierogi, debt. And then a back room in the chemistry department, a bulb over the sink, and a woman in mismatched underwear who trusts you more than you trust yourself.

And somewhere in the middle of all that, I stopped holding the valve.

Not on Val. On her, I held it to the very end, the way you hold something cupped in your hands over water. But all around us, I let it go. Completely.

The first thing I felt was that nothing was squeaking under us anymore. The cot springs, which three generations of lab techs knew by heart, went quiet. Then the canvas under Val's back spread out to half its thickness, light as sailcloth, and pulled tight as a drumhead: canvas is cotton too. Then the aluminum frame gave, softly, like wax, and flowed outward, spreading wide. The legs sank, grew shorter, spread out. The tubes got thinner, and the cot got lower and nearly twice as wide: there wasn't any more metal than before; it had just spread out. The head of it rose and curved into a wave, like a crest. The springs went from straight to coiled, soft and tight, and settled inside, under the canvas, in even rows. I didn't know what I was doing, but the power knew what I wanted. And what I wanted, apparently, was for us to have room and for nothing to squeak.

And the sheet under us flowed. What was left of the cotton with the faded bear unwound its spirals and laid its chains lengthwise, tight, right up against each other, booth to booth. Under our hands, under us, cotton was turning into silk. Smooth, cool, slippery. The bear stayed. But now he shone.

Only then did I realize what I was paying for all this with. Or rather, who was paying. The radiator against the wall, which a minute ago had been as hot as a stove, was ice-cold. Frost had formed on the pipe under the sink. A little more and the water in that pipe would freeze, and ice cracks cast iron. I understood that at once, and at once I stopped taking from there. I checked myself, really checked, the way you check your pockets: from Val, not a drop of warmth. Around her I was holding the valve so tight my fingers cramped. The Poly was paying. With the building's heat. I thought I'd have to work it off in fume hoods sometime, and forgot about it almost right away.

Val opened her eyes and ran her palm over the fabric.

"Tim," she said, out of breath. "This… This is the third level."

"What?"

"Hydrogen bonds. You did the third level. Without the blackboard."

And she laughed so hard that we both had to stop for a while.

---

At midnight we heard footsteps.

Far off at first, on the stairs. Shuffling, heavy. Then the jingle of keys. Then a voice mumbling something to the tune of an old song. Gus was doing his rounds. Third floor last. And now it was last.

We froze. Val with her hair down, wrapped in the silk bear; me next to her, in nothing but my socks. We both looked at the door of the back room. The door was shut. Latched. And the latch, I knew for a fact, was held on by a single nail.

The footsteps stopped at the entrance to the lab. The door creaked. The light switch clicked and turned on nothing at all, because earlier that evening Val had unscrewed the bulb over the entrance, "so it doesn't hit me in the eyes."

"Finch," Gus said into the dark. "You in here?"

Val clapped a hand over her mouth. Then she took it away and said, in a perfectly normal, daytime, lab-tech voice:

"In here, Gus!"

"All night again?"

"A synthesis, Gus! A long one!"

"And where's the repairman?"

Val looked at me. I looked at her. I was lying on a bed that half an hour ago had been a cot, and I couldn't say a word.

"In the fume hood!" said Val. "Something's wrong with the motor!"

A pause.

"In the fume hood," Gus repeated, in the same voice he'd used earlier that evening to say "Rags." "Well, let him fix it. Good for him." The footsteps went along the benches. Stopped. "Don't forget the kettle on the hot plate. And put a stopper in your flask; something smells."

"I will!"

"And tell the repairman," Gus added, already from the door, "that an instrument for measuring humidity is a hygrometer. He probably didn't know."

The door closed. The footsteps went off down the stairs.

We lay there without a word for maybe a minute. Then Val buried her face in my shoulder and started shaking. At first it scared me. Then I realized she was laughing. Silently, till the tears came, so hard that the bed under us… didn't squeak once. I checked on purpose. It didn't know how to squeak anymore.

"'Hygrometer,'" Val gasped. "He knows everything. He's known from the start."

"He knew when I was signing in," I said. "He saw the bear."

"He's been working here thirty years," said Val. "He's seen everything. He's seen grad students who went on to become professors. He's seen people come in here 'to fix the fume hood' who've been married for twenty years now." She lifted her head. "You know what he told me the first time I came to the department? 'Finch, there's always somebody in a lab at night. Just so long as nothing catches fire.'"

Nothing had caught fire. Almost.

---

Afterward we lay there and talked. For a long time. Under us was silk, over us the shelves of boxes, and on the other side of the wall, in the lab, the fume hood hummed, the one I was apparently repairing.

Val lay on her back with her hands behind her head, looking at the ceiling. Without her glasses. I don't think she could see the ceiling. She didn't need to.

"You know why I stayed in the department?" she asked.

"Why?"

"I could have left. They offered me a job at a factory lab, quality control. Three times the money. A white coat without holes in it. I said no." She was quiet for a moment. "I was twenty-three, and I wanted to do real science. Discover things. Understand what nobody understands. I thought that's what departments were for."

"And that's not what departments are for?"

"That is what departments are for," said Val. "When there's money. The last time the chemistry department had money was when they bought that fume hood you rewound. For six years I've been washing flasks, running the freshman labs, counting reagents we don't have and writing Dr. Andrews's reports for him, where my name goes in the acknowledgments. In small print. 'The author thanks lab technician V. Finch for her assistance with this work.' Assistance, Tim. I did that work."

I didn't know what to say. I kept quiet and listened.

"And then you came along," said Val. "With a thimble. And a crystal grew in my beaker in a second. And I realized that real science was right here. Lying next to me. It made me a silk sheet out of a bear."

She turned her head and looked at me. Nearsightedly, but very intently.

"I want to understand it," she said. "Not watch you do it. Understand it. Today I watched the curve go down, and it was better than…" She hesitated. "Almost as good as everything else."

"Almost?"

"Don't fish."

Then she said, more quietly:

"I'm afraid of one thing. That I'll end up as your assistant. The way I am for Dr. Andrews. You work the miracle, and I write it down in the notebook and write the acknowledgments. 'Timothy Nagel reshaped the cellulose; lab technician Finch added water to the flask.' I don't want to be the water in the flask."

"You're not the water in the flask," I said. "Without you I'd still be making loofahs out of despair."

"You're saying that now," said Val. "In bed."

"This is a department bed," I said. "It's on the inventory. Lying down is allowed. Lying isn't."

She laughed. But not all the way.

I thought she was right. Not that I'd deceive her. But that this was how it was set up. The power was mine. The thimble was mine. However much Val knew, she was next to the miracle, not inside it. And all of a sudden I remembered my notebook, the last line: "Need more hands. Where?" The word "Where" had turned out to be underlined twice.

"Listen," I said. "In volume twenty-something, the ones where the professor stopped being afraid to write, there's a line. That the thimble isn't a source, it's an amplifier. That the ability itself is something lots of people have, only it's quiet."

"I read that."

"With you it stayed silent," I said. "Remember, that time? But maybe an ear for music can be a quiet one too. And maybe the thimble has nothing to do with it at all. What if you have it?"

Val went silent. For a long time. I felt her tense up beside me, all of her, like a thread under the flask.

"That would be…" she said, and didn't finish.

"Let's test it," I said. "Not now. Later. With the notebook. Series Three."

"Series Three," Val repeated. And for the first time that whole night, I thought she was scared. Truly scared. Not of Gus, not of her bosses, not of getting caught. Of trying, and finding out she didn't have it.

I didn't tell her it would work out. I didn't know. And Val didn't like it when people said things they didn't know.

---

We fell asleep around three. I woke up at seven, from hunger.

The hunger was almost like after the gold. Heavy, animal, hard enough to make my hands shake. All night I'd been paying with the building's heat, but apparently I'd chipped in some myself too. I ate the last four hand pies in a minute, sitting on the edge of the bed, and studied it by the light of the bulb over the sink.

It wasn't a cot. It was something you won't find in any furniture store, warehouse or catalog. Low, wide, with a wave for a headboard, made of matte aluminum. Canvas pulled tight as a drum. Springs inside: even, coiled. One leg was a finger's width shorter than the others: the power knew what I wanted, but it had never heard of a level. The silk bear gazed up off the sheet, and for the first time in thirty years he looked pleased.

And in the middle of the headboard, at the very center of the wave, like a coat of arms, gleamed the tin tag with the inventory number.

"Val," I said. "We have a problem."

Val woke up, felt around for her glasses, put them on and looked.

She looked for a long time.

"This is department furniture," she said at last. "There's an inventory in January. A committee will come. Dr. Andrews, the bookkeeper and Gus with the keys."

"I'll put it back."

"You'll put it back," Val agreed. "But I want to write it down."

She wrote it down. The dimensions. The shape of the headboard. The number of springs. The time. She noted that I had been "in a state close to loss of control." She underlined that. She drew a diagram. In the "Eaten" column she wrote: "Unknown. Had other things on our minds." Below that: "Back room radiator ice-cold. Source: building heat." Then she thought it over and added: "No snaps were harmed."

Then I put the cot back.

That was hard. Much harder than making it. During the night I hadn't known what I was doing, but the power had known what I wanted. In the morning I wanted only one thing: for it to come out a cot. And I didn't remember what it had been like. I didn't understand how it was built: I hadn't studied it while it was in one piece. I touched the frame and tried to remember: two tubes along the sides, crossbars, legs that fold into an X. Straight springs, hooked at the ends.

Twenty minutes and two thermoses later, there was a cot standing on the back-room floor. Narrow, aluminum, on straight springs, with canvas the color of wet sand and the tag on its side. Almost the way it had been.

Val sat down on it. The cot didn't squeak.

Val stood up. The cot folded. By itself. In half.

The wrong way.

We watched it lying on the floor, folded at a right angle, legs in the air, like a beetle somebody had flipped onto its back.

"The committee," said Val, "won't notice. Dr. Andrews hasn't set foot in the back room in twenty years. The bookkeeper doesn't know which way cots are supposed to fold. And Gus…"

"Gus will notice," I said.

"Gus," said Val, "noticed last night."

Then we drank coffee made on the hot plate, in the cold back room, in our jackets, and Val copied the table over clean. The radiator was slowly coming back to life.

---

On our way out, Gus was sitting in his booth with a new crossword and the same thermos. He looked at us. At Val, in her glasses and her sweater, with the pencil in her hair, as if nothing had happened. At me with my empty tote. The bear wasn't sticking out of it anymore: Val had kept the silk sheet.

"Get it fixed?" he asked.

"Got it fixed," I said.

"Synthesis work out?" he asked Val.

"A hundred percent yield, Gus," said Val. "Even more."

"There's no such thing as more than a hundred," said Gus. "I'm telling you that as the security guard of a chemistry building."

He put a check mark in the log. Next to my name, in the "Time out" column. Then he looked at me over his glasses.

"The cot," he said, "next time you don't need to put back. It squeaked for thirty years. I could always hear it at midnight. Two floors down." He paused. "And the radiator on the third floor was cold by morning. I felt the pipe. A synthesis, you said?"

"An endothermic one," said Val.

"Uh-huh," said Gus, and wrote something into his crossword.

We came out onto the front steps. It was snowing. Val took my hand and said she was about to die of embarrassment. She didn't. Instead she laughed so loud, there on the steps, that a cap of snow slid off the spruce.

---

Mom called at eleven, when I was already on the bus home.

"Tim," she said. "Where are you?"

"On the bus, Mom."

"On a Sunday? At eleven?" A pause. I could hear her listening to the bus: the engine, the doors, the next stop being announced. "You didn't come home last night?"

I looked out the window at the snow, at the town, at the spruce by the Poly, which had already slid away behind us. And I said:

"No, Mom. Not home."

Mom was quiet for a long time. Then she said:

"Well, good. Just put a hat on."

---

At home, Lugnut met me in the hall. He sniffed my tote, which was empty, and gave me a reproachful look. Then he sniffed my pockets.

In my left pocket was the thread. The fourth one, the one that had straightened the paper clip. It turned out I'd absentmindedly coiled it up into a little ring and put it in my pocket the night before. Val had asked me to leave the samples with the notebook, but I'd taken this one: to remember it by. Shiny, thin, stronger than a paper clip.

Lugnut fished it out of my pocket with one swipe of his paw. The thread was shiny. That was all he needed to know.

"Give it back," I said. "That's a sample."

Lugnut looked at me. Thought it over. And went under the couch.

I lay down on top of the couch, still in my jacket. Underneath me, in his stash, the cat rustled. He was tucking away the thread I'd made by understanding how the market works. The main sample of Series One. Val was going to kill me. Or put it in the notes: "Sample number four lost. Cat."

I thought that, all in all, it was the right place for it. A thing should work, not sit around. But maybe some things are allowed to sit around for a while. At least until you figure out what they're for.

I closed my eyes and slept till evening.
