# Chapter 24. The Interview

Detective Keyes's office was on the third floor, at the end of a hallway where the linoleum had worn through to the concrete and a ficus stood on the windowsill that somebody probably watered once a quarter, whenever the inspectors came around.

They sat me on a chair across from the desk. The chair wobbled. The left back leg was just under a quarter inch short, and somebody had wedged an old report under it, folded in quarters. The report had gone flat, and the chair wobbled again.

I sat there thinking about the wrong things. I was thinking that the leg would take a minute to fix. Thin tubing, hardly any work hardening; it would have been glad to.

I sat on my hands. Literally.

I'd taken the rings off back in the car and stuffed them in my jacket pocket. The thimble I'd left on. Taking it off in front of people would have been as good as a confession.

Keyes came in twenty minutes later with a mug of coffee and a folder. He sat down. He took a sip. He winced. Apparently their coffee was no better than the meat-pie stand's.

"You're not under arrest," he said. "You're a witness in a criminal investigation. This interview will be on the record, and you're entitled to have an attorney present. Do you want one?"

"I do," I said. "I just don't know who."

"That's your right," said Keyes, the way people say "That's your problem." "While we wait, let's just talk. Off the record."

He opened the folder and laid an evidence bag on the desk, sealed, with Old Pete's and Katie's signatures on it. Inside was the pea.

"Tell me," he said. "Where it's from."

---

I told him. The part I could tell.

That I'd been fixing things at the market for twelve years. That in October I'd been in debt, forty-seven thousand, and I'd figured there was gold on old circuit boards. That everybody knows that: people write about it online, they show it on TV. That I'd gathered up old military-surplus connectors by the dumpsters and at the dump, and collected it. On my kettle.

"On your kettle," Keyes repeated.

"On my kettle."

"How?"

This was where it got hard.

I couldn't say "with the thimble." I couldn't say "reshaped." I could say what people write online: acids, solutions, precipitate. But I'd never done any of that. I'd trip over the second word, and he'd see it.

"I understand metal," I said. "Really well."

"You understand it."

"I fix everything. I can feel with my hands where the copper is, where the tin is, where the gold is. I… extracted it."

"Extracted it." He picked up a pen. Looked at it. It wasn't the same pen: that one was still somewhere with Lugnut. This one was blue, plastic, with a chewed end. "By what method?"

"Thermal."

"What temperature?"

"High."

He looked at me over the pen.

"Mr. Nagel. I've been a detective for seventeen years. The people who come through this office kill, rob, steal and lie. Everybody lies. But usually they're better at it. You lie like a man who's never lied before in his life. That worries me more than anything else."

I didn't say anything.

"All right," said Keyes. "Forget how you extracted it. What did you do with it? The report says you systematically manufacture items and sell them through the market. Bracelets. White gold, palladium."

"I never sold anything. Not one gram."

"And this?" He tapped the bag.

"That's been sitting in the case of hooks since October. I tried to sell it once. At a pawn shop. They wouldn't take it without papers. I left. It's been sitting there ever since."

"So you did try."

"I tried. I didn't sell."

"And the bracelet?"

"I fixed the bracelet. The clasp spring. For half a bag. Through the app. There's a receipt."

"Half a bag. How much is that?"

I told him. Keyes looked at me as if I'd fixed his car for a book of matches.

"On white gold, work like that goes for twenty times as much. I happen to know: my wife takes hers to a jeweler. Why did you charge half a bag?"

"Because it was half a bag's worth of work."

He put the pen down.

"You realize that's exactly what's suspicious?"

---

Then he read me a paragraph from the report. Slowly, without expression. I listened and recognized the handwriting. Smooth, precise, not one comma out of place. Like the bolt. Like the drop on the padlock.

"…Timothy Nagel systematically makes use of illegally obtained precious metals. The items are sold through a network of acquaintances and vendors at the Central Market. According to available information, Mr. Nagel possesses an unlicensed refining technology and reshapes items, altering their composition…"

"'Reshapes,'" said Keyes. "What does that mean?"

"I don't know."

"I don't know either. I asked our crime lab guy. He said there's no such term. You can melt it down. You can refine it. But 'reshape,' no." He paused. "Reshape, like remodeling? You need a permit for that."

"That's exactly why I don't reshape anything," I said.

He looked at me. The corner of his mouth twitched. Almost.

"But whoever wrote this used the word as if he knows it can be done," he said. "That worries me too."

I thought: why? For twenty years Glen had lived so that nobody would ever write about the thimble. And now he'd put a word into the report that led straight back to him. That wasn't a mistake. Glen doesn't make mistakes with words, any more than he makes them with steel. That word wasn't meant for Keyes. It was meant for me. Like the frost in Hank's garage: too much of it, on purpose, so that I'd know.

---

Keyes opened his desk drawer and took out a watch. A man's watch, inexpensive, on a steel band. One link had come apart; the pin had fallen out.

"Mine," he said. "It's been sitting there two weeks. The repair shop in our building closed. You're the fix-it man. Fix it."

I looked at the watch. At him. At the sheet of paper where he'd circled "reshapes."

"Is this a test?"

"It's a watch. I'm lost without it. I never know what time it is."

I thought about it for maybe five seconds. Refusing would look suspicious: a fix-it man who can't fix a watchband isn't a fix-it man. I didn't have pliers. But a link like that really can be squeezed shut with your fingers, if your fingers are strong. So I had to do it the way I would have by hand. Rough. Uneven. No better than by hand.

I took the band. The hole in the link had worn loose; the steel was cheap, soft, stamped. The thimble wanted to make it even, good as new. I didn't let it. I squeezed the link as if with pliers and left it a little crooked, with a visible dent on one side. I always keep a pin in my pocket, just in case. It went in. It held.

The heat came out of Keyes's mug. It was sitting closest. I hadn't asked; the power took it by itself, on the way, the way it always does when I'm scared. Fear makes the power run faster, but it costs three times as much. I paid the rest myself. My stomach went hollow, as if I hadn't eaten since morning. I hadn't eaten since morning.

Keyes put the watch on. Moved his wrist around. Looked at the crooked dent in the link.

"Without tools," he said.

"Strong fingers. Twelve years."

He took a sip of coffee. Winced harder than the first time.

"Coffee in this place goes cold like at some pie stand," he said, looking into the mug.

I didn't say anything. I took Mrs. Mazur's paper sack out of my pocket, took out a hand pie and ate it. Keyes watched me eat. Then he picked up the pen and wrote on a separate sheet, in big letters: "Reshapes?" And circled the question mark.

"All right. We'll wait for your attorney."

---

The attorney arrived forty minutes later.

Goldie came in without knocking, in her navy coat, with a folder, her ID already in her hand. Keyes looked up and stood. Almost automatically. Everybody stands up for Goldie.

"Cochran. Attorney. My bar card. My letter of representation. I represent Timothy Nagel." She laid the papers on the desk. "My client has been talking to you without counsel for an hour now. I trust none of it is on the record."

"None of it's on the record." Keyes looked at the last name on the card. Then at her. "That Cochran?"

"That Cochran. Just not the one you're thinking of."

Later, in the hallway, she explained. She'd passed the bar five years ago ("Dad said it would come in handy"). Then she'd put her license on inactive status when she became his director on paper. And in January, after that evening in my kitchen, she'd resigned as director and reactivated her license. Bull had signed the retainer agreement with her that morning, on my behalf, on the hood of his sedan. For zero.

"Like the receipt you wanted to ring up for Ms. Pratt," she said. "It came in handy. Just not for Dad."

Now she sat down next to me and for a second put her hand on my elbow. As if checking that I was still there.

"Don't say anything else," she said quietly. "Not even 'yes.' I'll do the talking."

And she began.

---

"An anonymous report by itself isn't grounds for opening an investigation," said Goldie. "So somebody followed up and wrote it up. Who checked, and what did they find?"

Keyes was quiet for a moment. Then, reluctantly:

"We interviewed a scrap buyer. In February."

"Did the scrap buyer confirm any sales?"

"The scrap buyer confirmed that somebody brought him gold."

"Brought it. Did he buy it?"

Keyes didn't answer.

"No transactions," said Goldie. "Not one sale, not one buyer, not one payment. And possession is only a crime in large quantities. Large means dozens of times more than six grams. Six grams of a man's own scrap, refined for his own use, isn't even a civil fine."

"Unlicensed refining is a civil fine," said Keyes. "Possibly."

"Possibly," Goldie agreed. "That's small change. And the bracelet your well-wisher writes about is my bracelet. Here it is."

She raised her arm. The bracelet, white gold with palladium, with its fine openwork clasp, was on her wrist.

"My client didn't make it and didn't sell it. He fixed the clasp spring. In October. Through his self-employed app. Here's the receipt. I keep all my receipts." She paused. "He told me back then the same thing he told you: 'That was half a bag's worth of work.' I didn't understand him then either."

"And how does the author of this report know it's white gold with palladium?"

"Now that's a good question," Goldie said slowly. "Two people know. My client, and an expert I showed the bracelet to. Through his security. At his house."

"Name?"

"Then I'll have to interview you as a witness," Keyes said, before she could answer.

"Then not today," said Goldie. "Today I'm an attorney. Tomorrow I'll come in myself. With a statement. Signed. I don't write anonymous letters."

I watched her from the side. She had just pointed a detective at the man who worked for her father. Her father was one of his three clients. Here, in this office, in front of a witness. Her fingers on the folder had gone white. Her voice hadn't.

Next to "Reshapes?" on the sheet, Keyes wrote: "Expert. Cochran: tomorrow."

---

The second to arrive was Ms. Pratt. With Old Pete, arm in arm, both of them in hats. Old Pete stayed out in the hallway.

"A witness to a search can't be a witness for the defense," he said. "That much I know. I read up on it."

"He can," said Ms. Pratt, without turning around. "But he isn't needed."

She came in with her folder.

"Pratt," she said. "Revenue Department. Auditor. I audited Mr. Nagel in October. I found a gray area. Twelve years of unreported income from repairs. Small income. For three years he paid what the law requires. And for the other nine, he came in on his own and paid those too. That was the first time I'd ever seen such a thing."

Keyes raised his eyebrows.

"The receipts," he said.

"I can't bring you our records," said Ms. Pratt. "Taxpayer confidentiality. Officially, only on your written request. You'll write me that request tomorrow." She laid a thick printout on the desk. "This, on the other hand, was printed out this morning by the neighbor's boy. From Mr. Nagel's app. With his consent. I checked it against our data. It matches. One thousand eight hundred forty-two receipts, October through March. I counted. Twice. Knife repair. Pruning shears repair. Eyeglasses repair. Repair, repair, repair. Metalwork taken in through Mr. Sarkisian is rung up separately, under Sarkisian; he has his own receipts. Not one receipt for the sale of any item. Not one."

She looked at him over her glasses. The ones that didn't fog up anymore.

"For twenty years I've been finding the places where people lie on paper. This man doesn't know how to lie on paper."

Keyes picked up a sheet. Read a line.

"'Eyeglasses, anti-fog. Ninety-four fifty.' Why such an odd amount?"

"Half a bag," said Ms. Pratt. "Those are my glasses. He offered to do it for free. I paid, by the book."

Keyes looked at her glasses. At his own watch, with the crooked dent in the link.

On the sheet next to "Reshapes?" another line appeared: "Glasses. Pratt."

---

The third was Bull. He didn't come in; he called Keyes's cell. Keyes looked at the number, winced, but answered. Listened. Winced harder.

"Bykowski," he said, putting the phone down. "George. I've interviewed him in two cases. As a witness. He says he's got twelve people down in the lobby. And every one of them wants to give a statement." He looked at Goldie.

"I'm formally requesting that you take statements from these witnesses," said Goldie. And I could see she hadn't known about the twelve people.

Keyes sighed. Looked at his mended watch. Stood up.

"Let's go. Let's have a look at your twelve."

---

The lobby was packed.

Bull stood by the front desk in his old windbreaker, holding a list. Behind him stood my people.

Greg the butcher, in his white apron over his jacket and without his knife: they'd taken the knife, sheath and all, at the door. Lottie, her eyes red. Roxanne with a pot of carnations. The same Roxanne who hadn't come to the glass-of-water test on Sunday because carnations mustn't get upset. She'd come to the police. With carnations. Next to her, Stefan the baker, who hadn't texted anything, and had come too.

Then the old newspaper man, in his glasses. Double-A. The pie man, in his stained smock. Mrs. Petrie, who'd been bringing Ashot her boots for fifteen years. Ashot, sour as an old boot, because they'd taken his hammer at the door. Mr. Abrams with his leather folder. And Val. She stood by the wall, in a blue sweater under her jacket, with her oilcloth notebook under her arm, and they wouldn't let her upstairs, because officially she wasn't anybody.

And one more. Broad, bald, with a gold tooth, in a sheepskin coat. He stood apart from everybody and acted as if he'd just happened to be passing by.

Lenny Kurgan.

I froze. Bull saw where I was looking and gave a tiny nod.

"Detective," said Bull. "Witnesses. The market. For years every one of these people has been bringing Timothy Nagel everything that breaks. Every one of them is ready to say it: he never sold anything. Only fixed things. Charged next to nothing. Here's the list, with signatures." He held out the sheet. "And this one here's going to tell you something else."

Keyes took the list. Looked at Lenny.

"Kurgan. Leonard. Scrap buyer. You and I talked in February."

"We talked," Lenny said reluctantly.

"So talk."

Lenny looked at me. There was something in his eyes I'd never seen there before. Something like shame, except Lenny didn't know how to be ashamed, so it came out crooked.

"Bull asked me," he said. "I owe Bull one." He paused. "This guy brought me a pea in October. Gold. I didn't take it. I don't take anything without papers. Rules."

That was a lie. He'd offered me fifteen bags. Keyes, I think, understood that. But he wrote it down the way it was said.

"And then?"

"He left. Broke my bolt on his way out, too."

"Broke it how?" asked Keyes, and glanced at his sheet, at the circled "reshapes."

Lenny was quiet. For a very long time. That bolt had been as thick as a wrist. It had gone soft as a noodle right in front of his eyes.

"Broke it," Lenny said at last. "It was rusty."

Keyes turned to me.

"Once, you said," he said. "To the pawn shop."

"Twice," I said. "The second time I wasn't selling. I was running away."

Somebody from the market snorted. Ashot said out loud, "That's true. That's how he always does business."

Keyes didn't laugh. But the corner of his mouth twitched again.

"Anything else?" he asked Lenny.

Lenny was quiet for a moment. Then he said, lower:

"In February some other guys came to see me too. Before you. Two of them. Asked about him. What he brought in, when, how much. And about bracelets. White gold with palladium, they asked if he'd ever brought any in. I said I never heard of such a thing. They paid well. For the conversation."

"Who?"

"Didn't introduce themselves. One big guy, black jacket, didn't say a word. The other one did the talking. Quiet, like. Polite."

White gold with palladium. The same words as in the report. Keyes wrote: "Two. February. Palladium." And drew an arrow connecting it to "Expert."

And I thought: not Glen. This winter Glen had left his house twice, and I knew where he'd gone both times. A big, silent man in a black jacket: Glen had two of those, his driver and his bodyguard. Which one, I didn't know. But I knew whose.

---

The interviews went on until evening.

Goldie had made her request, and Keyes couldn't turn it down. He took four statements on the record: Lenny, Greg, Ashot and Mr. Abrams. The rest wrote out short statements of their own. Greg said Tim had hardened his knife so it never goes dull, and charged him "about what a sharpening costs." Ashot said he'd spent twelve years across the aisle from Tim and that Tim "can't even buy a cheap no-name sole without getting cheated, that's the kind of businessman he is." Mr. Abrams said that Tim paid his rent, late but he paid it, and that "if he were selling gold, he'd pay on time." The old newspaper man wrote one word in his statement: "Oh." And signed it.

Keyes drank two more mugs of bad coffee. By the end he was sitting with his head propped on his hand, looking at the list of signatures the way Old Pete looks at a balance that doesn't come out right, but comes out wrong in a good direction.

At seven in the evening he called me back in.

"You remain a witness," he said. "I'm sending the metal to the lab for analysis. If it's confirmed to be scrap off circuit boards and there were no sales, it'll be returned or forfeited; the court will decide. Maybe there'll be a civil fine for the refining. Maybe not." He paused. "I'm not closing the case yet. Your pea doesn't interest me. What interests me is who wrote the report. Who this 'expert' is. And why he knows the word 'reshapes.' Will you tell me?"

"My client didn't see who wrote it," said Goldie. "And guesses aren't testimony."

"That's what I figured." No anger in it. Just tired. "Then I'll go see this 'expert' on my own. Without you."

He stood up and held out his hand. I shook it. His hand was warm, dry, ordinary. The watch on his wrist was ticking.

"Your fingers really are strong," he said.

"Twelve years."

"Right. Twelve years." He nodded at my wobbly chair. "Can you do anything about that?"

"With your permission," I said.

"Permission denied," said Keyes. "I'd hate to lose that report. It's been under there since ninety-seven."

I was already in the doorway when he said to my back:

"And ask your cat to give my pen back. It was a gift. From my wife."

The analysis, I thought, going down the stairs. It'll show four nines. The clerk at the pawn shop told me back in October: stuff like this only gets made at a refinery. Not on a kettle. It won't be today. But it'll come.

---

Outside it was dark. March, and the snow still lay in gray humps along the curbs.

Everybody was standing by the front steps. Greg, Lottie, Roxanne with her carnations, Ashot with his hammer back, Stefan, Double-A, the pie man, Mrs. Petrie, Mr. Abrams. Ms. Pratt and Old Pete, arm in arm. Goldie. Bull by his old blue sedan with the bald patches on its fender. Lenny wasn't there. Lenny had left right after giving his statement. Bull said, "He did what he owed. Don't expect any more from him than that. But don't expect any less, either."

Val stood under the streetlight. She was wiping her glasses on the end of her scarf, though there was nothing to wipe. Five hours she'd stood there, first in the lobby, then by the steps once they'd cleared the lobby.

"They let you go?" she asked.

"Witness."

"Is that better than suspect?"

"Goldie says a lot better."

I took the rings out of my pocket and put them on. All four. On the thimble hand. Val watched me put them on, one at a time.

Then she stepped up to me and pressed her forehead into my shoulder. I could feel her shaking. From the cold, probably. Five hours.

"I thought," she said into my jacket, "that if they locked you up, I'd be left with forty pages of excerpts, two tenths of a degree Celsius and somebody else's ring. And no you."

"The rings are right here. See."

"I know," said Val. "I don't mean the rings."

---

After that we stood by the sedan, and Bull handed out cold hand pies from Mrs. Mazur's sack. Nobody minded.

"George," I said. "Why'd you do it?"

Bull finished chewing.

"You pay me every Friday. Since November, not one missed. I never even have to call." He paused. "A collector's no good if he lets a good debtor go to jail. Who'd bring me my Fridays then?"

"And Lenny?"

"Lenny's owed me since my first year collecting. His mother was sick, so I didn't lean on him. Let it ride. He remembers." Bull grinned. "A debt's a debt. Sometimes it just takes a while to come back around."

Goldie was standing next to us.

"Timothy. The expert."

"I know."

"Keyes will find him. He's stubborn. I've seen the type." She paused. "And tomorrow I'll go see Keyes myself. With a statement. About the palladium." Another pause. "Dad will know by lunch."

"No regrets?"

"I signed something without reading it once," said Goldie. "There won't be a second time."

"And the notebooks?" asked Val.

"This won't bring them back," said Goldie.

I thought of Mrs. Stern. "Glen always returned books. Late. With notes in the margins."

Maybe.

---

At home Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill. No pen on him.

I found nothing under the couch. The police had looked under the couch themselves. Lugnut has more than one stash; I've known that since October. It took me half an hour to find the second one: in my father's old felt-lined boot, up on the shelf over the hall closet. Inside were a snap from my work shirt, a shiny jar lid, a scrap of the silk thread from the lab (sample number four), and Keyes's pen. Metal, engraved: "To Scott. Happy anniversary."

The ring from Grandma's spoon wasn't there. Not in this one either.

I took out the pen. Lugnut watched me from the windowsill like I was a man who'd broken into somebody else's apartment and was pawing through somebody else's things.

"This isn't yours," I said. "It was a gift from his wife."

Lugnut turned away. Offended.

I put the pen on the table. I'd take it back tomorrow.

Then I sat down in the kitchen and opened my notebook. The one with the hand pies and the balance, which Keyes had called "records of a household nature."

"Witness."

"Goldie: attorney, for zero. Ms. Pratt: one thousand eight hundred forty-two receipts. Bull: twelve people. And Lenny."

"'Reshapes': a word meant for me."

"Analysis: four nines. Later."

"Until the lease runs out: three weeks."

I closed the notebook. Looked at my hands. At the thimble. At the four rings, warm. Growing.

Twelve people had come to the police today to say: he only fixed things. For twelve years I'd thought I had nothing but the booth. And the booth turned out not to be the main thing.

Three weeks. The vacant lot behind the truck depot. A thousand joints. Eight students who'd stayed, and twelve people who'd come.

It was madness.

But for twelve years I'd been fixing madness for half a bag. It was time to build some over a weekend.
