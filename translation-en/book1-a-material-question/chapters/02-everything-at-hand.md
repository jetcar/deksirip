# Chapter 2. Everything at Hand

I live on the fourth floor of a five-story walk-up that has everything an old building is supposed to have: a peeling stairwell, mailboxes with the little doors pried off, and a smell that can't be described but that you could recognize with your eyes closed anywhere on earth. It's the smell of cats, cabbage and other people's memories.

My apartment is a one-bedroom, and I rent it. The landlady lives in another town and communicates with me exclusively through bank transfers. Once a month I send her money, and she sends back a smiley face. In three years, it's the most stable relationship of my life.

My door doesn't shut. Or rather, it shuts if you throw your shoulder into it, but then it won't open, and if you don't throw your shoulder into it, it stands ajar, as if it has opinions of its own. I wedge a chair under the knob. The chair wobbles too. It's not an apartment, it's a museum of decisions put off until later.

That evening I set the bag with Mrs. Stern's lamp on the windowsill, put three bags of pierogi in the freezer, wedged the chair under the door and sat down on the couch.

The thimble was in my shirt pocket. I could feel it through the fabric: warm, heavy, patient.

It was time to start. And that's when I realized I didn't know where.

---

There's a particular moment when you're holding something new and you don't know how it works. I know that moment well: people bring me gadgets I've never seen before and say, "It won't turn on." I've spent twelve years training for that moment. The main rule is: don't go poking at everything. First you look. Then you think about what could be inside. Then you test one thing at a time, and you always write down what you did, because three steps in you won't remember where you started, and the gadget remembers everything.

I got out a graph-paper notebook left over from some long-ago burst of resolve to track my spending (two pages filled in, the rest pure hope) and wrote across the top: "Experiments."

I thought about it and changed it to "Trials." It sounded more official.

Then I got out the kitchen scale. Digital, no-name, accurate to the gram. Somebody brought it in to be fixed once and never picked it up. That happens: a person brings in a thing and then decides a new life beats an old repair. Half my closet is things like that. I call it "the shelter."

The first question to test was a simple one: where does the material come from?

That afternoon I'd bent the wrench, and the wrench had stayed a wrench. It hadn't grown, and it hadn't shrunk. But I'd judged that by eye. And the eye is a bad instrument. It sees what it wants to see.

I put a teaspoon on the scale. An ordinary stainless steel one, with CAFETERIA stamped on the handle. Cafeteria spoons outlive people.

Twenty-four grams.

I put on the thimble.

The world took its step, and right away things got crowded: I could feel everything. The scale: plastic, thin wires, a little circuit board with beads of solder. The table: particleboard, sawdust pressed together with glue, nasty to the touch, like chewing paper. The spoon: dense, orderly, with a strict lattice in which iron, chromium and nickel were mixed together like raisins in a bun.

I took the spoon in my hand and thought that I wanted to make it flat. Not a spoon, a plate.

The steel gave. Slowly, stickily. I had to hold the thought the way you hold a heavy grocery bag: the moment I let up, the movement stopped. Half a minute later a flat steel plate with a handle was lying in my palm. Warm. Almost hot.

I put it on the scale.

Twenty-four grams.

I took off the thimble and wrote: "One: mass doesn't change. Shape changes. Spoon — plate — twenty-four grams."

Then I sat there and thought that, all things considered, this was obvious. But you test the obvious first. Half the broken appliances people bring me don't work because they aren't plugged in. The owners always swear they checked.

---

The second question was more interesting: can things be joined?

I took a second spoon. CAFETERIA again, twenty-three grams; somewhere in its long life it had lost a gram. I laid it on the plate and put on the thimble.

This turned out to be harder. Even while I held them in one hand, they were two things, and I could feel the boundary between them, thin but distinct, like a seam in fabric. The steel of one spoon didn't want to cross over into the steel of the other. Both lattices were perfectly good, but they faced in different directions, and at the boundary they didn't line up.

I thought this was like welding. When you weld metal, you melt it, and while the lattices are liquid they come to an agreement. And then they set as one.

So I had to melt them a little. Not all of it. Just at the boundary.

I focused on the seam and asked it to go soft.

The spoons hissed (for real, out loud) and a thin smoke rose from the seam: the grease they'd been hoarding since their cafeteria days was burning off. I was holding them by the tips of the handles, through a rag, but my hand still got very hot. I almost dropped the whole thing, but I hung on. And suddenly I felt the boundary disappear, felt the two lattices flow together, as if two crowds in a town square had stopped shoving and formed a single line.

I had one thing in my hand. Strange, crooked, like a spoon an elephant had sat on. But one.

The scale said forty-seven grams.

Twenty-four plus twenty-three. Not a gram more, not a gram less.

And that's when the hunger hit.

Not ordinary hunger, but the kind where it feels like somebody opened a window in your stomach and there's a draft. I got up, went to the kitchen and boiled the first bag of pierogi. I ate it standing up, straight out of the pot, burning my mouth, and I didn't go back to the notebook until there was nothing left in the pot but water.

I wrote: "Two: joining works. You have to soften the boundary. It's expensive. Welding: very expensive. One weld of two spoons: almost a bag of pierogi."

I thought about it and added: "Bending is cheap. Breaking and joining is expensive." Then I thought some more and underlined it twice, because this, it seemed, was the most important thing.

---

This is where I had to stop and think, so I did. Sitting on the couch with the pot in my lap, here's what I thought.

If bending is cheap and breaking is expensive, then I'm not all-powerful. I'm a very good blacksmith who doesn't need a hammer or a forge but who, like any blacksmith, gets tired. I can give a thing any shape I want, but I'll pay for every bond I have to break. Bending a wrench: three hand pies. Welding two spoons: a bag of pierogi. So something big, something that has to be cut apart and welded back together, would cost me…

I tried to estimate it and realized I didn't know how to count it. I didn't know how many bonds there were in a spoon. I didn't remember how much energy there was in a bag of pierogi. That was a number they print somewhere on the package in small type, and I never read small type, because experience has taught me that nothing good is ever written in small type.

I went to the freezer and read it. Two hundred twenty calories per hundred grams. Eight hundred grams to a bag. Seventeen hundred sixty calories. Nearly the whole daily requirement for a grown man who sits in a booth.

So one weld of two spoons was nearly my whole day. All the energy I'd have spent walking, thinking, breathing and arguing with Ashot had gone into forty-seven grams of crooked steel.

It was a terrible deal. It was such a terrible deal that I laughed. A new cafeteria spoon costs one bag of pierogi. I had just spent a bag of pierogi to turn two normal spoons into one abnormal one.

I wrote: "Three: not everything you can do is worth doing. Do the math!" and drew a circle around "Do the math."

---

After that I made rounds through the apartment, like a doctor on a ward.

The doorknob. It was loose because the spindle inside had worn down: a thin steel rod that, after twenty years of turning, had been ground down to the thickness of a toothpick. The normal repair: buy a new knob, two bags. I put on the thimble, took hold of the knob and felt it from the inside: the brass housing, rough, pitted; the steel spindle, skinny and tired; a spring coated in rust.

I didn't need to break anything. I needed the spindle to get thicker. And for that I needed metal the spindle didn't have.

I thought: what about the brass? There's plenty of it in the housing, more than it needs. I could take a little from the inside of the housing, from somewhere nobody would ever see, and add it to the spindle.

I tried. The brass didn't want to become steel. It moved over to the spindle and wrapped around it like a bandage, but it stayed brass: soft, yellow, different. The two lattices didn't match at all. They wouldn't even look at each other.

But the knob stopped wobbling.

I turned it. It worked. A brass sleeve around a steel spindle is, if you think about it, just a plain bearing. Good doorknobs come with one on purpose. Without meaning to, I'd made the knob better than it was when it left the factory.

The hunger was small. About a sandwich's worth. I wrote:

"Four: different metals don't really mix. But you can lay one over another.

Five: moving metal from one spot to another is cheap. As long as you don't break anything.

Six: doorknob: two bags saved. Cost: one sandwich."

And right there, somewhere around eleven at night, a number appeared in my head for the first time, a number that would go on to change my life many times over. The number was called "the difference." What it costs to buy something, versus what it costs me to make it myself. Two bags versus a sandwich. Almost two bags of difference.

With the spoons the difference had been negative: I'd lost. With the doorknob it was positive: I'd come out ahead. So the question wasn't whether I could do something or not. The question was what it cost me.

---

The bathroom faucet had been dripping for going on three years. One drop every four seconds, louder at night than during the day. That's a well-known law of physics that nobody has ever discovered, because all physicists sleep in apartments with working faucets.

I went into the bathroom, put on the thimble and laid my hand on the faucet.

And then something happened that I hadn't expected.

I didn't feel just the faucet. I felt the pipe that ran from it into the wall. And beyond that, the riser that ran through the whole building, from the first floor to the fifth. The metal was continuous, all connected, and my sense of it ran along it like water down a gutter: down to the basement and up to the attic. I could feel the threads at the joints, the rust on the seams, the thick layers of scale inside, like cholesterol in old arteries, and somewhere on the second floor, a thin spot where corrosion had nearly eaten through the steel. In a year, maybe two, it would start leaking there.

I stood in the bathroom with my hand on the faucet and felt the whole building.

It was so strange that I forgot what I'd come in for. Then I remembered. The faucet. The drip.

It was dripping because of the seat: the brass ring the washer presses down on. The seat had a chip in it, tiny, like a scratch from a fingernail. That's where the water was seeping through. I focused on the chip and asked the brass to close it up.

The brass obeyed. It obeyed much too well. I'd meant to fill the chip, but what was in my head, apparently, was "make it stop leaking," and the brass took that literally. It rose up, closed over the opening in the seat and sealed it shut, as if there had never been an opening there at all.

The dripping stopped. So did everything else. I turned the faucet on: nothing. Not a drop. The pipe was humming with pressure, and the water had nowhere to go.

I stood there looking at the dry faucet.

It was a very educational moment. It taught me more than the whole notebook of trials. The power doesn't do what you do. It does what you mean. And if what you mean is fuzzy ("make it stop leaking"), it'll carry that out fuzzily. However it understands it.

It's like customers. A guy says, "Make it stop making noise," and you could just snip the speaker wire. It'll stop making noise. But that's not what he meant.

It took me twenty minutes and another half a bag of pierogi to open the seat back up: carefully, all the way around, exactly as much as needed, holding in my head not "make it stop leaking" but a very specific picture: "a ring, an opening this size, an edge like this, smooth." The water ran. The dripping stopped.

I wrote: "Seven: think precisely. The power does what you mean, not what you want."

And underneath, in small letters: "Eight: metal conducts. Through the faucet, the whole riser. Second floor, thin spot. Tell somebody? Who?"

---

Around one in the morning I moved on to wood.

That was a mistake, but an educational one.

The chair I wedged against the door wobbled because of a leg: it had split along the grain and come apart. I put on the thimble and took hold of the leg.

Wood felt nothing like metal. Metal was orderly, comprehensible, like a brick wall. Wood was alive. Well, dead, of course, but put together the way only something that was once alive is put together. Long tubes bundled together. Inside the tubes, something like dry mush. Walls made of fibers, twisted like ropes and glued together with something I didn't understand at all. And all of it different, every inch its own thing.

I tried asking the crack to close, the way I had with the wrench.

The wood didn't obey. Or rather, it obeyed in its own way. The crack closed, but the leg around it went dark, dry and brittle, and when I flexed it, it snapped like a cracker. I looked at the broken piece. Inside it was black. Not burned, just black, like charcoal. The room smelled like a campfire, the window fogged up, and the leg had gotten noticeably lighter: everything in the wood that wasn't charcoal had gone off as steam and smoke.

It took me a few minutes to understand what had happened. I didn't know how wood was put together. I just told it, "Close." And it closed the only way I'd offered it, without realizing I was offering anything: by breaking everything complicated in it down into something simple. Charcoal is exactly that. Wood with everything gone but the simplest part.

Metal I understood. Not from a textbook, but with my hands. For twelve years I'd sawed it, soldered it, bent it, ground it, and I knew how it behaved. Wood I'd only ever sawed. And badly.

I wrote: "Nine: only works on what you understand. Metal: yes. Wood: no (charcoal!). Study?"

I put a question mark after "Study," but it was a rhetorical question. I remembered spending three years of materials science in the back row, doing other people's term papers for cash and thinking the whole thing was a waste of time. Somewhere out there, my crystallography professor was laughing right now. Professor Arkin, a small man with enormous glasses, who used to say, "Nagel, without structure there are no properties. You want to fix things without understanding what you're fixing? That's not repair. That's a rain dance."

Professor Arkin had been right. It was a very unpleasant thing to find out at one in the morning.

---

By two in the morning it had gotten cold.

At first I didn't notice. Old buildings are always cold at night, and I'd been sitting still at the table by the window, my knee pressed against the radiator, concentrating on yet another spoon. I was trying to make it as thin as a razor, to find out how precise I could be. It came out pretty well. The razor-spoon cut paper with a quiet hiss.

Then I noticed I could see my breath.

I got up, went over to the radiator and put my hand on it. The radiator was ice-cold.

Not warm, not cool, but ice-cold, like metal outdoors in winter. Beads of condensation stood out on it. I looked at the window: the inside of the glass was covered with a thin white pattern of frost. Something was pulling heat out of everything like a pump with no shutoff valve: out of the radiator, out of the air, out of the glass itself.

And that's when it dawned on me that I wasn't hungry.

I'd made a dozen thin spoons and a razor, I'd had the thimble on all evening, and for the last half hour I hadn't been hungry at all. I'd been cold. The cold was coming from the radiator, and the radiator, it seemed, was cold because of me. Without noticing, I'd been taking heat not from myself but from it. From the metal right under my knee. From the hot water running up the heating riser through the whole building. And from the look of it, I'd been taking not as much as I needed but as much as was on offer. A dozen spoons don't cost that much. The thimble pulled like a pump with no shutoff valve, and everything that hadn't gone into the spoons had gone somewhere. Not into the spoons, and not into me; I'd have been cooked. In the margin I wrote: "Where to?"

Somebody knocked on the door.

I froze with the razor-spoon in my hand. Two in the morning. Chair against the door. Ice-cold radiator. Thimble on my finger.

The knock came again: short, demanding, the kind of knock that only comes from older women who are sure they're in the right.

I took off the thimble, shoved it in my pocket and moved the chair. The door, as always, swung halfway open by itself.

On the landing stood Mrs. Mazur from the fifth floor. In a bathrobe, with a wool shawl around her shoulders and her old felt-lined boots on her feet. She was holding a pot covered with a dish towel.

"Timothy," she said. "Is your radiator warm?"

"No," I said truthfully.

"Mine neither. Nothing. The whole riser, top to bottom. I called the emergency line, and they say everything's fine at the boiler, there's pressure. How can there be pressure and no heat? They're lying, like always. I thought maybe you'd know. You fix things."

I looked at her and tried to figure out what to say. I couldn't tell her the truth. Lying didn't feel right either: she was standing on a cold landing in her felt boots at two in the morning, hoping I'd know what was wrong. And I really did know.

"Probably air in the system," I said. "An air lock. I'll bleed it, and it'll get going."

That was a half-truth. There was no air in the system, but I knew that if I stopped pulling heat out of it, the hot water from the boiler would warm the riser back up in half an hour. All I had to do was nothing. Which, by the way, is also a very valuable skill, and one I hadn't developed much back then.

"Oh," said Mrs. Mazur, relieved. "I just knew you'd know. I brought you some hand pies. Cabbage. I can't sleep anyway."

She held out the pot. I took it.

"Mrs. Mazur," I said. "Why aren't you asleep?"

"How am I supposed to sleep when it's cold?" she said reasonably. "And my iron's broken, too. I'll bring it by tomorrow."

"Bring it," I said. "I'll fix it for free."

"Not for free," Mrs. Mazur said sternly. "Nobody values what's free. I'll bake you some hand pies."

And she went back upstairs, her boots shuffling on the steps.

I shut the door. Wedged it with the chair that was now missing a leg and stood on three. That was fine; it held. I opened the pot. Inside were twelve hand pies, golden brown, still warm. She must have baked them that evening and then brought me the most valuable thing she had.

I ate four of them standing by the ice-cold radiator. The radiator was slowly warming up. Somewhere above me, on the fifth floor, Mrs. Mazur was probably feeling her own radiator right now and thinking that Timothy from 42 was a good boy who knew everything.

I felt ashamed and amused at the same time. Amused because I'd just discovered a free source of energy, and it turned out to be Mrs. Mazur's radiator. Ashamed for the same reason.

I wrote: "Ten: you can take heat from metal nearby. From the radiator. Then no hunger. BUT! The radiator isn't mine. Mrs. Mazur is cold. It takes extra. Keep an eye on it."

I thought about it and underlined "isn't mine."

That was a very important thought, and it took me a long time afterward to think it all the way through. The heat in the radiator isn't mine. It belongs to the boiler that made it, to the building that pays for it, and to Mrs. Mazur, who's cold without it. If I take it, it doesn't vanish. It just ends up with me instead of with her. It's like taking hand pies out of somebody else's pot, except the hand pies are invisible and nobody knows who ate them.

Well, except me.

---

By four in the morning I was sitting in the kitchen, taking stock.

Lugnut the cat (I have one, I forgot to mention, though strictly speaking the cat doesn't belong to me, he belongs to himself) was sitting on the windowsill next to Mrs. Stern's lamp, watching me with yellow eyes. Lugnut is gray and scruffy, with one torn ear, and he comes and goes through the transom window. I never adopted him. He just took hold, the way mold does, or a habit. I named him Lugnut because he wedges himself in anywhere (the closet, a bag, under the couch), and once he's in there, you can't get him out without a breaker bar and a lot of swearing.

Toward morning I did one last thing. I had one silver spoon, my grandmother's. The only one left from the set; Mom sold the rest during the hard years and kept this one "to remember her by." Silver felt different from everything else: soft, warm, like butter. It wanted to change. I thought I could make a ring out of this spoon for my mom. She always said she had nothing of her mother's except a spoon, and you can't wear a spoon.

I made a ring out of the handle: thin, simple, a smooth band. The rest of the spoon I rolled into a little silver bar. Let it sit there; silver doesn't go to waste. It took hardly anything out of me: silver is soft, and it barely resisted. I set the ring on the table and went to put the kettle on.

When I came back, the ring was gone. The little bar was right where I'd left it. Apparently, in Lugnut's opinion, it wasn't pretty enough.

Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill, looking out the window and pretending he had nothing to do with it. He was very convincing. Only guilty cats and honest politicians look like that.

"Give it back," I said.

Lugnut yawned and jumped out the transom.

I watched him go and realized this was a rule too; it just needed writing down. I wrote: "Eleven: put away anything shiny. Lugnut."

---

Then I sat and looked at the notebook. There were eleven items. I copied them onto a clean page, short, so I could see them all at once:

One: you can't create mass, only move it around. Two: bending is cheap, breaking and joining is expensive. Three: count what it costs to buy versus what it costs to make. Four: different materials don't mix, but you can lay one over another. Five: through metal I can feel a long way. Six: think precisely. Seven: only what I understand. Eight: you can take heat from nearby, but it belongs to somebody. Nine: the nut.

Now there were nine items, because some of them had merged, like those two spoons.

And here's what I thought about. At the very beginning, that afternoon, I'd had one thought in my head: "I've got the most valuable thing in the world in my pocket." And a second thought had grown straight out of it: "So I can make a lot of money." It was like a formula out of a textbook: valuable thing, therefore money.

But now, after this night, I could see the formula was wrong. What makes a thing valuable isn't what it can do. It's what I can do with it, once you count everything it costs. If every weld is a bag of pierogi, then welding together a new car is something I'll never be able to afford. If I don't understand wood, I won't be making any furniture. If I'm taking heat from Mrs. Mazur, then it isn't free. It's just on somebody else's tab.

On the other hand, the doorknob: almost two bags of difference. The faucet: two bags of difference. A plumber would have charged at least three, and I'd spent half a bag of pierogi, plus however much it cost to wreck the thing first. The wrench: a bag and a half of difference. And the thin spot on the second floor of the riser, if I shored it up now, might be a flood across three floors that never happened. How much that came to in pierogi, I couldn't even imagine.

In other words: not earning. Not spending.

I opened the notebook again, and on the last page, where people usually write phone numbers and passwords, I made a list of everything in my apartment that was broken. It came to thirty-two items. Next to each one I wrote a number: how many bags it would cost to buy a new one or call somebody in. The washing machine that won't spin out. The fridge that revs for takeoff but never goes anywhere. The zipper on my jacket. Winter boots that let in water. Knives that won't cut. A frying pan with the handle broken off. The door. The chair. The faucet, already crossed off.

I added up the column.

Ninety-one bags.

More than a third of the debt. Not money I was going to get, but money I'd have had to spend someday and now, maybe, wouldn't.

I sat over that number while it got light outside, and somewhere on a garage roof, probably, Lugnut was sitting and admiring my grandmother's ring.

Ninety-one bags isn't fifteen thousand by Friday. Bull won't take a repaired doorknob from me. A payday loan doesn't accept pierogi. Debt is the part of life no thimble can reshape, because it isn't made of metal. It's made of agreements between people, and to change it, you need other people.

But if I spend less, I'll have more left over. And if I have more left over, then…

That's where I fell asleep. Right there at the table, cheek on the notebook, thimble in my pocket.

The last thing I remember is a thought, very clear and very simple, as if somebody had said it out loud:

"In the morning, eat all the hand pies. Then go to the market. It's full of broken things."
