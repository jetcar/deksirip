# Chapter 19. The Lockpick

On January second, at nine in the morning, somebody was shouting out in the yard.

It wasn't the shouting that woke me. It was Lugnut. He was sitting on my chest, looking me in the face the way a cat looks at you when his bowl is empty and something more interesting than the bowl is going on outside the window. Then I heard the shouting. A man's voice, hoarse, with the kind of details they don't print in books.

I looked out the window. Down below, by the garages behind our building, a little crowd had gathered. In the middle stood a short, broad man in a striped undershirt, a sheepskin coat thrown open over it. He was shouting and pointing at an open garage. The garage was sheet metal, painted green once upon a time. One of its doors hung wide open. Inside sat an old boxy compact, and around it was the particular kind of empty you get in a place where things usually are.

It was Hank, the garage guy from the next building over. I knew him the way you know a man whose radio you fixed ten years ago. Hank lived in that garage more than he lived at home. He had a workbench in there, a vise, a welder, two cases of tools and, rumor had it, a couch.

Now there was nothing left but the car and the couch.

I got dressed and went down.

On New Year's Eve, Val and I had stood on Mrs. Mazur's balcony with slices of napoleon and watched the fireworks. The whole yard was full of light and smoke. I remember thinking it was the best New Year's of my life. I didn't know that somewhere down below, by the garages, at that very minute, Mikey was holding somebody else's padlock in his palm.

---

"In the night!" Hank was shouting. "I hung it on the thirty-first! All day yesterday it was hanging there fine, I walked right past it! Not one scratch! And today it comes open in your hand! Like it was never locked!"

He held up the padlock. Heavy, old, with a thick shackle. The body wasn't damaged. Hank turned the key, snapped the shackle shut and let go.

The shackle slid back out. On its own. With a soft, tired click, as if it couldn't be bothered to hold on.

"See that?!" said Hank. "It won't hold! It was hanging there like new, and it won't hold!"

People shook their heads. Somebody said, "It's the cold." Somebody said, "Cheap no-name junk." Old Pete, in his hat, with his ledger under his arm, said, "They do it on purpose. So everybody buys new ones. Six months from now, you'll see: double the price in the stores."

I took the padlock from Hank so that the finger with the thimble ended up inside my fist. As if I were just looking it over. I didn't change anything; I only felt. That's almost free, but my mouth still went sweet and hungry.

Inside, I could feel, there was a small steel spring. Once it had been hardened: springy, mean, the way a spring is supposed to be. Now it was soft and calm, without any tension at all. It wasn't holding. It was lying there.

Cold doesn't break things like that. That happens when somebody asks the steel to rest.

My stomach went cold, and the thimble had nothing to do with it.

"Did you call the police?" I asked.

"I called!" said Hank. "The officer said he'd be here by lunch!"

I gave him back the padlock and looked up. At the third floor of our building. Right below my window was Mikey's. The curtain in it twitched and went still.

---

Mikey didn't open right away. First I knocked. Then I said through the door, "Mikey, it's me. Just me." Then the door opened on the chain, and Mikey's eye showed in the crack. Red, as if he hadn't slept.

"Mom's at work," he said. "Till tonight."

"I'm not here for your mom."

He took off the chain.

We sat in the kitchen. Mikey didn't say anything for a long time, just turned an empty mug around in his hands. Then he told me.

After the paper clip at Mrs. Mazur's, he couldn't sleep. He lay there thinking: if steel wants to rest, and I help it, what else does it want? In the morning he started trying. Everything in the apartment that was steel and under strain. The spring in a ballpoint pen. The latch on a cabinet door. His own bike lock.

"Springs are the easiest," said Mikey. "They're all mean. They all want to let go. I just hold it in my hand and ask. At first it took a minute. Then half a minute. Then as long as it takes me to count to ten. My fingers freeze, and I get so hungry that in two days I ate everything Mom made for the holidays. She figured I was having a growth spurt."

"And you didn't tell anybody."

"Just Dylan," said Mikey. "From the next building. We're in the same class. He said I was lying. On New Year's Eve, when everybody was setting off fireworks, he said: bet you can't do one on a garage. The garage ones are serious. And he pointed at Hank's."

"And you could."

"I held it for a minute," Mikey said quietly. "My fingers wouldn't bend for half an hour after. White spots on the tips; Mom thought I got frostbite at the fireworks. The shackle came out. We looked inside. There's a couch in there. We didn't touch anything. Honest, Tim. We looked, and that's it. I pushed the shackle back in and it seemed to catch. It hung there like it was locked. I thought it'd go back to the way it was."

A minute. Without a thimble, through the body of the lock. I hadn't known that was possible. It's a small spring, and letting it go is downhill, and he'd been practicing for five days. But still. A minute.

"It won't go back to the way it was," I said. "Soft steel doesn't harden itself again. It has to be hardened all over again. Did you know that?"

Mikey shook his head.

"I thought it was like… like a rubber band. You let go and it snaps back."

"No," I said. "It's like bread. Once it's gone stale, it doesn't turn fresh again on its own."

He looked at me, and I could see it sinking in. Slowly. Like the column in Val's thermometer: creeping, creeping, and then suddenly a number.

"So somebody came later," he said. "Pulled on it, and it was open. And took everything. The tools. The welder." His voice wobbled. "Because of me."

"Because of you," I said. "And because of whoever took it."

And because of me, I thought. But I didn't say that out loud. Not yet.

And I thought something else, too: two people had known Hank's garage was open. Mikey and Dylan. Mikey hadn't told anybody. Who had Dylan told?

---

The officer showed up at two. Young, round-faced, his knit cap pushed to the back of his head. His name was Sommers. Not Kevin: his kid brother, judging by the ears. Kevin's crystal back in school had been the size of a fist, because he added something secret to the solution. This one, I suspected, had a secret ingredient of his own. He looked over the doors, the padlock, the car, the couch, typed it all into his tablet and said the sentence that turned Hank purple:

"No signs of forced entry."

"What do you mean, no?!" said Hank. "Right here! The padlock won't hold!"

"The padlock won't hold," Sommers agreed. "But nobody broke it. Not a scratch. Not a saw mark. Either somebody opened it with a key or it opened by itself. Who has a key?"

"I do!"

"Just you?"

Hank went quiet. It was sinking in for him the way it had for Mikey, slowly.

"What are you getting at?"

"I'm not getting at anything," said Sommers. "I'm writing it down. No signs of forced entry, one key, in the owner's possession. The tools were expensive, you said? Insured?"

Hank turned purple again, though it hadn't seemed possible to get any more purple.

I stood off to the side, and I knew what was coming. Sommers would leave and write "Circumstances unclear." And Hank would be left with an empty garage and the suspicion that he'd robbed himself. And the whole yard would talk about it. Until spring.

Mikey came out of the building.

I hadn't called him. He came out on his own. In a hoodie, no hat, his ears red. He walked up to the officer and said, looking not at him but at Hank's car:

"I opened it. On New Year's Eve. On a bet. I didn't take anything. But I opened it. And it's because of me the padlock won't hold."

It got so quiet by the garages that you could hear a crow scratching around on the roof.

"Opened it with what?" asked Sommers.

Mikey looked at me. I understood that he didn't know what to say. That "I asked the steel to rest" isn't an answer you can type into a tablet.

"With my hands," said Mikey.

Sommers looked at his hands. At me. At Hank. At the padlock without a single scratch on it.

"With your hands," he repeated, and typed it in. I could see that he typed exactly that: "with my hands." "Did you take anything?"

"No."

"Who was with you?"

Mikey was quiet for a moment.

"One other person. He didn't take anything either."

"We'll check on that," said Sommers. Then he turned to Hank. "Sir. I've taken your report of the theft. If we find anything, I'll let you know. As for the minor…" He looked at Mikey. "As for the minor, you'll have to work that out with his parents. Damages go through them."

And he left. I never did figure out whether he believed it or not. I don't think he'd figured it out himself.

But I figured out something else. Today I wasn't the one who'd lit up. Today it was Mikey. And on Sommers's tablet it was written down in three words: "with my hands."

---

That evening there was a meeting in the stairwell.

It was Old Pete's idea. He said that since "an emergency incident has occurred involving a member of the collective," there had to be minutes. He wrote out a notice by hand and posted it on the first floor by the mailboxes, with a copy on his own door, under DON'T RING. I KNOW. "GENERAL MEETING. AGENDA: RESPONSIBILITY. ATTENDANCE MANDATORY."

Everybody came. Mrs. Mazur with hand pies. Judy the nurse from the third floor, old Shirley with her knitting, Katie from 40 with thread between her teeth, because she was in the middle of hemming somebody's pants. Val: I'd called her, and she'd come all the way across town, a scarf on over the sweater she wore around the house. Mikey's mom, Lana, came. Straight from her shift at the bread plant, in a jacket that smelled of warm bread. With the face of somebody who'd found out everything about her son all at once, today. And Mikey. He sat on a step between the second and third floors, his arms around his knees, looking at the floor.

Hank came too. Nobody had invited him, but Old Pete said the injured party had the right.

The meeting was held on the second-floor landing, because not everybody would have fit into Old Pete's apartment. Old Pete sat on a stool with the ledger on his knees. Lugnut sat on the windowsill, watching Old Pete's pen.

"So," said Old Pete. "Agenda: responsibility. Who is to blame? That's the first question. The second: what is to be done?"

"Those have been asked before," said Val. "By the classics."

"The classics didn't keep the books," said Old Pete. "Timothy, you have the floor."

I stood up. It was a dumb thing to do, standing up on a staircase. But I stood up.

I told them. Not everything. Not about the thimble: Hank was an outsider, and Old Pete still believed it was "imported technology." I said I'd taught Mikey a thing or two at Mrs. Mazur's. That Mikey had turned out to have a knack for it. That he'd done something he didn't fully understand. And that I'd known he had a knack for it, and hadn't told him where the edge was.

"That Saturday I saw him sitting in the corner, going pale over a paper clip," I said. "And I thought: what if he'd picked something other than a paper clip? I thought it. And I didn't do anything. I didn't give him a single rule. Because I don't know the rules myself."

Lana was looking at me. I couldn't tell whether she was angry or not.

"So who's to blame?" asked old Shirley, without looking up from her knitting. "The boy or you?"

"Both," I said. "And whoever took the stuff."

---

That's when it started.

Hank spoke first.

"I don't care who taught who what," he said. "Somebody walked out of my garage with a welder, two cases of tools and a vise. Twenty years I spent putting that together. I want to know one thing: who's going to give it back to me."

"Fair enough," said Old Pete, and wrote it down.

"And another thing," said Hank. "If your boy opens padlocks with his hands, what's he going to open a year from now? Apartments? Cars? The register at the store? You can't teach him. He needs to be…" He didn't finish. He looked at Lana and stopped.

"You can't teach him," Old Pete repeated thoughtfully. "That's a thought. If nobody's taught, nobody opens anything."

"People cut with knives too," said Mrs. Mazur. "So what, nobody sharpens knives anymore? Timothy sharpened Greg's knife, and Greg cuts meat with it. He could cut something besides meat. So what, nobody sharpens Greg's knife?"

"A knife is a knife," said Hank. "You can see a knife. This is with his hands. No marks."

"And who decides who gets taught?" asked Katie, taking the thread out of her mouth. "You, Mr. Palmer? You'll decide? Or Timothy?"

Old Pete opened his mouth and closed it again.

"If we teach in secret," said Val, "then it'll be like…" She stumbled. "Like a certain man I've read about. He can do more than anybody, and he doesn't let anybody near him. If we decide behind closed doors who gets taught and who doesn't, we'll turn into the same thing. Only there'll be eight of us."

"And if you teach in the open," said Hank, "there'll be more thieves."

"And more watchmen?" said Judy the nurse. It was the first thing she'd said all evening. Everybody looked at her. "If everybody can do it, then somebody will make a padlock you can't sweet-talk with your hands. And somebody will learn to lock up your garage, Hank, in a way nobody knows how to now. I've been at the clinic twenty-five years. If we kept how to give a shot a secret, people would give themselves shots anyway. Just worse."

Hank was quiet. Then he said, more quietly now:

"That doesn't get me my tools back."

"No, it doesn't," I said. "That's true."

---

We argued for two hours. The hand pies ran out after forty minutes. Old Shirley finished one sock and started on the second. At some point Lugnut climbed down off the windowsill, strolled past Old Pete and walked off with his pen. Old Pete went on writing in pencil without noticing.

I don't remember everything people said. I remember that at some point I stopped listening to the words and started listening to what they were really about. Hank was afraid of being robbed again. Lana was afraid for her son. Old Pete was afraid the books wouldn't be able to keep up. Val was afraid of turning into Glen. Mrs. Mazur wasn't afraid of anything; she was seventy-one, and she wanted everybody to eat. And I was afraid of what I'd thought that Saturday over the paper clip, and of what Glen had told me: "Whoever glows always ends up drawing the people who are looking for light."

But the conclusion Glen drew from that was: so you have to put out the light. And the people on the stairs, it seemed, were drawing a different one. So you need lots of people glowing, and everybody knowing how not to get burned.

Toward the end, Old Pete stood up. It was a solemn moment; he almost never stands up.

"I propose we write down rules," he said. "Short ones. So everybody understands and nobody can twist them. I kept the books for thirty-one years. The worst kind of rule is a long one."

He wrote them down. He read them aloud, slowly, point by point. We voted by show of hands. Hank raised his too.

"One. We teach in the open. Whoever wants to learn, we teach. No secret lists."

Everybody raised their hands. Hank, after a pause.

"Two. Whoever does the harm answers for it."

Everybody raised their hands.

"Three. So does the one who taught him, if he knew the student could do it and didn't tell him where the edge is."

Everybody raised their hands. I raised mine first.

"Four," said Old Pete, and stopped.

"What's four?" asked Val.

"There is no four," said Old Pete. "Three is enough. I kept the books for thirty-one years. The worst rule is rule four. Nobody ever remembers it."

He thought for a moment and added at the bottom of the minutes: "Instrument: lockpick. One. A minor." He underlined it.

---

Then came the most important part.

Mikey got up off his step. He walked over to Hank. He stood in front of him, still without a hat, and said:

"I'm sorry. I opened your garage. I didn't take anything, but I opened it. If I hadn't opened it, you'd still have everything."

Hank looked at him for a long time.

"Words," he said. "Words won't get me my welder back."

"I know," said Mikey. "I'll work it off. I'll fix your padlock. And the doors. Your left hinge is rusted, it's going to fall off soon. I saw it. Tim will help me. But I'll do it myself. It has to be me."

Hank looked at me.

"It's true," I said. You could see the hinge without any thimble: the rust was eating it from the edge in. "It'll come off within a month."

Hank was quiet. Then he waved his hand: not "get lost," but something else, halfway between "fine" and "we'll see."

"And the tools?" he asked.

I thought about it. You can't make a welder out of nothing. A vise you can, out of scrap metal, but it wouldn't be the same vise, not the one from twenty years of collecting. Tools you can buy. New. For money. The outside world only takes money. I'd written that down once already.

I ran the numbers. The welder: forty bags or so, if we buy used. Two cases of tools: the same again. My half: a week of good days. Mikey's: a lot of Saturdays.

"The tools, in money," I said. "Split down the middle. Half from me, under rule three. Half from Mikey; he'll work it off. And I'll make you a vise myself, if you don't turn your nose up at it, out of scrap from the junkyard. It'll be better than your old one."

Lana flinched.

"We don't have the money," she said quietly. "Timothy, we really don't."

"Mikey will," I said. "He'll spend his Saturdays with us at the market. Writing up orders. That's paid work. And Mikey gives the money to Hank. It's not a gift. It's a job."

Lana looked at her son. Mikey was looking at his mother.

"I agree," said Mikey.

"You already agreed," said Lana. "On New Year's Eve. You just didn't know it yet."

---

After the meeting, Lana caught up with me on the stairs, between the second and third floors.

"Timothy," she said. "Can I ask you something?"

"Sure."

"Did you ask me? Before you started teaching him?"

I stopped.

"No," I said.

She nodded. Not angrily. Tiredly.

"I work nights," she said. "Twelve-hour shifts at the bread plant, two on, two off. He's on his own. So he sits in front of his monitors. I thought: well, fine, at least he's home. And it turns out he's been going to see you. Bending paper clips." She paused. "I'm not against it. Honestly. You're better for him than those monitors. But next time, ask. It matters to me."

I'd heard that before. Val, the night of the buttons: "Ask. It matters to me." My rule: don't touch without permission. I'd thought it was only about bodies. It turned out it was also about other people's kids.

"I'll ask," I said. "I promise."

Old Pete had said there was no rule four. I wrote one down for myself, that same night: "Minors: only with their parents' permission."

---

I called Ashot that same evening. His booth was closed until the fifth, but I knew Ashot was sitting at home missing his hammer.

"Ashot," I said. "I need a helper on Saturdays. To write up orders. Fifteen years old, handwriting like a printer. I'll pay him myself, out of my share."

"Who?" asked Ashot.

"Mikey. From my building."

"The one who opened Hank's garage with his hands?"

I didn't say anything. The whole market already knew, it turned out. The market always knows everything before the police do.

"I'm not asking," said Ashot. "Let him come. Half a bag a Saturday, and a meat pie. The meat pie is from me. Sorting heel tips I won't let him do: I have my own system there, he wouldn't understand it. But writing, fine. And one more thing, Tim." He paused. "Tell him not to touch my padlock. I've been hanging it up for twenty years. I'm used to it."

"I'll tell him," I said.

"And tell him," Ashot added, "if he wants to touch something with his hands, let him touch leather. Leather was alive once. You have to treat it right. Maybe he'll learn something."

I didn't tell him that leather isn't alive, it's what's left of something alive. Ashot, I think, knew that himself. He'd just been treating it right for thirty years.

---

We made Hank's padlock on Sunday.

That is, Mikey made it. I sat next to him and watched. I told him, "You let the spring go. Now do the opposite." That was hard. Strictly speaking, that night he hadn't just let it go: he'd annealed it, softened it all the way. Letting it ease off is what we'd do afterward, in the oven, just a little. Hardening is uphill. You heat the steel red-hot and cool it fast, so it sets with the strain locked inside. People have been doing that for three thousand years, by hand, with a forge and a bucket of water.

Mikey hardened it by hand. Ordinary hands, no power at all. I lit the gas torch for him, and he held the spring in the blue flame with pliers until it glowed red, then dropped it into a jar of oil. The oil hissed. Then he let it ease off a little (not until it was soft, just until it was springy) on a sheet pan in the oven, the way I'd shown him. It took an hour. No magic at all. Just work.

Then he put the padlock back together, and the shackle snapped shut. And held.

"Try it," I said. "With your hands. Like that night."

Mikey looked at me. Then at the padlock. Then he took it in his palm and closed his eyes.

Half a minute later, he opened them.

"I could," he said. "Same as before. A minute. It's mean again, and it wants to rest again." He paused and handed me the padlock, still locked. "I'm not going to."

"There you go," I said. "The metal won't protect against this. But anybody who can do it is going to stand at the doors for a minute, pale, with white fingers. Then go eat like he hasn't eaten in a week. People can see that. That's not 'no marks' anymore."

Mikey thought about it.

"So everybody needs to know what it looks like," he said. "So if somebody's standing there like that, you can tell."

"There you go," I said. "You came up with it yourself. I'd never have gotten there."

---

We fixed the hinge that same Sunday, in the morning, before the padlock. It was so cold the doors rang if you knocked on them.

Without a thimble, Mikey couldn't have turned the rust back into iron: that's uphill, tearing the iron away from the oxygen, and expensive. So we did it by hand. Mikey scraped off the rust with a wire brush, straightened the hinge with a hammer on an old wheel rim, the way you would on an anvil, and greased it. Just once, with my back turned to Hank, I added a little metal from the scrap pile by the fence into the eaten-away edge. That took a minute and half of one of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies. Mikey did the rest himself. Two hours.

At first Hank stood by the doors with a thermos and didn't say anything. Then, still without a word, he brought out a second cup and poured Mikey some tea. Then he said, without looking at us:

"You know why I spend more time here than at home? My wife's been gone six years. It's quiet at home. Out here there's metal. It's never quiet with metal. It creaks, it rusts, it breaks. It always needs something." He paused. "So what I need is for somebody to need me."

Mikey stopped hammering. Then he started again. More quietly.

At eleven, like every Sunday, Mom called. She heard the doors creaking and asked:

"Tim, where are you? What's all that banging?"

"Fixing some doors, Mom. With my apprentice."

"Your apprentice?" said Mom. "You?"

"Me."

Mom was quiet. Then she said, as if not to me but to Aunt Wanda next to her:

"Wanda, Tim has an apprentice." And to me again: "Your father would have been so happy. He always said you'd make a teacher. Or a scientist. I forget which, by now."

By lunchtime the hinge had stopped creaking. The door swung smoothly, at the push of one finger. Hank opened it, closed it, opened it.

"Your dad show you how to do a hinge?" he asked Mikey.

"Tim did," said Mikey.

"Well," said Hank, "then he can show you how to do a vise, too."

---

That night I sat in the kitchen writing in my notebook. Lugnut came back from somewhere with Old Pete's pen in his teeth, set it on the table and lay down beside it. I didn't take it away from him.

I wrote down Old Pete's three rules. Word for word. Then I looked at the third: "Three. So does the one who taught him, if he knew the student could do it and didn't tell him where the edge is."

I knew. I didn't tell him. Half the welder is mine.

That was fair. And somehow it made me feel lighter than if nobody had blamed me for anything at all.

Below it I wrote: "Glen puts out the light so nobody finds him. The building decided differently: everybody glows, but everybody knows where the edge is. What does that cost? Today: half a welder. What does it cost Glen? Everything else."

Then I thought about it and added: "Teaching doesn't mean showing what you can do. Teaching means showing where the edge is. I don't know how to do that. I'll have to learn it myself."

I put down the pen. Old Pete's pen. Lugnut looked at it, then at me. And didn't carry it off.

He must have figured that with me, it was on the books.
