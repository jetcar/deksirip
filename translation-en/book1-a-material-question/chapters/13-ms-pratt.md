# Chapter 13. Ms. Pratt

At ten o'clock on Friday morning a woman came up to my booth in a gray coat, carrying a gray folder and wearing glasses that immediately fogged up.

That was the first thing I learned about her: her glasses fog up. It was cold outside, October was almost over, and in my booth the soldering iron had been heating since morning and three thermoses of boiling water stood on the counter like coffee urns at a church supper. The woman stepped into the warmth, and her glasses went white. She took them off, wiped them with a handkerchief and put them back on. They fogged up again. She took them off again and looked at me without them: squinting, closely, the way you look at a document you suspect has an error in it.

"Nagel, Timothy?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Pratt, Nina. Revenue Department." She showed me her ID. I couldn't make out a thing on it, but I could tell it was real. Only real IDs have faces like that. "We've received a complaint about you."

"What kind of complaint?"

"Unlicensed commercial activity on a large scale," said Ms. Pratt, opening her folder. "Resulting in the formation of lines."

The line standing behind her, seven people with knives, scissors and one meat grinder, exchanged looks. Across the aisle, Ashot stopped hammering.

"Resulting in the formation of lines," I repeated.

"That's what it says," said Ms. Pratt. "I don't make things up. I check them."

---

I should explain that for twelve years I'd lived in a gray area. Not at the dark end of it: I'd never hidden anything or cheated anybody. But not at the clean end either. I worked out of a booth, took cash, paid rent to Mr. Abrams, and Mr. Abrams, presumably, paid somebody else. I wrote out receipts on blank forms I'd bought at an office-supply store years ago, and on the backs of those receipts I wrote down my thoughts. The Revenue Department didn't know about me. I tried not to think about it either.

It worked as long as I was making five bags a day. Five bags a day doesn't get noticed. It's like water dripping from a faucet: everybody knows it's there, but nobody measures it.

Thirty-three bags a day is no longer a drip. It's a stream. You can see it from a long way off. And a line at a booth isn't just people who've come to get their knives fixed. It's what I'd thought myself on Monday: an advertisement. Only you never know who's reading it.

Somebody had read it and filed a complaint.

I didn't know who. Maybe the repair shop around the corner, whose customers I'd taken. Maybe one of my neighbors in the row of booths, out of envy. Maybe Lenny Kurgan: he had his reasons not to like me. Or maybe just somebody who didn't like the line. There are people like that: they see a line, they file a complaint.

"Do you have registration documents?" asked Ms. Pratt.

"No," I said honestly.

"A cash register?"

"No."

"A record of income and expenses?"

"Yes," I said, and reached under the counter.

Ms. Pratt raised her eyebrows. Apparently she hadn't expected that.

I pulled out my stack of receipts. Crumpled, written on both sides. On the front: "Umbrella repair, half a bag" and a last name. On the back: "Price for what?", "Value = the thing + trust", "Ashot sells. I make."

Ms. Pratt took the stack, put on her glasses (they fogged up at once), took them off again and started reading without them, holding the receipts right up to her face.

She read for a long time. The line waited. Ashot came out of his booth and stood beside her with his arms crossed, like a man ready at any second to say something very persuasive.

"Mrs. Pratt," said Ashot. "How is your health? How is your husband?"

"Ms.," said Ms. Pratt, without looking up from the receipts. "I'm not married. And that's not relevant."

Ashot shut his mouth and went back to crossing his arms. It was the first time I'd ever seen his method fail.

"What is a 'bag'?" asked Ms. Pratt, still reading.

"A bag of pierogi," I said. "That's how I count. A hundred eighty-nine a bag."

Ms. Pratt slowly raised her head.

"You keep track of your income in pierogi?"

"It makes more sense to me that way."

"And what's this?" She turned a receipt over. "'If for time, I go broke. If for the result, am I robbing people?' Is this bookkeeping?"

"It's philosophy."

"Philosophy," Ms. Pratt repeated. She put the receipts back on the counter, carefully, in a stack, squaring the edges. "Mr. Nagel. Over the past week, if I'm reading your records correctly, you earned roughly three or four times as much as in any week before that. Is that so?"

"Roughly."

"With no registration. No cash register. No taxes."

"No taxes," I agreed. There was no point lying to her. She had the face of someone who'd heard every explanation in the world and hadn't believed a single one.

"That's called unlicensed commercial activity," said Ms. Pratt. "A fine. Back taxes for the entire period. And if the amounts are large, a different agency."

The line behind her stopped exchanging looks and froze.

---

That was when Old Pete stepped in.

I don't know how he ended up at the market at ten on a Friday morning. Old Pete doesn't go to the market. He considers it "a place where everybody lies about the price." But there he was at my booth, in an old coat and a hat, with a thick ledger under his arm. Later it came out that he'd taken the first bus in to present me with my balance in person. A debtor, in his view, ought to know his own deficit by sight.

"Madam Auditor," said Old Pete. "If I may."

Ms. Pratt turned around.

"And you are?"

"Peter Palmer. Accountant. Thirty-one years in the profession. The precast concrete plant, then the shoe factory, then retirement. I keep the books for a mutual aid association. And the complaint," he added, lowering his voice, "was filed by property management. They do it on purpose."

"The complaint was anonymous," said Ms. Pratt.

"Exactly," said Old Pete.

"What association?"

"The Exchange," said Old Pete with dignity, as if it were the name of a major corporation. "Of Stairwell Number Three, 17 Factory Street. Mr. Nagel is a member. Here are the books."

He set his ledger on my counter, on top of the receipts, and opened it to the first page.

Ms. Pratt looked at the ledger. Put on her glasses. The glasses fogged up. She took them off and bent over the ledger so low her nose nearly touched it.

I watched her face change. Slowly. First there was irritation. Then bewilderment. Then something else entirely. Interest. Maybe even admiration. The way a musician looks at somebody else's score and finds it written correctly.

"Double-entry," she said quietly.

"Double-entry," Old Pete confirmed. "By the book. Debit, credit. Opening balance, activity, closing balance."

"In hand pies."

"In hand pies," said Old Pete. "The unit of account is one hand pie by Dorothy Mazur, medium, cabbage. Approved by a general meeting of the membership. The minutes are on file."

"The minutes are on file?" asked Ms. Pratt.

"Everything's on file with me," said Old Pete.

Ms. Pratt turned a page. Then another. Then went back to the first.

"Mr. Nagel," she said slowly, "has a balance of minus thirty-one."

"Quite correct," said Old Pete. "Mr. Nagel eats a great deal and charges very little. He owes the association."

Ms. Pratt looked at me. Then at Old Pete. Then back at the ledger.

"Mr. Palmer," she said, in an entirely different voice. "Where did you study?"

"State College of Commerce," said Old Pete. "Graduated with honors."

"So did I," said Ms. Pratt. "Only later."

They looked at each other the way people do when they suddenly discover they speak the same rare language. It was the first time I'd ever seen Old Pete look at anybody like that. Usually he looked at people as if he knew something about them that they didn't know themselves. This time he looked as if he'd found somebody who knew the same things he did.

"With honors too?" Old Pete asked quietly.

"With honors too," said Ms. Pratt.

---

None of which solved my problem.

"The association is all very well," said Ms. Pratt, closing the ledger. "Mutual aid isn't taxable when no money changes hands. But Mr. Nagel does have money. Thirty-three… bags a day. That's income. That's taxable."

"I understand," I said.

"You should have registered."

"I didn't know how."

"Ignorance is no excuse."

I looked at my line. At Ashot. At Old Pete. At Ms. Pratt, standing in front of me with her steamed-up glasses in her hand.

And here's what I thought. For twelve years the gray area had suited me, because it didn't cost anything. I didn't pay taxes, so I saved. It had never occurred to me that there was a price for that too. It just doesn't come due right away. It comes due when you get noticed. The moment something finally starts working out for you, the gray area suddenly sends you the bill. For all the years at once.

It was like corrosion. Rust builds up where you can't see it, for years, at a thin spot in a pipe. Then one night: bang. And water through three floors.

"What should I do?" I asked.

Ms. Pratt opened her mouth to answer.

But the answer didn't come from her.

---

"Register as self-employed," said a woman's voice from the line. "Right now. On your phone. Ten minutes."

Everybody turned around.

In the line, between a butcher from the next aisle over and a woman with a meat grinder, stood a woman I'd never seen at our market before. That was obvious right away. The women who shop at our market don't wear coats like hers. The coat was navy blue, long and straight. I don't know anything about clothes, but even I could tell it would have cost more than my whole booth. Under the coat, a suit. In one hand, a small leather bag. Blond hair, smooth, pulled back so that not a single strand escaped. A calm, beautiful face with very attentive gray eyes. Not gray like Val's. Colder. Like steel that's been polished well.

In her other hand she was holding a broken watch bracelet. Metal, thin, with an openwork clasp.

"Sorry to butt in," she said to Ms. Pratt. "I'm an attorney. Goldie."

She didn't give a last name. At the time I didn't pay any attention to that.

"Self-employed status," Goldie went on, "lets you register in ten minutes through an app. The tax is a small percentage of each sale, no filings, no cash register. For a craftsman who works alone, it's ideal. Registration effective today. As for the prior period…" She glanced at Ms. Pratt. "He can voluntarily submit an amended statement. Voluntary disclosure before a determination is a mitigating factor. As you know."

Ms. Pratt looked at Goldie with the expression of someone who has just had her own job explained to her, very politely.

"I do know," she said. "Only it isn't an 'amended statement,' it's a return for the prior period. Under the standard rules, not the new ones: the rate's different. And it only counts in mitigation if he comes in on his own before I write up my report."

"You're right," said Goldie, not the least bit embarrassed. And I realized the two of them were playing some kind of game at my booth, and I was the board.

"Then let's get you registered," said Goldie. She turned to me. "Do you have a phone?"

---

Twelve minutes later I was self-employed. My payday loan had also come through in twelve minutes, once. That had gone just as fast, and a lot worse.

Goldie did everything herself. She took my phone (old, with a screen I'd replaced myself at some point), downloaded the app, typed in my information from my driver's license, took a picture of me right there in front of the booth, pressed some buttons and handed the phone back.

"Done," she said. "Now every time somebody pays you, you open the app and ring up a receipt. The amount, and what it's for. The tax comes out on its own. If you'd like, I can show you later how to do it fast. It's no harder than your receipts."

"And the past?" I asked.

"The past, you add up honestly. From your receipts. In pierogi, if that's easier for you, and then convert." She smiled slightly. Her smile was brief and precise, like a signature. "You'll file a return. Ms. Pratt, as far as I can tell, is a woman of principle, but a reasonable one. If you come in on your own, that's one thing. If somebody brings you in, that's another."

Ms. Pratt stood beside her with her lips pressed together.

"I'm a woman of principle," she confirmed. "But reasonable. Mr. Nagel, you have one week. In one week I expect your return. If everything's honest, the fine will be minimal. If not…"

"It'll be honest," I said.

"We'll see," said Ms. Pratt.

She put her glasses on again. They fogged up again.

That was the twelfth time in half an hour. I'd been counting.

"Ms. Pratt," I said. "May I have your glasses?"

She gave me a suspicious look.

"What for?"

"They keep fogging up. I can make them stop."

"Is this a bribe?"

"No," I said. "It's…" I thought about it. "It's on the house. Through the app. I'll ring it up for zero right now."

Goldie laughed. Briefly, unexpectedly, as if she hadn't expected to laugh herself.

Ms. Pratt looked at me for a long time. Then she took off her glasses and set them on the counter.

"Ring it up for half a bag," she said. "By the book. I'll pay."

---

I took her glasses and went to the back shelf, my back to the counter, as always. The soldering iron smelled of rosin. Behind me Ashot was loudly telling the line that the people at the Revenue Department were human too, and their glasses fogged up too.

Glasses fog up because steam settles on the cold lens and gathers into tiny droplets. Each droplet is like a little lens of its own; together they scatter the light, and everything turns white. If the water didn't bead up but spread across the glass in a thin, even film, you could see through the film as clearly as through clean glass. I remembered that from back when I used to fix people's headlights and read online about why they fog up on the inside. There are special coatings that make glass "water-loving." Water doesn't bead up on it. It spreads out.

First I checked what the lenses were made of. Glass: real glass, heavy, not plastic. I wouldn't have touched plastic: the latch on Mikey's phone still lived in my memory as a pinch of white dust. Ms. Pratt, apparently, didn't trust anything new. Neither did I.

I didn't have a coating. But I did have glass. And I knew glass was a liquid that forgot how to flow.

I put on the thimble, touched the lens and asked its surface (only the very surface, to a depth no eye could ever see) to become ever so slightly uneven. Not rough: that would have made the glasses cloudy. Covered instead with tiny, even, identical dimples, smaller than a wavelength of light. That was in *Crystal Physics*, in a chapter I'd read because of a picture of a moth's eye. The eye can't see dimples that small, but water can, and it spreads through them like water through a sponge.

It was hard. Glass remembered grudgingly, as always. I held a picture in my head: a smooth field, and on it tiny, tiny pits, like the marks raindrops leave in sand, only shrunk ten thousand times over. The thermos under my left hand went cold. I ate a hand pie from the pot Mrs. Mazur had sent over with Mikey that morning. Then I did the second lens.

Ten minutes or so. Two thermoses. Two hand pies.

I went back to the counter and handed the glasses to Ms. Pratt. She took them, held them up to the light, saw nothing special. Put them on.

She breathed on them.

They didn't fog up.

She took the glasses off. Breathed on them again, right on the lens, up close, the way you breathe on a mirror. A thin, even film of moisture appeared on the glass and dried at once. No whiteness. No droplets.

Ms. Pratt put the glasses on and looked at me. Through clear lenses. For the first time in half an hour, with her eyes instead of a squint.

Her eyes turned out to be light brown, very attentive and very tired.

"How did you do that?" she asked.

"New technology," said Ashot, over my shoulder.

"Imported," I added.

Ms. Pratt looked at the two of us over the top of her glasses. It was the look of a revenue auditor filing an answer away to check later.

"I'll look into it," she said. "Whether that technology is in the registry."

She paid. Half a bag. I rang up the receipt (the first of my life), the app beeped, and somewhere deep in the bowels of the government my half a bag split into mine and not mine.

Ms. Pratt took her receipt, folded it in four and put it in her folder.

"Good day, Mr. Nagel," she said. "One week."

And she left.

Old Pete watched her go. For a long time. Then he said, to nobody in particular:

"State College of Commerce. With honors."

"Tim," said Ashot. "Your accountant, what, he fell in love?"

Old Pete didn't answer. He straightened his hat and went after her, clutching the ledger to his chest.

Ashot and I looked at each other.

"What a day," said Ashot.

---

Goldie was still standing at the counter. She hadn't left with the line, hadn't left with Ms. Pratt. She stood there and watched me work: closely, appraisingly, the way you look at something you're planning to buy. Or at someone you're planning to hire.

"What about the bracelet?" I asked. "You came over with a bracelet."

"Oh, right," she said. As if she'd forgotten. Though I could see she hadn't. People like her don't forget anything.

She held out the bracelet. A thin chain of white metal, flat openwork links, the clasp small and intricate, with a tiny spring. The spring had snapped. The links by the clasp had pulled apart.

I put on the thimble. I recognized the gold right away: warm, heavy, pleased with itself, like my pea among the bobbers. But there was something else in it too, something pale and ringing. Not nickel: I knew nickel from the CAFETERIA spoons. I remembered the forums where people talk about getting gold off circuit boards: white gold is made with palladium. That had to be it.

I weighed the bracelet in my palm. Eight grams or so. The metal alone was worth two hundred-some bags. Almost my whole debt as of the morning I found the thimble.

The spring was steel, a separate piece, as thin as a hair. I mended it, put it back in place and bent the spread links closed. One minute and half a thermos: the spring was thin, but it was tempered, and those never come cheap for me.

Goldie took the bracelet, fastened it on her wrist and checked it. She looked at me.

"How much?"

"Half a bag," I said. "Through the app."

She raised an eyebrow.

"Do you know what this bracelet is worth?"

"I've got a guess."

"And you're charging half a bag?"

"I charge for the work," I said. "Not for the bracelet. That was half a bag's worth of work."

Goldie looked at me for a very long time. Then she took a business card out of her bag and laid it on the counter. White, heavy stock, embossed. I didn't read it right away.

"I'll pay half a bag," she said. "Through the app. By the book." She paid. The app beeped. "And I'll leave my card. In case you ever need an attorney. Or…" She paused. "In case you'd like to talk about how your line could be worth ten times as much."

"It can't be worth ten times as much," I said. "I can't keep up as it is."

"That's because you're on your own," said Goldie. "And because you have a booth. There are other ways to do this."

She turned and left. Without looking back. Her coat swung like a theater curtain.

Ashot watched her go.

"Tim," he said. "You know who that is?"

"An attorney."

I looked at the card. "GOLDIE. Business Legal Services." That was all.

"An attorney," I repeated. "It says so right here."

"It says what she does," said Ashot. "Not whose she is. No last name. When a person in a coat like that doesn't put her last name on the card, it means her last name is known at our market."

I looked in the direction Goldie had gone. She was already out of sight.

"She helped me," I said.

"She helped," Ashot agreed. "For free. That's what's suspicious. Coats like that don't help for free."

---

That evening I sat in the kitchen and did the math.

Not bags. Taxes.

It was a strange feeling: for the first time in my life, figuring out how much I owed not to Bull, not to Ashot, not to Mrs. Mazur, but to the government. The government isn't a person. You can't feed it hand pies, and you can't talk it into waiting a month by fixing its valve. All it wants is money. Only money.

Naturally I didn't have twelve years of receipts: I'd always lost them. The only ones that survived were the ones with thoughts on the back. I worked out the earlier years from my old column, thirty bags a week, converted the bags into money and multiplied by the rate. Ms. Pratt had said the rate for the past would be different, a little higher. It came out to more than I'd hoped, but less than I'd feared.

And then a thought came to me.

The Exchange works on the inside. Inside the stairwell, inside the market, between people who know each other. There you can trade hand pies, valves, heels, chemistry, a month's grace. There you hardly need money.

Then there's the outside. The rent Mr. Abrams collects. The debt Bull collects. The tax Ms. Pratt collects. The electricity the meter counts. That's the outside world. It doesn't know Mrs. Mazur and doesn't eat her hand pies. It only takes money.

And come to think of it, it doesn't just take. Taxes pay for the clinic, where Judy takes people's blood pressure for a paycheck, not for hand pies. Taxes patch the road Bull drives his old blue sedan on. Taxes keep the Poly going, and the library, where Mrs. Nicholls lends out books for nothing. Meanwhile the blood pressure pills Judy recommends are sold at the pharmacy, and the pharmacy doesn't take hand pies: the pharmacist doesn't know Mrs. Mazur. The outside world is an exchange too. Just a very big one. Everybody there is a stranger to everybody else, so its measure isn't a hand pie. It's money.

I wrote in my notebook: "Inside: hand pies. Outside: money. Money is for people you don't know personally."

I thought it over and added: "Taxes are hand pies for strangers."

I read it again. I liked it. Then it occurred to me that I'd better not show that formula to Ms. Pratt. She'd ask where my twelve years of hand pies for strangers were.

Then I picked up Goldie's card and turned it over in my fingers. White, heavy stock, embossed. "Business Legal Services." On the back, handwritten, a cell number. In neat, even, confident handwriting.

"How your line could be worth ten times as much."

I thought about Greg, whose knife doesn't go dull anymore. About Roxanne and her pruners. About the old newspaper man who'd said "Oh." About how there was a line at my booth now, and at Ashot's too. About how for the first time in twelve years I'd rung up a receipt, and I wasn't ashamed of it.

Ten times as much. That would be… I tried to picture it. I couldn't.

I put the card on the shelf, between *Crystal Physics* and the third volume of Professor Stern's notebooks. I didn't throw it out. I didn't call, either.

Lugnut came over, sniffed the card and sneezed.

"Agreed," I said.

My phone beeped. A message in the Exchange group. From Old Pete.

"Notice to members. Auditor Pratt, Nina has expressed interest in the association's accounting methods. A consultation has been scheduled. Tuesday, 5:00 p.m. Attendance optional."

Right after it, one from Mrs. Mazur:

"Pete, that's a date."

Old Pete answered a minute later:

"It's a consultation."

And a minute after that:

"I know."
