# Chapter 16. Goldie

Goldie called on Wednesday, at eight-thirty in the morning, while I was opening the shutter.

She had my number. She'd typed it into the app herself, on my own phone, the day I became self-employed. I hadn't thought about that at the time. That day I hadn't thought about much of anything, except Ms. Pratt and her foggy glasses.

"Timothy Nagel?" she asked. Her voice was even and quick, like a dispatcher who knows where every truck is. "This is Goldie. Business Legal Services. Remember me?"

"The bracelet," I said. "The spring on the clasp."

"The spring is holding," said Goldie. "I check it every morning. You know, it's the first thing in my life that anybody ever fixed better than it was made." A pause. "I'd like to invite you to dinner. On business. Friday, at seven. The Summit, on the top floor of the hotel by the train station. I've reserved a table."

"What business?"

"The business I mentioned at your booth," she said. "How your line could be worth ten times as much. I ran the numbers again. It comes out to more than ten."

Behind me the shutter creaked. All by itself, in the wind. I'd left it unfixed on purpose, for cover, and it was doing its very best.

"I'll think about it," I said.

"Think about it until Friday," said Goldie. "Seven o'clock. Dress however you like. But not the work jacket, please. There's a coat check, and the coat-check lady will suffer."

And she hung up.

Ashot was standing by his booth with a hammer in his hand. The one that doesn't break anymore. He'd watched me on the phone, and he'd read the whole thing on my face.

"The coat?" he asked.

"The coat," I said.

"Where?"

"The Summit."

Ashot laid the hammer down on the counter. This was serious. Ashot never puts down his hammer until he's finished a heel tip.

"Tim," he said. "At the Summit, a salad…" He stumbled. "A salad there costs what I used to make in a day. Four bags. On a bad day. I was there once. At my nephew's wedding. For six months afterward I dreamed about their napkins."

"Why the napkins?"

"Because they're cloth," said Ashot. "And nobody takes them home. That's what amazed me most."

---

All day long I thought about Friday. I made knives and plates for forever heel tips, hardened a new pair of pruners for Roxanne, straightened Lottie's fish scaler. She'd dropped it under her counter, and a delivery guy had stepped on it. And the whole time, I was thinking.

I thought the way I'm used to: on the back of a napkin. Meaning, on the back of a receipt.

First I figured out what I had. That was easy. On a good day, the line brought in fifty bags or so. A quarter to Ashot left thirty-seven and a half. Tax through the app: two. Rent, which had gone up. And food. Food was the biggest item: about ten bags a day. That included Greg's meat, the lunches I sometimes treated Ashot to, and the boiling water and gas I paid the meat-pie stand for, ever since I'd had to give up my evening cord because of Corey and his aluminum splices. I ate so much that Greg the butcher once offered to sell me meat wholesale. Thermoses, hand pies, fried meat pies, fried meat buns, two bags of pierogi in the evening. After all of that I had about twenty bags a day left in my hands. Good. Better than ever in my life. Bull got paid once a week. The rest went to living.

Next I tried to work out "ten times as much." Two hundred bags a day. Then I realized I'd multiplied the wrong thing: she'd been talking about the line, not about what I kept. That made it even more. I couldn't picture it. I pictured two hundred bags of pierogi and understood that they wouldn't fit in my freezer or in my head. It wasn't food anymore. It was a wall.

And what would I do with a wall like that?

Pay off the debt. All at once, on the very first day, not once a week. That's one. New windows for Mom: it's drafty at Aunt Wanda's. That's two. An analyzer for Val. The one she'd been dreaming about, for the silicon. That's three.

I got stuck on number four.

I had nothing to buy. It was a strange discovery. The shelf I'd made myself. The chair, myself. The fridge I'd fixed. My pots don't burn, my knife doesn't go dull, my door shuts. I didn't have a bike, but come to think of it, there was one lying by the dumpsters, just waiting for me to get around to it. I got hand pies for fixing irons. Chemistry, for repairing balances.

What I needed couldn't be bought. And what could be bought, I already had. Or I could make it.

I wrote on the receipt: "Money is for what you don't know how to do. And I know how to do more and more." I looked at it. Thought about it. Added: "But not everything."

The windows for Mom I couldn't make myself: she lived out in the country, at Aunt Wanda's, a long way off. The analyzer for Val, even less. That was electronics: complicated, precise, a hundred parts I don't understand. And then there was Bull. What Bull needed wasn't hand pies.

So maybe I did need a wall after all. Twenty bricks or so, at least.

---

On Thursday evening I told Val.

We were sitting in my kitchen. She was grading freshman lab reports in red pen and now and then groaning out loud. I was sewing the strap back onto her tote: by hand, with a needle, no power at all. The strap was nylon, and I don't understand polymers. Val said she loved that tote as a monument to the new one she still hadn't bought. The thimble was on my finger. Since Saturday I'd hardly taken it off.

"I've been invited to dinner," I said.

"By who?"

"Goldie. The one from the Revenue Department. No, not the Revenue Department—the attorney. The one who signed me up as self-employed."

Val raised her head from the lab report. The pen in her hand stopped above somebody's formula.

"The one with the coat," she said. It wasn't a question.

"How do you know about the coat?"

"Ashot told Mikey when Mikey brought Mrs. Mazur's pot by the market. Mikey told Mrs. Mazur, Mrs. Mazur told Shirley, Shirley told Katie in 40, and Katie told me, when she was hemming my jeans. The whole building knows about the coat, Tim. Pete thinks she's a foreign spy."

"Why foreign?"

"Because nobody from around here wears a coat like that." Val took off her glasses, wiped them on the hem of her sweater and put them back on. "Where?"

"The Summit. It's business. She wants to offer me a job."

Val looked at me for a long time. Without any teasing. I felt the snaps on my shirt stir, and I held them down with my palm.

"Go," she said at last.

"You don't mind?"

"I mind," said Val. "But that doesn't change anything. You're a grown man, she's a grown woman. It's a business dinner. If you don't go, you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what would have happened. And I'll spend it thinking you didn't go because of me. Go." She went back to the lab report. The pen started moving again. "Just wear the shirt with the snaps."

"Why?"

"So that if anything happens, I'll know," Val said, without looking up.

I couldn't tell whether she was joking. I still can't.

---

On Friday at seven I was standing at the entrance to the Summit.

I'd put on the only sport coat I owned. It had been my father's. The last time anybody wore it was to his funeral. Me, not him. It was tight in the shoulders and loose at the waist, and before I left I'd altered it a little: I didn't touch the fabric, I just moved the buttons up a little, by hand. I'd pressed my pants with Mrs. Mazur's iron, the one I'd fixed once myself. The shirt was the one with the snaps. For half a bag, Ashot had shined my shoes until you could see the elevator in them. I didn't take off the thimble. I was afraid to leave it at home with Lugnut, and in a pocket it's useless. So I spent the whole evening with it on my middle finger and kept that hand under my napkin.

The elevator took a minute to get to the twelfth floor. In that minute I managed to count my reflections: seven of me, in mirrors on three walls. In all seven I looked like a man who'd come to fix the air conditioning.

The coat-check lady took my jacket with two fingers. I thought Goldie had been right, and then right away I thought I should make a point of never telling her so.

Goldie was sitting by the window.

All of Granton lay outside it. Evening, December, all lit up. I'd never seen it from above. I'd seen it from below: from the market, from the bus, from my fourth-floor balcony. From up here it looked like my solar wafer under the magnifier: a dark field with bright veins of streets. I found the train station. I found the river. I found the dark patch on the bank where the beach is in summer, where Val and I had gotten our sand. And I found the market. It was dark (nobody's there in the evenings), with a single streetlight at the gate, by the meat-pie stand.

"Do you like it?" asked Goldie.

She wasn't wearing the coat. That was the first thing I noticed. A black dress, simple, off the shoulder, and on her left wrist, the bracelet. My bracelet. Hers, I mean, but with my spring.

"The town," I said. "I've never seen it from above."

"From up here you can see best what could be changed in it," said Goldie. "Have a seat, Timothy."

I sat down. The chair was soft and heavy, with curved legs. Without thinking, I touched a leg with the finger that had the thimble on it. Oak, solid, glue, and a steel pin inside. Good work. A chair like that will last fifty years, as long as nobody tips back in it.

"You touch the furniture," Goldie observed.

"Occupational hazard," I said.

"I know," she said. "I saw. You touch everything. As if you're checking what the world is made of."

I didn't like that. Not because it wasn't true. Because it was true, and somebody had seen it.

---

The waiter brought the menus. The menu had a leather cover and was heavy, like one of Professor Stern's volumes. I opened it and started counting.

On its own, automatically. I couldn't help it. Caesar salad: four bags. Porcini mushroom soup: five. Steak: twelve. Twelve bags of pierogi is almost a week of my dinners. For one plate. And on the plate, a sprig of something green that nobody eats.

"You're counting," said Goldie.

"What?"

"Your lips are moving. You're counting in something. Not in money." She tilted her head slightly. "In what?"

"In pierogi," I said honestly.

Goldie laughed. The same short, unexpected laugh as at my booth. As if the laugh had gotten out without her permission.

"And how many pierogi are in a steak?"

"Twelve bags."

"Is that a lot?"

"That's a week."

She stopped laughing. She looked at me. Then at the menu. Then at me again.

"I'll order you the steak," she said. "On the company. Don't argue. I want you to taste a week."

I wanted to argue. But I'd worked at the market all day, I was starving, and I could already smell the steak from the kitchen. I didn't argue. I'm still a little ashamed of that. That's where it all started.

---

The steak was good. Very good. I ate it slowly, trying to taste the difference between it and a bag of pierogi. There was a difference. Just not twelve times over.

Goldie hardly ate. She drank her wine in small sips and talked.

She talked the way she'd worked at my booth: quickly, precisely, forgetting nothing. She had a plan. It was a beautiful plan.

There's a company. Her father's company, and she has her own law firm that handles it. "I handle what's already built," she said. "What gets built is Dad's call." The company builds. Not booths: buildings. Shopping centers, offices, apartment complexes. And in buildings, everything breaks. Elevators, doors, railings, display windows, pipes, fences, steel framing. Something every week. And every time it means a contractor, an estimate, parts, deadlines, penalties.

"And you fix in a minute," said Goldie, "what other people spend a month replacing. I saw the bracelet. I showed it to an expert. A very good, very private man who makes special alloys for us. He said there's no such thing as soldering like that. That there's no seam. That the spring looks as if it grew in one piece." She looked at me over her glass. "He'd very much like to meet you. I told him where you work. I'm sorry, I didn't know then that it was a secret."

Something inside me clenched, cold. I understood why, but I didn't want to understand. An expert in alloys. A very private man. Twenty years. "Don't search."

"Please don't," I said.

"As you wish." Goldie didn't press, and that only made it worse. "So. I'm offering you a contract. An exclusive one. You work only for the company. Your own shop in a new building. An office, if you want one. Assistants. Health insurance. A salary of…"

She named a figure.

I converted it into bags. Then I converted it again, because I didn't believe it. Then I looked at my napkin, the cloth one that nobody takes home, and realized Ashot was right: I'd remember this napkin for six months.

"That's thirty times what you keep now," said Goldie. "I did the math. Your line, your prices, a quarter to the cobbler, rent, tax. And how much you eat. I've seen how much you eat." She smiled slightly. "At the market, everybody can see it."

"Thirty times," I repeated.

"Thirty." Goldie set down her glass. "Plus bonuses. Plus a share in your technology. We won't patent it: a patent means explaining to the whole world how it's done, and why would we want that? We'll treat it as know-how, a trade secret. I'm a good attorney, Timothy. I'll make sure nobody can ever take your technology away from you. Nobody will be able to do the same thing. Only you. Forever."

Only you. Forever.

I put down my fork.

I thought about Professor Stern. About thirty-one notebooks in two plaid shopping bags. "I understood a great deal, and I dared nothing." About how for thirty years he'd kept his object inside a lamp, under the wiring, and nobody, nobody knew.

And I thought: That's a lamp too. Just a golden one. With health insurance and a TOP SECRET stamp.

"What about the market?" I asked.

"What about it?"

"The booth. Ashot. The line. Greg, whose knife doesn't go dull. Mrs. Mazur's iron. The Exchange."

"What's the Exchange?" asked Goldie.

I told her. About the building, about the hand pies, about Pete's ledger and the balance. About how Judy takes Shirley's blood pressure, and Shirley knits socks for Mikey, and Mikey fixes Pete's phone, and Pete writes all of it down. About how my balance was in the black, and science got its own line.

Goldie listened very carefully. She didn't interrupt. When I finished, she was quiet for a moment.

"That's very sweet," she said. And I could see she really meant it, without any mockery. "Honestly. It's like… like swapping stickers at recess. But, Timothy…" She leaned forward, and I caught the scent of her perfume, something bitter and expensive. "You do understand that you're doing this because you have no choice? You trade an iron for a hand pie because you don't have the money for a hand pie. And if you have money, why would you need to get hand pies for irons? You'll buy the hand pie. And the time you spend on other people's irons, you'll spend on something of your own."

It sounded logical. Very logical. I couldn't come up with an answer. I sat there thinking that there was an answer somewhere, I just didn't understand it yet. Same as with plastic.

"Exclusive," I said. "Does that mean I won't be able to fix Mrs. Mazur's iron?"

"It means," Goldie said gently, "that you'll be able to buy Mrs. Mazur a new iron. The very best one. Every year."

And she laid her hand on mine.

---

Then something happened that I hadn't expected.

The snaps on my shirt came undone.

All of them. One after another, top to bottom: click, click, click. Quietly, but distinctly, just like on Saturday in the back room. Goldie had laid her hand right on my thimble hand. The power listens to what I want. I didn't want Goldie. What I wanted, I think, was for it to be Val: a hand on my hand, the snaps, Saturday. And the power doesn't care whose hand it is.

I grabbed my shirt with my free hand. Goldie was staring at me in astonishment.

"Sorry," I said. "It's… it's the shirt. It's old. The snaps."

"All at once?"

"That's how they are," I said. "They do everything together."

I did the snaps back up with one hand, behind my napkin, and felt my ears burning. Goldie watched. And I could see she'd decided it was because of her. That she made me that nervous.

She wasn't entirely wrong. She wasn't entirely right either. It wasn't nerves; it was Saturday. The power wanted what I wanted, and I wanted Val. Her "shirt with the snaps." "So that if anything happens, I'll know."

She'll know anyway, I thought. The snaps will tell her. They always tell on me.

Goldie slowly took her hand away.

"I didn't mean to embarrass you," she said. For the first time all evening, her voice was a little less precise. "This is a business dinner. Really."

"I know," I said.

"Although," said Goldie, and her smile went short and precise again, "I won't say I don't like you at all. I like you, Timothy. You're the first person in years who's told me the price of the work instead of the price of the thing. It's…" she searched for the word, "refreshing."

"There's someone…" I began.

"The lab tech," said Goldie. "Finch. I know. I'm a good attorney."

I went cold: I'd never told her about Val.

Goldie took a sip of wine. "I'm not making any claims. I'm just saying what I think. Maybe someday dinner won't be business. And maybe not. The contract doesn't depend on that. I never mix the two."

She did mix them. I could see it. She mixed in everything she had. The steak, the view out the window, the dress, the bracelet with my spring. Thirty times, the insurance, her hand on mine. She fit it all together as precisely and neatly as I'd laid down the cellulose chains. Booth to booth. So it would hold.

And it held. That was the scariest part. It held.

---

He came over to our table when dessert arrived.

Tall, gray-haired, in a charcoal suit that fit him the way my sport coat had never fit anybody, ever. A big face, with a tan that had nothing to do with December. On his lapel, a small round pin. I'd seen pins like that on TV, on city councilmen.

"Goldilocks," he said. And he kissed the top of her head. "So you're here. I'm with some people from City Hall, over in that corner. Saw you and came over."

"Dad," said Goldie.

And for the first time I saw her flustered. Really flustered. Like someone caught somewhere other than where she'd meant to be.

"This is Timothy Nagel," she said. "The craftsman. I told you about him."

"Ah, the craftsman." He held out his hand. It was warm, dry and strong. I shook it, and the gold of his heavy ring settled right under my thimble. Good gold, heavy. "Vance Cochran. Pleased to meet you. Goldie tells me you work miracles."

"I fix things," I said.

"Modest," Cochran said approvingly. "Good. I like modest." He turned to his daughter. "So, collecting craftsmen? Smart. Grab the best ones while they're still at the market. By spring there'll be a foundation pit there, and good craftsmen don't just lie around in the street. Well, they do, but not for long." He laughed. "You snooze, you lose."

Goldie didn't say anything. She was looking down at her plate.

"A foundation pit," I repeated.

"Well, sure," said Cochran. He seemed surprised that I didn't know. "The Central Market. It's city land, their lease runs out in March, the decision's practically made. There'll be a shopping and entertainment center. Six stories, a parking garage, a movie theater. A thing of beauty." He clapped me on the shoulder. "And you, Timothy, are going to work in it. Somewhere warm, somewhere clean. Not in a booth out in the wind. Goldie will take care of everything. Right, Goldilocks?"

"Right, Dad," Goldie said quietly.

"Well, I won't keep you." He kissed the top of her head again and went back to his corner, where the people from City Hall sat, big and quiet, like refrigerators.

I looked at the dessert spoon in my hand.

It began to twist.

Slowly, all by itself, from the handle to the tip: a corkscrew. Like twisted wire. On the outside, silver, thin as the foil on a chocolate bar, and underneath it cupronickel: copper and nickel. I knew nickel from the CAFETERIA spoons. The silver stretched along with the spoon and didn't tear. The cupronickel creaked. I was angry. I'd never been that angry in my life: at Lenny Kurgan's I'd been scared, and that's something else. It turned out that when I'm angry, the power takes it out on spoons. The twelve-bag steak went into that spoon, all of it. A minute later I was hungry again, as if I hadn't eaten.

Goldie was looking at the spoon.

I laid it down on the tablecloth. It lay there, twisted, like the spring from her bracelet, only a hundred times bigger.

"Is that old too?" Goldie asked very quietly. "Like the snaps?"

"No," I said. "That's new."

Goldie looked at the spoon longer than she looked at me. And I understood that in the morning her expert would hear about more than the bracelet.

---

We sat in silence for maybe a minute. Down below, outside the window, lay the town. The dark patch of the market with its single streetlight by the meat-pie stand. Ashot, Greg, Lottie, Roxanne, Stefan with his sheet pans, the old newspaper man. Mr. Abrams with his five booths and his wife with the bad knee. A foundation pit by spring.

"You knew," I said.

"I knew the market was going to be torn down," said Goldie. She looked me in the eye. Straight. She didn't lie, and afterward I spent a long time thinking about what that said about her. "Everybody knows it'll be torn down someday. It's old, it's on good land, it's ugly. I didn't know it would be so soon. Dad doesn't…" She paused. "Dad doesn't always tell me everything."

"Then why did you come to my booth?"

"Because I saw a craftsman it would be a shame to lose," said Goldie. "I really did think that. I thought: sooner or later they'll close the market, and you'll be out on the street. With your hands. With your line. I wanted you to be inside a building when that happened. So you'd have everything." She paused. "I still think that."

"What about Ashot?"

"What about him?"

"Ashot will be out on the street too."

Goldie didn't answer.

"And Greg. And Lottie. And Roxanne. And Stefan. Are they getting offered a shop in the building too?"

"Timothy," said Goldie. "I can't save everyone."

"Neither can I," I said. "I can't save anyone at all. All I can do is fix things."

It sounded stupid. But it was true.

Goldie was quiet for a moment. Then she took a thin folder out of her purse and laid it on the table next to the twisted spoon. Inside the folder was a contract. Ten pages of fine print, with a space for a signature on the last one.

"This still stands," she said. "No matter what you think of me right now, or of my father. It's a good contract. I wrote it myself. There are no traps in it. I checked every word, the way you check the legs of chairs." She slid the folder toward me. "Think about it. Until New Year's. And I'll put the spoon on the bill. On the company."

And I didn't say no.

That was the worst thing about the evening. Not the twelve-bag steak. Not the snaps. Not the spoon. Not Cochran and his foundation pit.

The worst thing was that I took the folder.

I said, "I'll think about it." I folded it in half and slid it into the inside pocket of my father's sport coat. And I shook Goldie's hand goodbye. She shook mine (firmly, briefly, businesslike), and in her handshake there was something I didn't understand and didn't want to understand.

---

I walked home. From the train station to Factory Street is about forty minutes. It was snowing. I froze, even with my jacket over my father's sport coat, but I didn't care.

I was thinking.

Thirty times. I worked it out again and again, and every time it came out the same. The debt: paid off on the very first day. Windows for Mom. For Val, the analyzer, and a whole lab to go with it. An apartment of my own, not a rental. And…

And nothing more. Again. The list stopped at number five. On Wednesday, on the receipt, it had stopped at number four. Thirty times had added exactly one item.

And what would I be giving up?

The booth. The shutter I leave unfixed on purpose. Ashot, who puts down his hammer when things are serious. The line, where the old newspaper man said "Oh." The Exchange. Mrs. Mazur's iron. Old Pete with his ledger. Val's evenings in my kitchen. Exclusive means you work where they tell you, when they tell you. And one more thing, the most important, which didn't occur to me until I got to my building.

"Nobody will ever be able to do the same thing. Only you. Forever."

I thought about it outside my building, under the streetlight, with the snow falling on my face. I thought about how a week ago, in my kitchen, I'd written in my notebook: "Need more hands. Where?" The word "Where" was underlined twice, and I still couldn't remember doing that. And Goldie was offering to make sure that there would only ever be one pair of hands. Mine. Stamped TOP SECRET.

I should have said no right there at the table. Over the twisted spoon.

I hadn't said no.

I climbed to the fourth floor. Lugnut was sitting in the hall, looking at me the way you look at someone who's come home late smelling of somebody else's perfume.

"Don't start," I told him.

I took off the thimble and put it on the shelf, then took off the sport coat and hung it on the back of a chair. Lugnut sniffed the coat. Slowly, thoroughly, from the hem to the lapel. Then he stuck his nose into the inside pocket.

He didn't touch the thimble on the shelf. He never touched it.

The folder, though, he pulled out. The whole thing, in his teeth, by one corner. He laid it on the floor. And sat down next to it, looking at me.

There was nothing shiny in that folder. Just paper. Ten pages of fine print.

"That's not yours," I said.

Lugnut looked at me. Without blinking. Like Ms. Pratt over the top of her glasses. Like Val when she said "Go."

I picked up the folder and put it on the shelf, between *Crystal Physics* and the third volume of Professor Stern's notebooks. Next to her business card. Let them keep each other company.

Then I sat down in the kitchen and took out my phone. It was a quarter to eleven. Val wasn't asleep yet, I knew that: on Fridays she graded lab reports until midnight.

I looked at her name in my contacts for a long time.

I needed to tell her. About the steak. About the snaps coming undone on their own. About the hand on mine. About the foundation pit by spring. About thirty times. About the folder on the shelf.

About how I hadn't said no.

I wrote: "Home. Everything's fine. Tell you tomorrow."

I looked at "Everything's fine." Deleted it.

I wrote: "Home. Tell you tomorrow. Everything."

Sent it.

Val answered a minute later.

"Snaps?"

I looked down at my shirt. All the snaps were fastened. I'd done them up myself, by hand, under a cloth napkin at the Summit, and they were holding.

I wrote: "Came undone. On their own. When she took my hand. I did them back up."

I sent it. And I sat there looking at the screen, and I could almost hear Val, somewhere on the other side of town, reading it, and taking off her glasses, and wiping them on the hem of her sweater.

The answer came five minutes later. The longest five minutes of December.

"So she took your hand, and the power remembered me. Good. Tomorrow, everything. Go to sleep."

I read it three times. Then I put the phone face down.

Lugnut came into the kitchen, jumped up onto the windowsill and sat there looking out. Out there, past the buildings, past the factory smokestack, in the dark, was the market. You couldn't see it from here. But I knew where it was.

I took out my notebook and wrote: "Thirty times. The list stops at number five. Foundation pit in March."

I thought about it. Added: "I didn't say no. Why?"

I didn't have an answer. Only shame. Warm, heavy, dense, like a twelve-bag steak.

I sat over the notebook until one in the morning, and the answer never came.
