# Chapter 11. The Neighbor Across the Aisle

Ashot came across the aisle slowly, the way people walk when they've already decided what they're going to say and are just giving themselves time to change their minds.

He didn't change his mind.

He stopped at my counter, right in front of the line. The line went quiet. Everybody at the market knows that when Ashot leaves his booth in the middle of a working day, it's either a fire or a conversation. There was no fire.

"Tim," he said. Not loudly. Ashot never shouts, ever. For twenty years he's been haggling in a way that makes people hand him their money and thank him for it. He has no use for shouting. "Can I have a minute?"

"I've got a line."

"I see," said Ashot. "I see you've got a line. Yesterday I saw you had a line. Today I see I don't have a line. I see a woman who has brought me her shoes for fifteen years, and today she's standing in yours. I see a lot of things, Tim. Can I have a minute?"

The woman with the boot looked down. The man with the knife in front of her pretended to be studying the We Fix Everything sign.

I came out of the booth.

---

We went around behind my counter, over to the wall where the crates are stacked. Ashot sat down on one. I sat on another. People with shopping bags kept going by along the aisle next to us, and the market hummed around us the way it always does, and at the same time it seemed to have gone quiet, listening.

"How much did you make yesterday?" Ashot asked.

"Twenty-two."

"Twenty-two," Ashot repeated. "And me, one and a half. One and a half bags, Tim. For a day. Twenty years I've been at this market. I've never made one and a half. Not even when I was sick. Not even when my wife was in labor and I sat here with my phone, and my hands were shaking, I made four."

He was quiet for a while. So was I. I didn't know what to say.

"I have a son," said Ashot. "Arsen. He's studying to be a doctor. In another city. That costs money, Tim. The dorm, the books, food. I send him money every month. Every month. For twenty years I put heel tips on shoes so my son wouldn't have to put heel tips on shoes." He looked at me. "And now people don't come to me. Because yours last forever."

"I don't make heel tips that last forever," I said.

"But people say you do."

"People are lying. I can't do rubber. I tried it once on a phone, and the plastic crumbled into powder. I don't touch rubber."

Ashot looked at me for a long time. Then he said:

"It doesn't matter, Tim. It doesn't matter what you do. What matters is what people think. They think everything you've got is better. Knives, better. Pruners, better. So heel tips must be better too. People don't know the difference. They go where the line is."

That was true, and it made me feel sick. Yesterday I'd thought the line was a reward. That people were coming to me because I did good work. But it turned out a line is also a force: it pulls everything around it along behind it. It even pulls in things I have nothing to do with.

"I didn't mean to," I said.

"I know you didn't mean to," said Ashot. "You're not like that. I've known you twelve years. You're not capable of meaning to. You're too honest to mean to." He sighed. "But that doesn't change anything. You didn't mean to, and I made one and a half bags."

---

I sat on the crate and thought.

The first thing that came to mind was to shut the line down. Tell people, "I'm not taking knives anymore." Work the way I used to. Then the line would disappear, and people would go back to Ashot.

But that was stupid. Greg already knew there were knives that didn't go dull. Roxanne knew there were pruners that didn't rust. If I stopped, they wouldn't forget. They'd just be unhappy. And Ashot still wouldn't get back the woman with the boot, because she already knew I had a line.

The second thing was to split the market. "Ashot, you do everything that's leather and rubber. I do everything that's metal." But that was exactly how we'd been splitting it for twelve years, along the line called "heel." And today that line wasn't working. People don't know where leather ends and metal begins. They go where the line is.

The third thing…

I raised my head and looked at my line. Twelve people. They stood there shifting from foot to foot, and somebody was already starting an argument. The man with the knife: that he was first. A woman with a pot: that she'd been there before him, she'd just stepped away. A kid with a bicycle bell: that his mom was coming any minute and then they'd all see.

And I thought: who's minding that line right now? Nobody. I'm sitting on a crate with Ashot. The line's on its own, with nobody in charge. In five minutes somebody's going to shove somebody, and there'll be a scene. And yesterday, while I was working, I wasn't minding the line either: I was inside the booth with my back to the people and the thimble on my finger. Yesterday the line had been on its own too. Yesterday people had argued about who was first, and I couldn't look up, because I was holding the steel's lattice in my head.

I was doing the work. But nobody was doing all the rest. Talking to people. Writing things down. Setting a price. Explaining what would be ready by lunch and what would be ready tomorrow. Telling the woman with the pot, "Five minutes, and I'll take you." Smiling.

And Ashot had all of that. For twenty years that was exactly what he'd been good at: talking to people so they believed him. I'd seen it two weeks ago, when he stole my customer with the handbag. She'd gone to him because he looked like a man who knew everything about leather.

I reached into my jacket pocket, where I kept old receipts. I went through them: a wad of crumpled slips, every one with something written on the back. I found the one I was looking for. From the first day. Two weeks ago.

"Ashot sells. I make. Which one matters more?"

I looked at that line. Two weeks ago it had felt like a grudge. Ashot had stolen my customer, taken two bags for my rivet and given me one. I'd thought at the time: he's stealing from me.

And now I was sitting across from him, and I had a line I couldn't hold together, and he had an empty booth and a pair of hands that knew how to hold a line together.

"Ashot," I said. "What if you did the selling?"

---

Ashot looked at me the way he probably looks at a shoe somebody's brought in to be stitched that turns out to be made of cardboard.

"What?"

"Selling. Taking orders. Talking. You stand by the line. You talk to people. You write down who brought what. You tell them how much it'll be and when it'll be ready. You know how to do that. I don't. When I name a price, I start apologizing before I get to the end of the number. And two weeks ago you sold my rivet for two bags, and the woman went away happy."

"I am not a secretary," said Ashot. Slowly. With dignity. "I am a craftsman. Twenty years I've been a craftsman."

"And you'll still be a craftsman. Everything that's leather, rubber, thread: that's yours. Heel tips, soles, bags, belts. You do those, I stay out of it. Everything that's metal, I do. But you're the one who talks to people. For both of us. Because you're better at it. Way better. And meanwhile I get to keep my head down and do twice as much."

Ashot didn't say anything.

"And the people standing in my line," I went on, "can hand you their shoes while they're at it. Because you'll tell them, 'While you're waiting, let me put a heel tip on that for you.' They're standing there anyway. And you'll tell them. You know how."

Ashot was quiet even longer.

"And the money?" he asked at last.

That was the right question. I'd known he was going to ask it. And I didn't know what to answer.

---

We haggled for twenty minutes.

Honestly, it was the funniest thing I'd seen all week, and this past week I'd seen a cat put back a pencil and Old Pete blame a broken valve on the gasoline.

Ashot haggled with me the way he haggles with everybody. And I haggled with Ashot the way I haggle with everybody: badly.

"Half," said Ashot.

"Half of what?"

"Of everything you make while I'm taking orders."

"Ashot, that's highway robbery."

"It's not robbery. It's the market. Without me, your line gets into fights and you go hungry. With me, your line stands still and you work."

"A tenth," I said.

"You're joking."

"Two weeks ago you gave me one bag out of two. For my own rivet."

"Back then the customer was mine," said Ashot. "Now the customers are yours. That's different."

"A fifth."

"A third."

"A quarter," I said. "And whatever people from my line give you is all yours. Heel tips, soles, bags."

Ashot narrowed his eyes. I could see him calculating. He calculates faster than I do: not in a column, but all at once, the way musicians read music. Twelve people in my line. Each one waits twenty minutes or so. If one in three hands him a shoe while they're waiting…

"A quarter," said Ashot. "And you make me a hammer yourself. One that won't break. This one's had a crack in it since spring."

"Done," I said.

"And nails. Shoe nails. That don't bend."

"Done."

"And one more thing," said Ashot. "You'll tell people I'm the one who taught you."

I laughed.

"Taught me what?"

"Doesn't matter," Ashot said, perfectly serious. "People like it when an old master teaches a young one. It inspires trust. I taught you hardening. Twenty years ago, in the army. Got it?"

"Twenty years ago I was twelve."

"People don't know that," said Ashot. "People know I have a mustache."

Ashot did have a mustache. Thick, black, going gray. It really did inspire trust.

We shook on it.

---

Ashot went back to my line, and for the first time I saw how he works. Really saw it. Not from across the aisle, but up close.

He didn't stand in front of the line. He went into it. He walked up to the man with the knife and said:

"A knife? Tim'll have it done by lunch. Goes dull? You cut meat? Beef, pork? Pork, so the bone's soft, we'll make the edge a little thinner." He didn't know a thing about edges. He'd just heard me explaining it yesterday to a butcher from the next aisle over. But it sounded as if he'd done nothing for twenty years but harden knives. "That'll be four bags. No, it's not expensive. You won't have to sharpen this knife for ten years. What do you spend on whetstones now, a month? There, you see. I'm writing it down. Last name?"

The man with the knife gave his last name. Ashot wrote it on a slip of paper, folded the slip, tucked it under the handle of the knife and passed the knife to me over the counter. Without even looking. The way a scrub nurse hands over a scalpel.

Then he went over to the woman with the pot.

"The handle? Five minutes. Tim'll do it between knives. Half a bag. And what's this you've got?" He looked down at her feet. "Boots. The heel's worn down. Let me do it while you wait. Sit down on the crate, take them off. It's fine, it's not cold, I've got slippers."

The woman with the pot, who had come to get a handle fixed, was sitting on a crate five minutes later in Ashot's slippers while Ashot, in his booth, tapped a new heel tip onto her boot. The pot sat at my place. The woman looked perfectly happy.

Then he went over to the woman with the boot. The one who'd brought him her shoes for fifteen years and today had gotten in my line.

"Mrs. Petrie," he said. He knew her name. He knew everybody's name. "You're here for me."

The woman blushed.

"Ashot, I thought… people are saying Timothy makes heel tips that…"

"Timothy does iron," said Ashot. "Heel tips, I do. We're together now. He does iron, I do everything else. Give me the boot."

She gave him the boot. He went back to his booth. I watched him sit down on his stool, pick up his hammer and go to work. And his face was completely different from an hour before. Calm. The face of a man who has a boot in his hands again.

---

Then something happened that I hadn't expected at all.

Ashot was tapping a heel tip onto Mrs. Petrie's boot and suddenly stopped. He looked at her. Then across the aisle at me. Then at the heel tip again.

"Tim," he called. "Come here."

I went over. I had five minutes between knives.

"Look," said Ashot. He was holding a heel tip in his hand: rubber, black, with little nails around the rim. "A heel tip wears down. Rubber. You can't do rubber. I can do rubber. But rubber wears down anyway, however well you nail it. Make a good one, three months and it's gone. Make a bad one, a month."

"I know."

"And under the heel tip, what's there?"

I looked. Under the heel tip, in the heel itself, there was plastic, hard and gray, which the heel tip fit into.

"Plastic," I said.

"Plastic," Ashot agreed. "And what if it isn't plastic? What if it's iron?"

I didn't get it.

"What if there's," Ashot said slowly, "a little iron plate in there. Thin. Under the rubber. The rubber wears down to it, and then it stops wearing. The person's walking on iron. Click-clack. Like in the old days. My grandfather had it like that: metal taps on his boots. Only they put the taps on the outside, and they slipped. But what if it's inside? Rubber on top, iron inside. The rubber wears down, the iron holds. New rubber I can put on in five minutes. And the iron lasts forever."

I stared at him.

That was something I never would have come up with. Never. Because I didn't know how a heel is put together. I didn't know that rubber wears down to a certain point, and past that there's nothing left to wear down. I didn't know how heel tips go on or why they come off. I knew steel. Ashot knew heels.

But together we knew a forever heel tip.

"Can you do it?" Ashot asked. "A plate. Thin. Won't bend, won't break. Shaped to the heel. With holes for my nails."

"I can," I said. "If I've got something to make it out of."

"And I can put it on," said Ashot. And he smiled. For the first time that day. Under his mustache. "There's your forever heel tip, Tim. Not yours. Not mine. Ours."

---

I made the first plate at my place, with my back to the aisle and the soldering iron smelling of rosin, out of a broken-off piece of a steel ruler that Ashot dug out of his box. As thick as a coin, hard at the rim, like Greg's knife, and springy in the middle, so it wouldn't crack under the heel, with four holes for the nails. It cost me a hand pie and half a thermos.

Ashot took it, turned it over, tapped it with his fingernail and spent a long time looking at my soldering iron.

"Good technology," he said finally. "Imported. I'm not asking, Tim. See? I'm not asking."

Then he set it into Mrs. Petrie's heel, put the rubber on over it, checked it and tapped it against the counter.

"Clack," said the heel.

"There," said Ashot. "Music."

Mrs. Petrie walked off in her boots, clacking on the asphalt, looking like somebody who had just been handed a medal.

By lunchtime Ashot had a line.

Not like mine: a smaller one. But a line. People who were waiting in mine with their knives handed Ashot their shoes while they waited. People who'd heard about the "forever heel tip" went to him, and he'd say, "Yes, forever, it's our joint technology, Tim does the iron, I do everything else." And he charged twice as much for it as for a regular heel tip. And nobody argued, because it was "forever."

I made plates between knives. It was cheap. Ashot had enough scrap in his booth for a hundred pairs: his whole life he'd never thrown away old taps, nails, steel shanks, buckles. An old tap is practically a plate already: bend it to fit the heel, spread the metal apart for the holes, harden the rim. Half a thermos for three pairs. The nails and buckles I didn't touch: fusing little bits together is like gluing a cup back together out of crumbs. He had his own shelter, the same as I did. Only mine was for broken things, and his was for metal that would come in handy someday.

It came in handy.

---

After lunch, Mr. Abrams showed up.

He saw the line from a long way off, first at my booth, then at Ashot's, and came over with the look of a man who has found a wallet in the street and is already estimating what's inside.

"Timothy," he said. "Business is picking up, I see."

"It is," I said.

"Then we ought to revisit the rent. It's a good spot, plenty of people. Another twenty percent, starting the first. On top of the twenty already."

I opened my mouth to say something. Something that would probably have started with "I'm sorry."

But I didn't get the chance.

Ashot came out of his booth, wiping his hands on his apron, and stood next to me.

"Mr. Abrams," he said. "Hello. How's your health? How's your wife? I heard she's having trouble with her knee."

Mr. Abrams was taken aback.

"Her knee," he said. "Yes. How do you know?"

"The whole market knows," said Ashot. "Everybody's worried about her. Me too." He paused for exactly as long as he needed to. "Mr. Abrams, do you see this line?"

"I see it."

"Those are people. They came to the market. They walk past the meat, past the fish, past the bread. They buy things on the way. Yesterday Greg sold a third more meat, because people who were standing in Tim's line picked up some burger patties while they were at it. Roxanne sold more flowers. The meat-pie stand by the gate sold out by two o'clock." He paused. "This is your row, Mr. Abrams. You've got five booths here. When Tim has a line, all five get more customers. And when there are more customers, everybody pays you on time. But if you raise Tim's rent, and he leaves…" Ashot spread his hands. "Where will the people go?"

Mr. Abrams didn't say anything.

"And one more thing," Ashot said confidentially. "I heard your wife's cane broke. The one she walks with. The handle cracked. Tim can make her a new one. Light, strong. Lasts forever. As a favor between friends."

Mr. Abrams looked at Ashot. At me. At the line. Then at Ashot again.

"All right," he said. "The twenty starting the first, the way we agreed. No new increase for now. For now."

"Bring the cane on Wednesday," said Ashot. "It'll be ready by lunch."

Mr. Abrams left. I watched him go and couldn't believe it.

"Ashot," I said. "How do you know about the knee? And the cane?"

"I've been at this market longer than you," said Ashot. "I know everything. That's my job, Tim. Not just heels. People. You fix iron. I fix people. A little."

I thought he was right. And that this was something I couldn't do at all. And that it was probably worth no less than the thimble.

---

That evening we sat on the crates behind my booth and counted.

Ashot pulled two plastic shot glasses and a bottle of something dark out from under his counter. He poured. We clinked. It was something homemade, from the old country, and it hit like a rabbit punch.

I counted my take. Forty-four bags. Minus a quarter for Ashot: thirty-three. Yesterday I'd made twenty-two and given up by lunch. Today, twice that, and I'd lasted till evening and was still on my feet. Even though Ashot had taken a quarter.

At first I didn't see how that was possible. Then I did. Yesterday I'd been pulled away twenty times: by the line, by arguments, by "so when will it be ready?" Today, not once. I worked. Just worked. One thing after another. Ashot passed me a knife with a slip of paper over the counter, I did it and passed it back, he gave it to the customer, took the money and said, "Thank you, come again." I didn't even see the people. All I saw was steel.

And at eleven, when my hands started shaking, Ashot went off to the meat-pie stand without a word and came back with a full kettle and a bag of fried meat pies. "You'll look at their vent hood on Thursday," he said. "I worked it out. Boiling water till closing is on them." I ate six fried meat pies and drank three thermoses. Ashot kept count of how much I ate, and for the first time, I think, he understood why I'm always so skinny.

And Ashot counted his take. Eight bags for his own work: heel tips, soles, forever heel tips at twice the price. The plates, by the way, I'd been giving him for free. I realized that on the second shot. Ashot had realized it that morning. Plus a quarter of mine: eleven. Nineteen. Against one and a half yesterday. Against six on a good day.

He looked at that number for a long time. Then he poured another.

"Nineteen," he said. "Tim, in twenty years I've never once made nineteen."

"And in twelve years I've never once made thirty-three," I said. "Even though I gave you a quarter."

We were quiet for a while.

"How is that?" asked Ashot. "You gave to me. And you've got more. I took from you. And I've got more too. That's not how it works."

I looked at the glass in my hand.

"It is," I said. "If each one does what he's better at."

I said it and was surprised that I understood it. I'd been thinking about this for two weeks. Ever since that receipt. And now, sitting on a crate with a glass of Ashot's homemade stuff in my hand, I suddenly understood.

It wasn't about which one matters more. It was about having different hands. I started to think one more thing (and what if I could do heels too?), but I didn't get to finish the thought: Ashot poured the third.

I took out the old receipt. The one from the first day. "Ashot sells. I make. Which one matters more?" I thought about it. And underneath, below the old question, I added:

"Neither. Together we make more."

Ashot looked over my shoulder and read it.

"Is that about me?" he asked.

"About us."

"Let me see," said Ashot.

I showed him. He spent a long time looking at the two lines, the old one and the new one. Then he said:

"'Ashot sells.' That was you being sore at me?"

"It was."

"And now?"

"Now I've figured out that selling is work too. And that you do it better than anybody at this market."

Ashot nodded. He folded the receipt carefully and tucked it into my shirt pocket.

"Hang on to that," he said. "Show it to Arsen when he's a doctor. So he knows his father didn't just put heel tips on shoes."

---

As I was leaving, Ashot called after me:

"Tim! Don't forget the hammer! The one that won't break!"

"Tomorrow!"

"And the nails!"

"Tomorrow!"

"And Mr. Abrams's cane!"

"Ashot, you've already lined up a week's worth of work for me!"

"See?" Ashot said, pleased. "I find the work. You make it. Together we make more."

He'd remembered it. After one reading.

I walked home thinking that today something very important had happened, I was pretty sure. Not the thimble. Not the hardening. Not the forever heel tip. It was that for the first time in my life I'd worked with somebody instead of alone. And that turned out to be every bit as good as a miracle.

Lugnut was waiting for me at home. He was sitting on the windowsill, looking at me with suspicion, as if he could smell Ashot's bottle on me.

"I'm in business with somebody now," I told him. "I've got a partner."

Lugnut thought it over. Then he jumped down off the windowsill and went into the kitchen, to his bowl.

It seemed to me he understood. He had a partnership of his own, with Mrs. Mazur, who slipped him bits of burger whenever I forgot to feed him. He'd known how this works for a long time.

I'd only just now caught up with the cat.
