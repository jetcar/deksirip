# Chapter 7. Make It, Don't Buy It

On Monday I opened my notebook to the last page, where the list of broken things was, and looked at it for a long time.

Thirty-two items. Ninety-one bags.

A week ago I'd written that list at four in the morning, after my first night with the thimble, and back then it had felt like a revelation. Ninety-one bags I wasn't going to spend. Almost like finding a wallet on the street, except the wallet had been in my own apartment all along, spread around the place in the form of broken things.

Now, after Stern's notebooks, after the copper sulfate and the watch, I looked at the list with different eyes. And I saw something in it I hadn't seen then.

Back then I'd thought: I can do all of this. Now I thought: which of these is actually worth doing?

Those are two different questions. I'd come up with "the difference" that very first night (the faucet in the plus column, the spoons in the minus), but back then I'd only counted it in pierogi. Now it turned out every item had not two numbers but three: what it costs to buy, what it costs to make, and the hour I'm not at the market.

I picked up a pen and wrote a second number next to each item. My own price. In pierogi, in hours, in hand pies. It was hard, because I'd never counted my own time. My time had always been free, like air. There'd been plenty of it and not much money.

And then it hit me that my time wasn't free at all. I was fifty-nine bags short for Friday. Every day at the market was eight bags, if I was lucky. And every hour I spent at home fixing my own fridge was an hour I wasn't fixing somebody else's umbrella.

I didn't like that discovery. It meant that even at home, on the couch, I was sort of at work. Just without the pay.

I wrote: "Time is money." Looked at it. Crossed it out. I was sick of pretty formulas; they kept lying to me. I wrote it a different way: "An hour at home is an hour I'm not at the market. Fix things for myself only if they'll pay for themselves."

And I started crossing things off.

---

The first thing I crossed off was the vacuum cleaner. I'd been promising myself I'd fix that vacuum for three years, and in three years I'd never once missed it. I sweep with a broom. Brooms don't break. If something's been lying around broken for three years and you don't miss it, you don't need it. That came as a surprise: part of my list turned out not to be repairs at all, just things I'd bought once and forgotten to throw away.

Then I crossed off the TV. It hadn't worked since spring, and I hadn't missed it either.

Then the tape deck that had been my father's. That one I didn't cross off. I moved it to a line of its own, with no number. The tape deck needed fixing, but not because fixing it would pay. It just did.

Then about a dozen small things along the same lines: the second cheese grater, a hair dryer I never turned on anyway, a desk lamp, when I have a perfectly good ceiling light… The faucet and the doorknob I'd already crossed off that first night.

Of thirty-two items, nineteen were left. Of ninety-one bags, sixty. Thirty-one bags had evaporated, just like that, from one question: "Do I actually need this?" I looked at those thirty-one bags and realized they'd never been real. That was money I'd never have spent anyway. I'd just have carried it around in my head like a rock, along with the thought "I really ought to fix that."

I wrote: "Not everything broken needs fixing. Some broken things you just need to throw out of your head."

---

After that came the real items, and here I had to think differently.

The fridge. It hummed so loud that guests (if I ever had guests) would have asked where I kept the power substation. It hummed day and night, and I'd long suspected it ate more electricity than I did. A new fridge: a hundred bags. A secondhand one: forty, but secondhand is always a lottery.

I pulled the fridge away from the wall, lay down on the floor and put my right hand on the black, hot housing of the compressor, on a spot where the metal ran solid all the way to the motor. Then I put on the thimble.

The fridge turned out to be more complicated than anything I'd touched so far. It wasn't one thing; it was a lot of things joined together. The compressor: heavy, iron, with copper windings inside and a little piston going back and forth. Copper tubing: thin, coiled like a snake, with something flowing through it, gas or liquid, I couldn't tell. A thermostat with a tiny spring. A fan. Plastic, plastic and more plastic, which I still didn't understand.

And I realized I couldn't "fix the fridge." Not the whole thing at once. It would be like asking a doctor to "cure a person" without telling him what hurts.

Professor Stern had written about this in the ninth volume. "A complex thing is not one thing. A complex thing is many simple things that have come to terms. To repair the complex, you must first take it apart in your mind into the simple. Part by part. Otherwise the object will take as much as it's given and do whatever it sees fit."

I took the fridge apart in my mind. Part by part. It took maybe half an hour: I just sat on a stool with my hand on the warm housing and listened. The way a doctor listens to your lungs.

And I found it.

It wasn't the compressor that was humming. It was the bearings in its motor, the little steel rings the shaft turned in. Twenty years had worn them down, and the shaft rattled around inside them like a key in a worn-out lock. The motor shook, so the whole compressor shook, and the whole fridge with it, and I'd been listening to that hum every night for the last three years.

The bearings were steel. Steel I understood.

I closed my eyes and held one precise picture in my head. Not "make it stop humming": I'd already learned that lesson on the faucet. This instead: a ring, a shaft inside the ring, and between them a gap just so, thin as a hair and even all the way around. I held that picture the way you hold a hot cup in your hands, firmly and carefully. And I asked the steel to become that. There wasn't enough steel: for twenty years it had been wearing away into the oil. I took what I needed from the inside of the thick housing, from a spot where nobody would ever miss a sliver, the way I'd once borrowed brass for the doorknob.

The steel obliged.

My hand on the side of the fridge felt the vibration draining away. Not all at once: it was as if somebody were slowly turning down the volume. The hum got lower. Then quieter. Then it turned into an even purr, and then even the purr was gone. Only a faint rustle was left, somewhere inside, where whatever was supposed to flow through the tubing was flowing.

I opened my eyes. I was hungry, but not very. The electric kettle on the floor next to me, which I'd kept under my left hand, had cooled down to warm. I could do that now too. Not out of myself; out of the kettle. And not with extra to spare, but exactly as much as it took, because I'd been holding a precise picture in my head. I was starting to get the hang of the valve Professor Stern had written about.

I sat on the stool in front of the fridge and listened to the silence. It was the most beautiful silence of my life.

Lugnut came in from the other room, sat down next to me and stared at the fridge too. I don't think he understood what had happened either. He'd lived with that hum his whole cat life and probably thought it was just what an apartment sounded like.

---

That evening I did something new: I went out on the landing and spent half an hour staring at my electric meter. The little wheel inside crept along so slowly that at first I tapped on the glass. That morning, before I started on the fridge, I'd jotted down the numbers, just in case. Now I did the math.

The fridge had been eating half my electricity.

Half. Before, it had never shut off: the worn bearings dragged on the motor, and the compressor ran nonstop. Now it clicked its relay and stayed quiet for forty minutes at a stretch. One fridge, humming away in my kitchen, had been eating half of everything I paid for electricity. Every month. For years on the landlady's bill, and then three years on mine. And nobody had noticed, because a hum is just a hum and a bill is just a bill, and nobody had ever drawn a line between them.

I worked it out. Half the bill came to a little over a bag a month. Now the fridge ate a third as much. The savings: almost a bag a month, ten a year. A new fridge cost a hundred. So in about ten years, that humming fridge had eaten a whole new fridge. An entire fridge, gone into the hum. Then it occurred to me that it hadn't hummed from day one, only once the bearings wore out, so it came to somewhat less. It still stung. Especially since the fridge was the landlady's and the electricity was mine.

And that's when I understood a difference I'd never thought about before. Some repairs save you money once. Like the doorknob: I didn't buy a new one, I saved two bags, and that's it. And some repairs save you money every month, forever, for as long as the thing lasts. The fridge was that kind. Not two bags once, but almost a bag every month, for life.

I wrote that in my notebook and underlined it twice: "Some savings happen once. Some happen every month. The second kind matters more. Look for what eats every month."

---

After the fridge, I knew what I was looking for. I walked around the apartment looking not at what was broken but at what was eating.

The faucet: it had been eating water. I'd already fixed it. One more small but permanent savings.

The windows. Old wooden frames, dried out and shrunken, with gaps that let in enough of a draft in winter to stir the curtains. Heat in our building is billed by the square foot, not by how much you use, so the draft wasn't eating my money directly; it was eating my warmth. And when it's cold, I turn on the space heater. And the space heater eats electricity.

There was nothing I could do about the frames: wood. I'd spent two evenings in a row reading *The Properties of Wood* and figured out that wood was put together even more intricately than I'd thought, which meant it would most likely turn to charcoal in my hands, like the chair leg. But the gaps didn't have to be closed with wood. In the shelter I had a coil of old aluminum wire. I drew it out into a thin strip, smooth as foil, and laid it along the gaps, pressed tight against the frame. Aluminum I understood. It sat snug and didn't budge. The curtains stopped stirring too.

My boots. The winter ones that let in water and were number seventeen on the list (I'd remembered that, it turned out). The sole had worn through, and under the sole, beneath the arch, between the heel and the ball of the foot, there was a steel shank plate, and it had snapped. The sole I couldn't do: rubber, the same spaghetti as plastic. But the shank I mended. And I took the sole to Ashot.

"Can you put a new sole on this?" I asked.

Ashot took the boot, turned it this way and that and looked inside.

"You put a new shank in?" he asked.

"I mended the old one."

"Mended it with what?"

"New technology."

"Imported," said Ashot, deadpan. "I've got imported technology too. It's called 'doing it myself.'" He paused. "Half a bag."

"Ashot. Half a bag? From me? Your neighbor?"

"And you charged me a whole one for a rivet yesterday."

"That was last week."

"I remember everything," said Ashot, and kept the boot.

I went back to my booth and thought this over. Ashot would do that sole in an hour. It would take me a week, even with the thimble, and I'd eat my way through every bag of pierogi in Granton. He's been doing it for twenty years, and he does it well. And if I give him half a bag, I get back a whole boot.

In other words, buying a sole from Ashot isn't spending. It's saving too. Just a different kind.

It was a very strange thought, and I didn't know where to write it down. My notebook said "Don't buy what you can make." But here it came out: buy it, because making it costs more. So the rule didn't always work. It only worked for things I understood. And for everything else, somebody else understood them better than I did, and it paid to pay him.

On a receipt I wrote: "Ashot: soles. Why isn't this spending?" And I couldn't come up with an answer.

---

On Tuesday I didn't go to the market.

It was a risky decision: after Monday I was still fifty-one bags short for Friday, and every day counted. But there was something I needed to test. And the only place to test it was the trash.

The trash corner behind our building isn't just a row of dumpsters. It's an institution. Next to the dumpsters, people leave things that are too good to throw out but that they don't need anymore: old chairs, nightstands, boxes of dishes, broken bicycles, baby strollers. It's the unwritten rule of every old neighborhood: if you leave something beside the dumpster instead of in it, you're telling people they can take it. Things live there for a day or two, and then they either disappear into somebody's apartment or the garbage truck hauls them away.

I went down to the dumpsters at nine in the morning. Old Pete was already there. He was digging through a box of old magazines with the expression of a man searching for evidence.

"Good morning," I said.

"I know," said Old Pete, and carried the magazines off.

By the dumpsters stood: an old iron bed frame, no mattress, with a wire-link spring and a headboard and footboard made of bent tubing. A washing machine, its motor pulled out and left on the ground beside it. Three chairs, two of them missing their seats. A kid's bike with a bent wheel. A rusty frying pan. A box of assorted pots. Somebody's old floor lamp.

Before, I'd have looked at all that and thought: trash. Now I looked and saw something else. I saw steel, aluminum, copper, cast iron. Pounds and pounds of metal that somebody, at some point, had dug out of the ground, smelted, rolled and bent into shape, and now all of it was sitting by a dumpster waiting for the garbage truck.

A thing doesn't stop being useful when it breaks. It stops being useful to its owner. Those aren't the same. Trash is just things that have run out of owners, not out of use.

I wrote that down on a receipt, right there by the dumpster, using my knee for a desk. Old Pete, coming back for a second box of magazines, saw me writing and said, "I know." I think he said it approvingly.

---

All day Tuesday I hauled the trash up to my apartment.

I took the bed apart on the spot. That part was easy: nuts and bolts and a half-inch wrench. The headboard and footboard were bent steel tubing, good thick-walled stuff, the old kind, built to outlast the people sleeping on it. The spring was hundreds of feet of steel wire, linked ring through ring.

At home, I made a chair out of the headboard and footboard.

It was the first thing I'd ever not "fixed" but actually made. From scratch. Out of nothing; or rather, out of whatever was at hand. I held a picture in my head: a chair, four legs, this high, a seat like this, a back like so. Not a blurry picture, but a precise one, like a blueprint. I even drew it on graph paper, square by square, the way they'd taught us in drafting class. And then slowly, tube by tube, I bent, joined and welded. Bending was cheap. Welding was expensive. I tried to weld as little as possible, and wherever I could, I bent the tubes and slid one inside another, like a kid's construction set, so the thing would hold itself together. Six welds. A bag and a half and two kettles. A chair in a store costs three. It paid for itself, just barely.

The seat I wove out of the wire from the bedspring. Not with magic: by hand. The way you weave a basket. That turned out to be a lot cheaper than welding every little wire, and a lot better looking.

By lunchtime I had a chair. Steel, heavy, a little crooked, but solid as a rock. I sat down on it. It didn't wobble. The first chair in my apartment in three years that didn't wobble.

I looked at the old chair on three legs, the one I'd been wedging under the doorknob. I looked at the new one. I thought.

And then I remembered the door.

The door wouldn't shut because the building had settled and the doorframe had gone out of square. Wood. The frame I couldn't fix. But the door hung on steel hinges, and hinges are steel. On the bottom hinge I grew a washer out of the hinge's own thick leaf, and the door rose just under half an inch, which was exactly how far the building had settled. It stopped rubbing. Then I shifted the hole in the strike plate over a little: steel, easy, a second's work.

The door shut. With a quiet click, the way doors do for normal people.

I ceremonially carried the old three-legged chair down to the dumpsters. An hour later somebody had taken it. Probably somebody who understands wood.

---

By Tuesday evening, this is what I'd done. The chair. A bookshelf, out of the bed frame's angle rails and a steel panel off the washing machine: for the first time I had a shelf to put the library books on, instead of keeping them on a stool. At first I'd wanted to make myself a new frying pan, too, out of the rusty one from the trash: ask the iron to become iron and the oxygen to fly away. I started, felt the thimble pulling (like two welds at once) and stopped a third of the way in. The rust was holding on to the iron harder than I was holding on to my rules. I took the rusty pan back down to the dumpsters and just welded the handle back onto my own. One weld. Half a bag. You can't see your face in it, but you can fry in it. I straightened out the kid's bike and thought I'd give it to Mikey. No: Mikey's fifteen, he'd be insulted. I'll give it to somebody younger.

The knives on the list I didn't replace; I resharpened them. The steel in them was bad, but there was plenty of it, so I just reworked the edges thinner and straighter. Practically free. And out of an old saw blade I'd found in the box of pots, I made one new knife: big, all one piece, handle and all. That was good steel, tool steel, hard. I could feel the carbon sitting in it, packed in tight, like raisins in a good fruitcake. The knives came out so sharp they'd cut paper if you just laid the blade on it. I hung them on a strip I'd glued magnets into, out of two dead speakers from the shelter.

I sat down on my new chair in the middle of my new apartment and ran the numbers.

Here's how it came out.

The apartment: rent, same as before. Rent's the one thing you can't make yourself. My landlady doesn't take payment in chairs. Electricity: down a third, since the fridge now ate a third as much. Then add the kettle: I was boiling it five times an evening now so I wouldn't have to eat. The kettle ate up about half of what the fridge saved. But a liter of boiling water is a tenth of a bag, and a chair out of a bed frame is two kettles and still a bag and a half on top. The kettle is good for small stuff. It can't carry anything big. Water: a little less; that was the faucet. Phone: same. Bus fare: for the first time it occurred to me that I could fix myself up a bike, also from the trash, and ride it to the market, and the bus fare would disappear altogether. Clothes: the boots were already at Ashot's, and there was also my winter jacket with the metal zipper, which I'd fixed today too.

I added up everything that used to go out every month, not counting rent, the debt and food: electricity, water, bus fare, and the eternal "something broke." And I added up the same things for now: honestly, without the bike, which didn't exist yet.

It came out sixty percent less. More than cut in half.

And then I added up food separately. And at first I thought I'd made a mistake.

Food had doubled. Half of what I'd saved was going into pierogi, fried meat pies and bread. Pierogi, fried meat pies, bread, and Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, which she'd brought me almost every evening that week in exchange for small repairs, and it still wasn't enough. I'd turned into a stove that ran on pierogi. The more I did, the more I ate. And that was something you couldn't fix, because you can't reshape food with a thimble. I had the atoms for pierogi right at hand, as it happened: carbon in old newspapers, hydrogen and oxygen in water, nitrogen in the air. But putting protein together out of them is not like welding a chair. Dough and meat I understood even less than wood. And even if I could do it, a burger made that way would cost more in pierogi than it gave back in calories.

I wrote: "Everything except food: down sixty percent. Food: doubled. My biggest expense now is me."

I looked at it. I laughed. Then I stopped.

Because here's what it meant. Everything I did for myself, I paid for in food. And food I bought with money. And money I earned at the market. And at the market I spent energy too, which meant I ate there too. It made a circle. Not a vicious circle. A normal one. The same as everybody's. Only for everybody else it's called "work, paycheck, food, work," and mine was called "magic, pierogi, magic."

And I couldn't see a way out of that circle. Unless somebody else cooked for me. But who, and why would they, and what would I pay them with?

I thought about Mrs. Mazur.

All week Mrs. Mazur had been bringing me hand pies. And I'd been fixing something for her: her iron, her faucet, a cabinet door, her glasses. We never kept track of who owed whom how much. She'd say "Nobody values what's free" and bring the pies. I'd say "Thank you" and fix things.

What if I did keep track?

I wrote: "Mrs. Mazur. Hand pies. Repairs. Figure out how much I owe her. Or she owes me. Or nobody owes anybody?"

And then I realized I didn't know how to count it. Hand pies and repairs are different things. They have no common measure. Except pierogi, and Mrs. Mazur doesn't measure things in pierogi.

---

That evening my mom called.

That was unexpected, because it was Tuesday, and Mom calls on Sundays. I got scared; I thought something had happened. But Mom was calling on video. On the screen I saw her face, very close and from below, the way it looks when a person is holding the phone at stomach level and can't figure out where to look.

"Tim, sweetheart!" said Mom. "Wanda put this app on my phone so we can see each other! Can I see you? Can you see me?"

"I see you, Mom. Just hold the phone up higher."

Mom lifted the phone. Now I could see her face, Aunt Wanda over her shoulder and a corner of a country kitchen with a big old wood stove.

"Show me how you live," said Mom. "I haven't seen your place once since you moved in. Just that one picture, with the wobbly chair and the pot on the windowsill."

I turned the phone slowly and showed her the apartment. The chair made out of a bed. The shelf with books on it. The frying pan with its welded-on handle. The knives on the magnetic strip. The door, shut. The fridge, silent.

Mom didn't say anything for a long time.

"That's your apartment?" she asked at last.

"It's mine."

"But you had that wobbly chair. So who did all this?"

"I did."

Mom looked at me through the screen. Then at Aunt Wanda. Aunt Wanda whispered something in her ear.

"Wanda says," said Mom, "that this is what happens when a man falls in love. He starts feathering the nest."

"Mom, I'm not in love. I just… started fixing my own stuff."

"Twelve years fixing everybody else's," said Mom, "and finally you've started on your own."

She said it just like that, in passing. And I sat there thinking that it was probably the most accurate thing anybody had ever said about me.

"Why is your chair made of iron?" Mom asked.

"Because I don't understand wood," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing, Mom. It's a long story."

"Are you eating?"

"A whole lot, Mom."

"Well, at least there's that," said Mom, relieved, and Aunt Wanda nodded along behind her shoulder.

---

After the call I sat on my iron chair and thought about Wednesday.

Tomorrow at six, Val would be waiting for me. The woman with a pencil in her hair. With a fresh copper sulfate solution, her own beaker and her own camera. And I didn't know whether I'd go.

I was scared. Not the way I'd been scared at Lenny's, in the garages behind the market. Differently. Back then I'd been afraid of getting caught. Now I was afraid of being seen. Really seen: on camera, on the record, in a lab log. After that there'd be no going back. Professor Stern, in thirty years, had shown it to no one but his wife and Glen. Glen, by the look of things, isn't showing it to anybody. And I was about to show the same thing a second time, only now on camera, on the record, to a person I'd met exactly once in my life.

On the other hand, I'd written it down myself on Sunday: "be afraid, and do it anyway. But do it where people can see."

Where people can see. That's exactly what showing it meant.

I looked around my apartment. At the chair, the shelf, the knives, the frying pan, the quiet fridge. At the list of broken things, which had gone from thirty-two items to nine, and first among those nine was the washing machine that won't spin. The motor it needed was lying down by the dumpster. Somebody else's. And I thought that this was knowledge too, the kind you won't find in any book. How to make a chair out of a bed. How to find what eats every month. How to see that buying a sole from Ashot is saving too.

Professor Stern knew everything there was to know about the thimble. But he didn't know how to make a chair out of a bed, because he'd never tried. He didn't need to. He didn't need to count pierogi.

I did.

Maybe that was the whole point. Maybe knowledge without need is like gold without trust. Pretty, heavy, and it just sits there. But once there's a need, knowledge suddenly starts to work.

Lugnut came over and sat down next to me. He looked at the knives on the magnetic strip. The knives gleamed.

"Don't even think about it," I said.

Lugnut turned away. But I could see he'd made a note of where they hung.

And then I went to bed on my old couch (I hadn't touched the couch; the couch was soft and good and didn't need fixing), and lay there a long time with my eyes open, listening to the fridge's silence.

Tomorrow was Wednesday.

I still hadn't decided whether I'd go.
