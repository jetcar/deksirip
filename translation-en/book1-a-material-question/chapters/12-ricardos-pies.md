# Chapter 12. Ricardo's Pies

On Wednesday evening Val came over with a notebook, a thermos and the look of a person who's about to teach.

Our deal went like this: two hours of me fixing the department's instruments, one hour of her teaching me chemistry. But on Wednesday I came home from the market so worn out you could have folded me up like an umbrella. That morning Mr. Abrams had brought in his wife's cane with the cracked handle. I kept the handle: a hand gets used to a handle. The cane itself I made new, out of an old ski pole that had been standing out on my balcony behind the skis. Aluminum is light but soft, so I slipped a springy strip cut from one of Ashot's steel shanks down inside it. I didn't fuse the two: aluminum and steel don't get along. I just squeezed the tube tight around the insert. It cost me three fried meat pies and a thermos. After that came Ashot's hammer, then forty nails that don't bend, then the usual line. Ashot took the orders, haggled, wrote things down. I made things. By evening I was so tired that I called Val and told her I wasn't going to make it to the department.

"Then the department will come to you," said Val. "Chemistry in a thermos."

She showed up at eight. We sat down in the kitchen. She opened her notebook to a page with big letters across the top: "POLYMERS. Why plastic isn't metal."

"Tonight, spaghetti," she said. "You've been complaining for two weeks that you don't understand plastic. So we're going to understand it."

That was when somebody knocked on the door.

---

I knew that knock. Only one person in our building knocks like that, as if the door owes her money.

I opened up. Mrs. Mazur was standing on the landing. She had a pot covered with a dish towel, and she was in her bathrobe, a head scarf and slippers. She looked past me into the kitchen. At Val. At the notebook. At the thermos. At Val again.

"Timothy," said Mrs. Mazur. "You have company."

"This is Val. She… we're doing chemistry."

"Chemistry," Mrs. Mazur repeated, in the tone of a woman who had been alive for seventy-one years and knew all about chemistry in a kitchen at eight o'clock at night. She handed me the pot. "Cabbage. And egg. I baked enough for two. I had a feeling."

"You couldn't have known."

"I heard you two talking on the stairs," Mrs. Mazur said simply. "These walls are like paper."

Val got up from the table, came over and held out her hand.

"Valerie," she said. "Val's fine."

Mrs. Mazur looked at the hand. Then at Val. Then at the purple stains on her fingers.

"What's that you've got there?" she asked.

"Gentian violet," said Val. "It's an indicator. It'll wash off by the time I retire."

"A chemist," Mrs. Mazur said with respect. "I worked thirty years in the cafeteria at the plant. The chemists always came in for lunch with hands like that. And they always went back for seconds. Eat, eat. Timothy won't eat on his own. Somebody has to feed him."

"I eat," I said.

"He eats pierogi," Mrs. Mazur told Val, as if I weren't there. "Frozen pierogi aren't food. They're despair."

Val laughed. Mrs. Mazur gave her a careful look, then nodded her approval, the way cooks nod when the dough has risen just right, and said:

"I'll sit for a bit. My radiator's barely warm again anyway."

I turned red. Her radiator was almost certainly fine. Still, I didn't argue.

---

There were no polymers that evening.

The three of us sat around my table eating hand pies, and Val asked how my week had gone. I told her. About the line. About Ashot. About the quarter. About how yesterday Ashot and I had sat on the crates counting money, and each of us had come out with more, even though each of us had given something to the other.

"And then I thought," I said, "what if I could do heels too? Then I wouldn't need Ashot at all. I'd make the iron and the rubber both. And keep everything."

"You can't do rubber," said Val.

"What if I could?"

Val thought about it. She put her half-eaten hand pie down on her plate.

"Okay," she said. "Let's not take rubber. Let's take something you can definitely do. No magic involved."

"Like what?"

Val looked at the pot. At the hand pies. At Mrs. Mazur.

"Pies," she said.

---

I should explain something here.

I know how to bake hand pies. That's unexpected, I know. But in my third year of college my father got sick, Mom took a second job, and I became the cook. Two years of cooking for three, plus whatever we brought to the hospital. Hand pies, because you can bake a batch that lasts two days. I baked them with cabbage, with potato, with egg and green onion. And to be honest, I was good at it. Mom used to say I had my grandmother's hands. Grandma had been a cook at a day care, and she could shape forty hand pies in the time it took you to blink.

I stopped baking when my father died and Mom moved in with Aunt Wanda. Baking for one means two hours of work for a dinner you eat in ten minutes. Pierogi are easier. But my hands remembered.

"Timothy can bake?" Mrs. Mazur asked suspiciously.

"I can," I said.

"I don't believe it."

"Let's test it," said Val. Her eyes had the same fire in them as the day she'd cracked my copper sulfate secret. "Mrs. Mazur, do you have any dough at home?"

"I always have dough," Mrs. Mazur said with dignity. "I don't live on frozen pierogi."

---

Twenty minutes later we were up at Mrs. Mazur's place on the fifth floor.

It was a contest. Val called it exactly that: "a contest." She set Mrs. Mazur's kitchen timer on the table, divided the dough and the filling into two equal piles and said, "One hour. Most pies wins. I'll be checking quality." Then she sat down off to one side with her notebook and pencil.

Mrs. Mazur rolled up the sleeves of her bathrobe. I took off the thimble and was about to put it in my pocket.

"On the windowsill," said Val. "Where I can see it."

I put it on the windowsill. Which was fair. No thimble. Just hands.

"Go," said Val, and started the timer.

Mrs. Mazur shaped hand pies the way she'd been shaping them for fifty years: fast, even, without looking, talking to Val the whole time about how young people nowadays don't eat anything. I shaped them the way I had ten years ago: also fast, also even, but in silence and in a sweat. My hands were remembering. Slowly at first, then faster.

"You crimp like a seamstress," Mrs. Mazur said without turning her head. "Too fine. It's a hand pie, not a dress."

"Twenty-seven minutes in," said Val. "The test subject is on number six. The test subject is sweating."

Around the eighth pie I caught my fingers reaching for the windowsill on their own, to ask the dough to be a little more cooperative. I pulled my hand back. I understood dough even worse than I understood wood, and if it had listened to me, Mrs. Mazur would have spent a week chipping charcoal off her sheet pan.

"Dough's a polymer too," said Val, without looking up from her notebook. "Gluten. The same spaghetti, only edible."

"You feel dough," said Mrs. Mazur. "You don't spaghetti it."

At the forty-minute mark Mrs. Mazur suddenly winced and rubbed the fingers of her right hand.

"What is it?" asked Val.

"Nothing," said Mrs. Mazur. "My joints. They always act up by evening. Don't mind me."

She kept going. But slower.

The timer rang. Val counted. Mrs. Mazur slid both sheet pans into the oven and announced that quality would be judged by her, not by the timer.

Mrs. Mazur had eleven hand pies.

I had fourteen.

Mrs. Mazur looked at my fourteen as if I'd just taken her own dough and made a knife out of it right in front of her. Then she picked one up, broke it open and studied the filling, the crimp, the thickness of the dough. She tasted it.

"Good," she said grudgingly. "Somebody's grandmother's hands. You can see it right away."

I was beaming. I don't think I'd ever been that proud of a single repair in my whole life.

"Great," said Val. "So Timothy bakes better than Mrs. Mazur."

"Faster," Mrs. Mazur corrected her, stung. "Not better. Faster."

"Fine. Faster," Val agreed. "And Timothy's faster at fixing things too, right? Mrs. Mazur, can you fix an iron?"

"I can," said Mrs. Mazur. "I take it to Timothy."

"And yourself?"

Mrs. Mazur thought about it.

"I could change a fuse. If somebody showed me. In an hour."

"Okay," said Val, and wrote something down. "So Timothy bakes faster and fixes faster. He's better at everything. Right?"

"Right," I said.

"Then here's my question," said Val. "Who should be baking the pies?"

---

I said, "Me." It was obvious. I bake faster, so I should be the one baking.

Mrs. Mazur didn't say anything, but the look she gave me told me I'd just said something very stupid.

Val smiled. Not in a mean way: the way teachers smile when a student is about to spot his own mistake, if you give him a minute.

"Tim, how much did you make today?"

"Thirty-something. That's after Ashot's quarter."

"In how many hours?"

"Eight to six. Minus the cane and the nails."

"Call it eight. So an hour at the booth is worth about four bags. Now picture this: tomorrow at lunch you close the booth for an hour and go bake yourself some hand pies. Fourteen of them. What would they cost at the bakery?"

"About a bag and a half."

"So to get a bag and a half's worth of hand pies, you give up four bags' worth of repairs. Every one of those pies costs you almost a third of a bag. The most expensive hand pies in Granton."

I didn't say anything.

"Now Mrs. Mazur. In an hour she can make eleven hand pies. Or change one fuse. What do you charge for a fuse?"

"Half a bag."

"So each of her hand pies costs her less than a twentieth of a bag. Yours cost you a third. You're the faster baker, but her pie costs her about a sixth of what yours costs you."

"I wouldn't be changing any fuses anyway," Mrs. Mazur snorted. "My show's on. I'm on my third time through it."

"All the more reason," said Val.

She set her pencil down on the table.

"It's called 'comparative advantage,'" she said. "An economist named Ricardo came up with it two hundred years ago, using wine and cloth as his example. We had to take economics sophomore year. I passed and forgot everything except this, because it was the only thing in the whole course that looked like a law of nature instead of an opinion. It doesn't matter who's better at everything. What matters is who it costs less. You fix, she bakes, you trade, and you both end up with more than if each of you did everything yourselves."

"Ricardo's pies," I said.

"Ricardo's pies," Val agreed.

Mrs. Mazur looked at the two of us over her glasses.

"I knew that without your Ricardo," she said. "I've been living like that for thirty years. I bake, Timothy fixes. I bake, Pete Palmer checks my electric bills; he was an accountant before he started knowing everything. I bake, Mikey sets up my phone. I bake, Judy from the third floor takes my blood pressure; she's a nurse. The whole building lives like that. We just didn't know it was called Ricardo."

---

We sat in Mrs. Mazur's kitchen. It smelled of hand pies, twenty-five of them at once. Val was on her fifth. I was on my eighth: one of my own, the most lopsided one, but good.

And here's what I was thinking.

Mrs. Mazur had just said the most important thing, and Val had just given it a name. The whole building lives like that. Pies for a pipe. Pies for a phone. Pies for blood pressure. Nobody pays money. Everybody trades. And everybody comes out ahead.

Only here's what was strange. If that was true, why was there so little of it? Why had I spent twelve years fixing people's things for money and only started trading with Mrs. Mazur two weeks ago? Why was Old Pete checking other people's bills while the riser in his own bathroom had nearly rotted through, and nobody had told him until I showed up "from property management"? Why had I been saying hello to my neighbors for three years without knowing what any of them could do?

"Because nobody knows who can do what," said Val, when I asked it out loud. "You know about Mrs. Mazur. Mrs. Mazur knows about you. Does Judy from the third floor know about you?"

"I don't know. Probably not."

"There you go. So if Judy's iron breaks, she'll go to the store and buy a new one. And a neighbor could take your blood pressure, but instead you'll go to the clinic and sit in the waiting room for three hours."

"I don't need my blood pressure taken."

"You do," said Mrs. Mazur. "The way you eat."

"And another thing," said Val. "You two trade hand pies for an iron. How do you keep track of who owes whom how much?"

Mrs. Mazur and I looked at each other.

"We don't," I said.

"We don't," said Mrs. Mazur. "You go by your conscience."

"And what if one person has more conscience than the other?" asked Val.

Mrs. Mazur was quiet for a long time. Then she said:

"Pete Palmer has a small conscience. He checked my bills, and then for six months he kept saying I owed him. I baked him enough hand pies for the whole building, and he still kept saying I owed him. We had a falling-out. He still says 'I know' to me. Like he's sore about it."

---

That was where the Exchange was born: in Mrs. Mazur's kitchen, at ten o'clock at night, over a batch of cooling hand pies.

I don't remember who suggested it first. I think it was all three of us at once. Val said, "It has to be written down." Mrs. Mazur said, "Everybody has to know who can do what." I said, "We need a common measure." And we looked at each other.

The measure was the hardest part. Money was no good: Mrs. Mazur had almost none, and mine had gone to Bull. Hours? An hour of my repairs and an hour of her baking were, as Val had just explained, two completely different things. Bags of pierogi? Mrs. Mazur gave me a look, and I took that suggestion back on the spot.

"A hand pie," said Mrs. Mazur.

"What?"

"A hand pie. One of my hand pies. That's the measure."

"Why yours?"

"Because everybody knows my hand pies," said Mrs. Mazur. "The whole building's eaten them. Everybody knows what one's worth. Nobody knows your pierogi. And nobody knows hours. A hand pie is a hand pie."

Val was looking at Mrs. Mazur with an expression I'd only ever seen on her face at the department, when a crystal grew all by itself.

"Mrs. Mazur," she said. "You just did the thing money started with."

"I didn't do anything," said Mrs. Mazur. "I bake."

"No, really. You took something everybody knows and everybody values, and you made it the measure. Way back when, they did that with salt."

"Well, good," said Mrs. Mazur. "A hand pie beats salt. At least you can eat it."

---

After that, things moved fast.

How many hand pies is an iron? We argued about it for ten minutes. Mrs. Mazur said five. I said two, an iron's simple. Val said you shouldn't count what the work costs the one who does it, but what it gives the one who gets it. A new iron runs two and a half bags. A hand pie at the bakery is about a tenth of a bag. So a fixed iron is twenty-five hand pies.

"Twenty-five!" said Mrs. Mazur. "That's over two hours of shaping for me."

"And it took me two minutes to fix," I said.

"And it pays off for both of you," said Val. "There's your Ricardo. But here's the problem: for Mrs. Mazur the iron is twenty-five hand pies, and for you it's two minutes. So what's my hour of chemistry worth?"

Nobody said anything again.

In the end we agreed on this: everybody names the price of their own work in hand pies. Whoever needs the work agrees or doesn't. Like at the market. Only instead of money it's hand pies, and nobody can make anybody do anything. And so it wouldn't end up like it had with Pete Palmer, everything gets written down: who did what for whom, and for how many hand pies. In a notebook. And once a month we look at who's got how much.

"What if somebody's in the red?" asked Val.

"Then they owe hand pies," said Mrs. Mazur. "They bake and pay up. Or they do something."

"And what if somebody's way in the black?"

"Then everybody goes to them," said Mrs. Mazur. "Means they're a good person. That, or it's Timothy."

I laughed.

"So who's going to keep the notebook?" I asked.

Mrs. Mazur thought about it. Then she said, very reluctantly, the way people say things they don't enjoy admitting:

"Pete Palmer. He was an accountant at the plant. Numbers, he really does know: who owes whom how much, going back to the nineties. It's everything else he knows wrong."

"But you two are on the outs."

"So we'll make up," said Mrs. Mazur. "If he's the one keeping the notebook, he can't go around saying I owe him. It'll be right there in writing."

Val looked at her with admiration.

"Mrs. Mazur," she said. "You're a genius."

"I'm a cook," said Mrs. Mazur. "It's practically the same thing."

---

In the morning, before the market, I called Mikey. Mikey knew how to set up group chats. Fifteen minutes later we had a group. Mikey named it "Stairwell Three Exchange." I suggested just "The Exchange," without the stairwell, because maybe someday…

"Tim," said Mikey. "Let's start with the stairwell."

He was right.

By lunchtime there were eight people in the group. I found that out at the meat-pie stand, lying on my back under their vent hood, per Ashot's deal, in exchange for boiling water. Mrs. Mazur, me, Mikey, Val, Judy the nurse from the third floor, old Shirley from the first floor, who knits socks, and Katie, the young mom in 40, my neighbor across the landing. In three years, all I'd known about Katie was that her baby screamed at night. It turned out she sews. She offered to hem pants. And Old Pete.

Mrs. Mazur invited Old Pete in person. She went down to the second floor and knocked on the door with the sign that said DON'T RING. I KNOW. I happened to be coming down the stairs with my thermoses, and I heard the door open.

"Pete," said Mrs. Mazur. "I need an accountant."

There was a long silence.

"I know," said Old Pete. But in a completely different voice. Not sore. More as if he'd been waiting twenty years for somebody to ask him.

Ten minutes later he was holding a thick ledger, ruled the way ledgers are supposed to be ruled. He'd ruled it himself: I saw that he had a whole shelf of them at home, empty, waiting. He made the first entry in his neat accountant's handwriting:

"Nagel, Timothy — Mazur, Dorothy. Iron repair. Twelve hand pies. Balance: Nagel plus twelve."

"Why twelve?" I asked. "We agreed everybody names their own price."

"Twenty-five is a new iron," said Old Pete. "Hers was an old iron. Depreciation. Half. I rounded down, in favor of the retiree." He turned back a page. "That's not everything. Opening balance. Tuesday two weeks back, two in the morning: one pot, twelve pieces; I heard her shuffling up the stairs. Wednesday: string bag, small pot. After that, nearly every evening. On the other side: iron, twice; faucet; cabinet door; glasses."

He drew a line.

"Balance: Nagel minus thirty-one."

"Why minus?" I asked.

"Because you eat like three people," said Old Pete, "and you fix things in two minutes."

"Pete," said Mrs. Mazur, "you're such a stickler."

"I know," said Old Pete. And for the first time in all the years I'd known him, he smiled.

---

That evening, at home, I opened my notebook to a fresh page and wrote across the top: "The Exchange."

And underneath, what I'd figured out over these three days. The day before yesterday, on the crates with Ashot, I'd figured out that together we make more. Yesterday, at Mrs. Mazur's, I'd figured out why. Not because everybody does what they're best at. Because everybody does what costs them the least. Even if somebody else can do it faster.

I bake faster than Mrs. Mazur. But baking costs me more. So she's the one who should bake.

It was like that first night, when I figured out that bending is cheap and breaking is expensive. Only it wasn't about steel. It was about people.

I wrote: "Not who's better at it. Who it costs less." And underlined it.

Then I thought for a while and added at the bottom of the page:

"The hand pie is the measure. Old Pete is the accountant. Mrs. Mazur is a genius. Val is Ricardo."

Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill, eyeing the jar of hand pies Mrs. Mazur had sent home with me: the same ones, mine, the lopsided ones, fourteen minus the ones we'd eaten.

"No," I told him. "That's the measure. You don't eat the measure."

Lugnut thought it over. Then he jumped down, walked around the jar and carried off its shiny lid. He had his own measure of what things were worth.

My phone beeped. A new message in "Stairwell Three Exchange." From Shirley on the first floor:

"Anybody need socks? Wool. Two hand pies a pair. Or a handle for my frying pan."

I looked down at my feet. At my socks. One of them had a hole in it.

"Me," I wrote. "I'll do the handle."

Old Pete answered a minute later. He was online too, it turned out. One word:

"Logged."
