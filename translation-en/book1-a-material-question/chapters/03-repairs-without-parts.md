# Chapter 3. Repairs Without Parts

For breakfast I ate eight of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, three sandwiches, a can of smoked sardines I'd been saving for New Year's, and, while lost in thought, a bay leaf out of a packet of seasoning. It didn't taste good, but I finished it. Then I looked at my empty fridge and realized that if things kept going like this, my savings wouldn't go to the debt. They'd go to my stomach.

That was the first problem of the day. The second was waiting for me on the stairs.

On my way down, out of habit, I ran my hand along the railing. Ours is iron, welded together out of angle iron back when the building went up, and painted maybe fifteen times since, in layers, like a cake. The thimble was on my finger. I'd put it on back in the apartment, I'm not sure why. I guess it's like a wedding ring in the first few days: you're scared to take it off in case you lose it, and you're not used to wearing it.

Through the railing I could feel the stairs, and off to one side, in the wall, very close, the water riser. The same one, with the thin spot on the second floor.

I stopped on the second-floor landing. This was where Old Pete lived: Pete Palmer, a retiree who knew the truth about everything except what was actually going on. His door was padded with leatherette and decorated with a sign: "DON'T RING. I KNOW."

The riser ran right behind that door, through his bathroom. I put my palm flat against the wall where I could sense the pipe, and through the brick I felt the thin spot. Rust had eaten almost all the way through the steel; what was left was a crust as thick as an eggshell. In a year, maybe two, it would burst (at night, most likely, and in winter, most likely), and the water would go down to the first floor, into the basement, into the wiring.

Fixing it wasn't my job. The riser belonged to the building, the building belonged to the property management company, and the property management company belonged to some person nobody had ever seen. If I did nothing, someday the emergency line would send a truck, shut off the water for a full day and weld the pipe. Old Pete would file a complaint. The people on the first floor would be drying out their carpets.

If I did something, nobody would ever know anything had happened.

And that's where I got stuck on the landing for about five minutes, because it was a very strange question. Who was going to pay me for this? Nobody. Who was going to thank me? Nobody, because nobody would know there was anything to thank me for. You can't thank somebody for a flood that never happened.

But a flood that never happened is something too, isn't it? It's a dry carpet on the first floor. It's wiring that didn't burn out. It's a whole day with running water. It has a price; it's just that nobody will ever see it or pay it.

I thought the world was probably full of things like that. Breakdowns that never happened, because somebody prevented them and nobody found out. And if you think about it, most of what makes life normal got made exactly that way: somebody, sometime, tightened a nut that then never worked loose.

I could feel through a wall. But I could only reshape what I was touching. I sighed and rang the doorbell under the sign that said DON'T RING.

The sign hadn't lied. Old Pete opened the door, looked at me over his glasses and said, "I know," before I'd even opened my mouth.

"I'm here to look at your riser," I said. "Property management sent me."

"I know," Old Pete repeated darkly, and let me into the bathroom. "They're doing it on purpose. So later they can say it was always like that."

I didn't ask what exactly they were doing on purpose, or who "they" were. I shut the bathroom door behind me, put my hand on the cold pipe with its cloudy paint and concentrated. I took a little steel from a healthy stretch of the pipe, where the wall was thick, and drew it over to the thin spot. Quietly, slowly, in a layer. It was like darning a sock from the inside. A couple of minutes later, the pipe on the second floor was the same thickness all the way along: a little thinner than it had been at the factory, but whole.

The hunger that came was noticeable, as if I'd walked to the market and back. Twice. Old Pete said "I know" a third time by way of goodbye, and when I got out on the landing, I wrote in the notebook, right there on the windowsill: "Riser: done. Cost: half a bag. Paid by: nobody." Then I thought about it and added: "Question: who's supposed to pay for what didn't happen?"

I didn't have an answer. I put the notebook in my pocket and went to the market.

---

The market greeted me the usual way: the sunflower-seed lady nodded. Everything was the same as always, except me. And that, it turned out, showed.

"You look like you didn't sleep," Ashot said from across the aisle.

"I didn't sleep at all."

"Ah," Ashot said knowingly. "A woman?"

"A radiator."

Ashot looked at me with pity, the way you'd look at a man who spends his nights with a radiator, and went back to his shoe.

I sat down behind my counter and started figuring out how I was going to work now.

It wasn't as simple a question as it seemed. Last night I'd realized I could fix metal almost instantly. A wrench, a handle, a faucet: each of those would take a minute. But if somebody brings me a watch, and I take it, touch it with my finger and hand it back working ten seconds later, then, first of all, he won't believe I did anything, and second of all, he'll tell the whole market. In a week, people from TV would show up. In two weeks, people who weren't from TV, but had very serious faces.

So I'd have to fake it. Look like I was working the way I always did, with a screwdriver, a soldering iron, tweezers, and do the real work on the sly, under cover.

I laid out all my tools on the counter. I switched on the soldering iron: let it heat up, let it smell of rosin. I put the magnifier visor up on my forehead. I flipped the sign to OPEN.

And I waited.

---

The first customer was a woman with an umbrella. One of the ribs had broken: the thin steel tube had snapped in half, and the canopy hung like a wounded wing.

"How much?" the woman asked.

"Half a bag," I said out of habit, then caught myself. "I mean…" And I named the price in money.

Normally I'd replace the whole rib: take off the broken one, put on a new one from my stock. I did have stock. I pulled ribs off the umbrellas people threw out, which I found in the dumpsters after every downpour. The job took about twenty minutes.

This time I took the umbrella, turned toward the shelf as if I were looking for a part, and squeezed the break in my fist. The steel was thin, simple, easy to understand. The two ends met and grew together. Hunger: practically none.

Twenty minutes of work, in five seconds.

I stood with my back to the woman for another minute, shuffling empty boxes around on the shelf, then turned, popped the umbrella open to check it, and handed it over.

"That was fast," she said, surprised.

"Simple break," I said.

She paid and left. I watched her go and realized that for five seconds of work I'd charged what I used to charge for twenty minutes. And that was right, because she wasn't paying for my minutes. She was paying for a whole umbrella. She didn't care how long I'd fiddled with it. She needed an umbrella that didn't hang like a wounded wing.

But right away I thought that I used to care. It used to be that my twenty minutes set the price, and my ribs out of the dumpster, and my soldering iron. Now the only thing setting the price was what the umbrella was worth to the person. And if I honestly charged for my time, the umbrella would cost pennies. But if I charged for the result…

On the back of a receipt I wrote: "Price for what? For the time, or for the thing working?" I thought about it and added: "If for time, I go broke. If for the result, am I robbing people?"

Again, no answer. The notebook was slowly turning into a collection of questions without answers, like an exam I'd flunked once, long ago.

---

By eleven I'd finished nine jobs. Usually by eleven I've done three.

A watch with a cracked balance staff: half a minute, and then another five minutes pretending to poke at it with tweezers. Mrs. Mazur's iron: she brought it in a string bag along with another pot of hand pies. The heating element had burned out, and I joined the break, holding it in my fist while I pretended to check the cord with a multimeter. A frying pan whose handle had fallen off. Two jacket zippers. A garage padlock with a snapped spring. That one I had to work at: the spring was hardened, stiff, and while I was knitting it back together, my hand got so hot I nearly dropped the whole thing on the floor.

The hunger was building. I ate Mrs. Mazur's hand pies, then went and got a fried meat pie from the stand by the gate. Then a second one.

And somewhere after the second meat pie, I came up with the thermos.

I have a thermos: old, metal, full of hot tea that I pour in the morning and drink all day. Last night I'd figured out that I could take heat from metal nearby. Mrs. Mazur's radiator wasn't mine. But the thermos was mine. And so was the tea.

I put my hand on the thermos, and while I worked on the next zipper, I tried taking the heat not out of myself but out of it.

It worked. Not right away; at first, like last night, I kept pulling from myself out of inertia. But then I found how to switch it over, like moving a bucket from one hand to the other. The heat ran out of the thermos, through my palm, into the zipper, into its little steel teeth, and I felt like I was hardly getting tired at all.

Three zippers later I opened the thermos to have a drink.

The tea wasn't just cold. There was ice floating in it.

I sat staring at iced tea in October and understood that I'd found one of the most important things of the past twenty-four hours. Heat is energy. If you take it out of hot tea, the work becomes free. Almost free: the tea has to be heated, and for that you need gas, and gas costs money. But gas costs a lot less than meat pies.

I did some quick math, and for once college actually came in handy. A calorie is exactly that: one gram of water, one degree. A liter of boiling water cooled all the way down to ice is about a hundred and eighty calories, the big kind on food labels. A tenth of a bag. Enough for a zipper, not enough for a weld. But the gas for that liter is about a hundred times cheaper than a meat pie. So if I carried not one thermos but three…

I wrote: "Heat from the thermos! Cheaper than food. Get more thermoses." And underlined it.

Then I looked across the aisle at Ashot's counter, where he kept an electric kettle, and thought that an electric kettle was basically a bottomless thermos, as long as you stood next to an outlet.

But I set that thought aside. There was one outlet in my booth, it belonged to Mr. Abrams, and it ran through a meter that Mr. Abrams checked personally and with suspicion. Energy, as it turns out, always belongs to somebody.

---

Ashot had been watching me since morning.

I could feel it, the way you feel a draft. He sat in his booth across the aisle, tapping his hammer on a heel, and every five minutes he raised his head. Ashot has been at this market for twenty years. He knows what a normal workday looks like for a repairman. And what he was seeing today didn't look like a normal day.

Around noon he couldn't stand it anymore and came over across the aisle, wiping his hands on his apron.

"Tim," he said. "What, you bought parts?"

"What parts?"

"You did more this morning than in a week. You bought umbrella ribs wholesale? Or somebody's helping you?"

"Nobody's helping. I just got lucky. Easy jobs."

Ashot looked at my counter. At the soldering iron, which had been heating since morning and hadn't touched a single circuit board. At the magnifier visor, which I hadn't once flipped down over my eyes. At the multimeter I was holding without turning it on. Ashot is not a stupid man. The reason he's so good at haggling is that he can tell when the other guy is hiding something.

"Your soldering iron doesn't smell," he said.

"What?"

"No rosin smell. You didn't solder anything today. But you fixed the iron. And you fixed that lady's hair dryer. You have to solder the wire in a hair dryer. How'd you fix a hair dryer without a soldering iron?"

I felt my ears start to burn. That was a new sensation too: for the first time in twelve years, I didn't have an answer to a question about my own work.

"Twisted it," I said. "Twist splice with heat-shrink. No solder. New technology."

"New technology," Ashot repeated, without expression.

"Imported," I added, for credibility.

Ashot stood there and looked me over once more, for a long time, the way he'd look at a suspicious heel, and left. But I knew he didn't believe me. And I knew that from now on, he'd be watching more closely.

I wrote: "Ashot. The soldering iron has to smell."

Then I took a lump of rosin and set it on the tip of the iron. The booth filled with sweet pine smoke. Across the aisle, Ashot raised his head and sniffed. It didn't seem to reassure him.

---

After lunch, Mikey came by.

Mikey Kravitz lives in my building, on the third floor, right under my apartment. He's fifteen, skinny as a screwdriver, wears a hoodie with the hood up in any weather, and talks in that special tone teenagers use with grown-ups when they're sure ahead of time that grown-ups don't understand anything. Usually they're right.

Mikey put a phone on the counter. The screen was cracked into a web: the classic pattern you get when it lands on a corner.

"Hey, Tim," he said. "How much?"

"To replace the screen?"

"Yeah. But I don't have money. I've got this much."

He named a sum. It was about a third of what a new screen cost, and that's for the cheapest kind, the knockoff that starts showing everything in green after a month.

I looked at the phone. Then at Mikey. Mikey was looking off to the side with the expression of a person who isn't asking for anything but is really hoping.

Here's what I thought. A screen is glass. I understood glass better than wood, but worse than metal. Glass is sand (silicon and oxygen) melted so fast that the atoms didn't have time to line up into a lattice and froze in a jumble, like a crowd at a bus stop. Professor Arkin told us about it in his second lecture, and that was the only lecture I remembered, because he brought a bottle to it and smashed it on the lectern. "There," he said, looking at the pieces. "An amorphous solid. A liquid that forgot how to flow."

If glass is a liquid that forgot how to flow, then maybe you could remind it. Just a little. Just along the cracks.

"Leave it," I said. "Come back in an hour."

Mikey left. I laid the phone on the counter screen-up, covered it with my palm and turned the thimble on my finger.

Glass was strange. Nothing like metal. Metal felt like an even brick wall, and glass felt like sand that had suddenly frozen in mid-motion. The cracks in it stood out clearly, like rips in fabric. I asked them to close. Carefully. The edges reached toward each other, reluctantly, slowly, as if the glass really were remembering that it used to be liquid. It took about three minutes and a lot of effort. I could feel the thermos under my left hand going cold.

When I took my hand away, the screen was whole.

Not just whole: perfect. Not a single scratch. Apparently, in my zeal, I'd closed up not only the cracks but all the little scratches the phone had collected over years in Mikey's pocket.

I looked at the screen and noticed something else. The phone wouldn't turn on.

The impact must have knocked loose the connector between the screen and the board. I opened the case (honestly, with a screwdriver) and saw that the tiny plastic latch holding the ribbon cable had cracked in half.

Plastic.

I remembered that plastic was made of long molecules, like spaghetti, all tangled together. That was all I remembered. But the latch was tiny, and I thought: what could possibly go wrong? It's just a little piece.

I squeezed the latch between my fingers and asked it to grow back together.

It did. I clicked the ribbon cable into place, put the phone back together and turned it on. The screen lit up, showed the logo, booted. Everything worked.

I was pleased with myself.

---

Mikey came back an hour later. He picked up the phone, turned it over in his hands, switched it on. He looked at the screen for a long time, closely, tilting the phone toward the light.

"Tim," he said slowly. "Where's the scratch?"

"What scratch?"

"There was a scratch right here. A deep one. You couldn't see it under the cracks, but it was there. I remember. I made it myself, when I wiped out on my bike last year."

"It's a new screen," I said. "I replaced it."

"I had a screen protector. With a sticker. Where's the sticker?"

That's when I realized that in my zeal I'd fused the screen protector and the screen into one piece. The sticker must have ended up between them and melted inside: if you tilted the screen toward the light, a faint rainbow swirl showed through under the glass.

"It came off," I said. "I took off the old protector. You can buy another one."

Mikey gave me the look teenagers give grown-ups when they realize the grown-ups are lying to them and decide not to say so out loud. For now.

"Thanks," he said, and paid me what he had, the whole third.

"That's too much," I said. "Keep half."

"No," said Mikey. "Nobody values what's free."

That was Mrs. Mazur's line. Apparently it was going around the building like a cold.

Mikey left, and I thought that Mikey, Ashot and Mrs. Stern made three people now who suspected something or knew something. And it had only been a day and a half.

---

At five o'clock Mikey came back.

He was walking fast, his face was angry, and he was holding the phone like it had bitten him.

"Tim," he said, and put it down on the counter. "It died."

I looked. The screen was dark. I pressed the button: nothing. I opened the case.

The latch I'd grown back together had crumbled.

Not broken, not cracked: crumbled to powder. A white dust like chalk lay on the board, and the ribbon cable was hanging loose. I touched the dust with my finger, and it smeared like face powder.

I figured out what had happened, though not right away. Plastic had gone the way wood had gone last night. I didn't understand how it was put together. I'd told it, "Grow back together," and it had, in some way of its own that had seemed right to me. But the spaghetti inside hadn't tangled back up the way it should have. It had just stuck together, like macaroni glued in a kid's art project. And an hour later, warmed up by the phone running, the glue had come apart.

"I didn't drop it," Mikey said. "It just shut off. In my pocket."

"I know," I said. "It's my fault."

Mikey raised his eyebrows. Apparently he hadn't expected a grown-up to admit it was his fault. To be honest, it surprised me too.

I dug into the drawer of parts I'd salvaged from dead phones in the shelter and found a latch that looked about right. A real one, from the factory. I put it in with my hands and tweezers, honestly. It took ten minutes. The phone turned on.

Then I gave Mikey all his money back.

"That's for the breakdown," I said. "Repairs are guaranteed. Doesn't work, money back."

Mikey looked at the money, then at me.

"What about the screen?" he asked. "The screen's new. You replaced it."

"The screen's on me," I said. "For you walking around a whole hour with a dead phone."

Mikey stood there. Then he asked, quietly, without the teenager voice:

"Tim. Did you really replace the screen?"

I looked at him. He was fifteen, he had no money, he had a phone that mattered more to him than anything in the world, and he'd just gotten it back in one piece. It would have been easier for him not to ask.

"No," I said. "I fixed it. But I can't tell you how."

Mikey nodded. Slowly, seriously, the way people nod when they've gotten an answer to a question they didn't expect to have answered.

"Okay," he said. "Then I won't tell anybody."

And he left. And I sat there thinking that I'd probably just done something very stupid. Or maybe something very right. I couldn't tell which.

---

In the evening I closed up the booth and sat down on a crate behind it to do the math.

Take for the day: eleven bags. Minus the money I'd given back to Mikey: ten and a half. That was almost twice my best day ever at the market. And five times a bad one.

Spent on food: two and a half bags. Mrs. Mazur's hand pies were free, except I still owed her for the iron, or rather, she didn't owe me, I owed her something for the hand pies, or the other way around; I'd completely lost track of who owed whom what. The meat pies. The sardines. It was a lot. But the take was bigger.

If this kept up until Friday, I'd have… I counted on my fingers and stopped smiling. Net for the day came to eight bags. Counting today and the two days left until Friday: twenty-four bags, and that's if I didn't eat any more than today. I'd still be fifty-six short of eighty. A whole freezer full of pierogi.

But twenty-four isn't zero. And with Bull, I'd learned over the years, a conversation with something in your hand and a conversation with empty hands are two different conversations.

Even so, the plan had three more cracks in it.

One: Ashot. Today I'd done nine jobs before lunch and another ten after. Nineteen jobs in a day. Some of them were people who used to go to Ashot, or to the repair shop around the corner, or would have just thrown the broken thing out and bought a new one. If I worked like this every day, in a month Ashot wouldn't have any customers left. Neither would the shop around the corner. I wasn't taking anything from them by force, but if people stopped coming to them, it would amount to the same thing.

Two: suspicions. Ashot, Mikey, Mrs. Stern. Three people in two days. If it kept up, in a week the whole market would know there was something off about Tim from We Fix Everything. And somebody would definitely want to find out exactly what.

Three: plastic. And wood. And everything I didn't understand. Today had shown that when I take on something I don't know, I leave it worse than I found it. Mikey could have gotten mad and gone around telling everyone that Tim takes your money and breaks your phone. Good thing he didn't. But next time it might not be Mikey; it might be somebody else. Somebody who wouldn't say "Nobody values what's free," but would say, "Give me my money back, and something for pain and suffering."

I wrote in the notebook:

"One: I can make a lot of money. But only off what I understand. Metal: yes. Glass: yes, but hard. Plastic: no. Electronics: only by hand, the old way.

Two: heat from the thermos is cheaper than food. Carry thermoses.

Three: ashot is losing out. What to do about it?

Four: mikey knows. Said he won't tell. Believe him?

Five: the riser. Nobody's going to pay. Did it anyway. Right?"

I drew a circle around the last one.

Then I took the receipt from that morning out of my pocket, the one with "Price for what?" written on the back, and looked at it for a long time.

I'd figured out one thing. Today I'd charged the usual way: for the thing working again. If I'd charged for my time, it would have come to about a twentieth as much. If I'd charged for the fact that nobody else in all of Granton could do it like this, it would have been who knows how many times more. And somewhere in the middle was a price that would be fair. But I didn't know where that middle was. All I knew was that my soldering iron had to smell of rosin, or Ashot wouldn't believe me. Which meant part of my price now wasn't work. It was theater.

That was an unpleasant thought. For twelve years I'd been proud that I didn't have to pretend. That I fixed things, period. And now I had to pretend I fixed them worse than I could.

The shutter squealed when I pushed it back up (I'd forgotten something inside), and the pigeons on the roof took off in a clatter of wings.

I put my hand on the shutter. Through the thimble I felt the rusty tracks, the worn-down rollers, the bent axle.

And I didn't fix it.

Let it squeal. If it suddenly stopped squealing, Ashot would be the first to know.

---

On the way home I bought two more bags of pierogi, ate both at home without even taking my jacket off, and fell asleep like that. Before I drifted off, I thought that tomorrow I needed to buy two more thermoses. And also figure out what to do about Ashot.

And also that, for the first time in years, I seemed to be a little less afraid of Friday.

It was a strange feeling. I lay in the dark trying to decide whether I liked it or not.

Then Lugnut squeezed in through the transom window, lay down on my feet and started to purr.

He didn't have the ring, of course. I checked.
