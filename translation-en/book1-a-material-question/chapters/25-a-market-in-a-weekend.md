# Chapter 25. A Market in a Weekend

You can't build a market in a weekend. I knew that from the start. All you can build in a weekend is what you've spent two weeks of evenings getting ready.

So for twelve days, we got ready.

---

Every evening after the market we went out to the vacant lot by the truck depot. At first in Bull's old blue sedan and Hank's old boxy compact; later on the bus to the end of the line and then on foot across the field, because we no longer fit in two cars. The lot was just the way Goldie had described it: no road, no power, no water. Only snow, weeds, the rusted shell of the truck depot with its windows knocked out, and a third of a mile past it, the dump. The dump smelled. Not much yet, in March. The frost was still holding it down.

On the tenth, Goldie went to Keyes with her statement. Her dad didn't call her, not at lunch and not after, and that was worse than a call. Keyes drove out to the pine forest. They didn't let him past the gate: "The owner is unwell. His attorney will be in touch." The analysis of the pea was promised by April. I thought about all of it every night. During the day there was no time.

The dump had everything. I'd said that before. But now we had a list.

Ms. Pratt drew up the list. Three pages, ruled, numbered. "Item one. Steel pipe, diameter no less than two inches, length no less than ten feet. Needed: four hundred. Found: one hundred forty-seven." And so on, down to item twenty-nine: "Plastic bottles, clear, labels removed. Needed: a lot."

"How many is 'a lot'?" asked Old Pete.

"It's when you stop counting," said Ms. Pratt. "That's the first time in my life I've ever written that."

Old Pete logged it as a historic event.

Everybody worked the dump. Ashot and Greg hauled pipe. Mikey and his class (Lana gave her permission, and five more parents gave theirs) collected bottles in sacks. Lottie sorted glass: "Clear goes on the left, for Hank, he's going to melt it down and cast something. Green and brown, later." And Hank really was casting. He didn't say "I'm not asking" anymore. He said, "Here, let me."

Val sorted plastic. That was her job, and she wasn't giving it to anybody.

"This is polyethylene," she'd say, holding up a jerry can. "Melts at a hundred and thirty degrees Celsius, doesn't smell, bends. This one's fine. This"—a soda bottle—"is fine too, but differently: it has to be dried out first, or it goes brittle. And this"—she'd pick up a scrap of linoleum—"is polyvinyl chloride. Chlorine. Heat it and you get hydrogen chloride. That'll burn your lungs. This one is no. Never. Separate pile, with a sign."

Mikey made the sign. In big red letters: "DO NOT HEAT. POISON. VAL FINCH."

Val taught me plastic in the evenings, by the wood stove in Hank's garage. Three evenings on a single jerry can. I finally understood why the latch on Mikey's phone had crumbled after an hour. Back then, I'd glued together chains that needed to be woven. Polyethylene isn't glass and it isn't steel. It's a tangle of long threads. If you just press them against each other, they come apart. But if you let them weave into each other, like the fibers in felt, they hold. On the fourth evening I made my first panel. White, cloudy, the size of a newspaper page, out of two jerry cans. I bent it. It didn't crack. I left it out in the cold overnight. In the morning it still didn't crack.

Val wrote: "Plastic. Nagel. Finally."

---

Energy was the main question. I ran the numbers on a napkin every evening.

The frame: a thousand joints. In January I'd figured ten bags a joint, because I was counting welding. But there was no need to weld. Crimping pipe into pipe is bending, not joining. Bending is cheap. But cheap isn't free. Half a bag a joint, five hundred bags for the lot. The plastic had to be melted, even if only at a hundred and thirty Celsius, and that was hundreds and hundreds of pounds of it. The glass had to be very nearly melted, and that was the worst of all. If I paid for all that in pierogi, I'd have to eat the whole meatpacking plant. And a thousand kilowatt-hours on the bill came to twenty-five bags. The meatpacking plant against twenty-five bags.

So: electricity.

There was a transformer at the truck depot. Old, shut off fifteen years or so. Goldie found out whose it was (the city utility's) and in a week she'd arranged a fifteen-kilowatt temporary hookup for the construction site, off the pole on the bypass, through a new meter. The association paid. Mr. Abrams took the receipt in personally, as if it were his own rent.

And Corey did the hookup.

The same Corey, the electrician from the property management company, who back in December had looked at my bare tips and said, "That thing'll kill you." I called him myself. He came out, looked at the lot, at the transformer, at me, at thirty people with pipe from the dump, and said:

"I'm not going to ask." He paused. "But I'm going to check."

And he did check. He put in a panel by the pole. A proper, grown-up one, with a breaker for every line and with ground-fault protection: the kind that trips if current goes somewhere it shouldn't, into the ground.

"So that if one of your people grabs a live wire standing in a puddle," said Corey, "it doesn't kill him. It trips first."

And then something interesting happened. The very first night, so I wouldn't have to drag a second wire through the snowdrifts, I did it the way I had back in October, the night of the gold: just the live wire to the thimble, nothing coming back, standing in rubber boots. The thimble is its own ground, after all. And the protection clicked off instantly. Corey came over with a flashlight, looked at the panel, then at me.

"Ground fault," he said. "Went out one wire and didn't come back on the other. Where's your current going?"

I couldn't tell him: into the thimble. That the thimble was my ground. That current going into the thimble looks, to the protection, exactly like current going through a man into wet concrete.

"I don't know," I said.

"Well, there you go," said Corey. "The protection doesn't know either. And it doesn't care. All it sees is current going where it shouldn't. So it trips. You want to draw power, draw it like you do at home: both wires. Then it won't notice."

And he was right. With the two leads, both of them, pressed to the thimble the way I'd done in December, the protection stayed quiet. The current went out along one wire and came back along the other. And in between, the thimble. Not me.

For the rings, Corey set up separate tables. Nine tables, each with its own line, its own breaker, its own protection and a rubber mat. Everybody with a ring goes to their own table. Nobody touches the next person's. Ms. Pratt wrote it into the instructions as point eight. Corey read it and said, "First safety briefing I've ever seen that says 'state out loud what you want.'" And signed it himself.

By then there were nine rings. Mrs. Stern gave us the silver: Professor Stern's sugar bowl, with his monogram. "Forty years he drank his tea without sugar, and that bowl just sat there. Let it be of some use now, at least." It was enough for five rings, with some left over. They grew on my hand, two or three at a time, in a chain, each one from the one before. Weaker and weaker, like somebody's retelling. For the construction, Ms. Pratt agreed to let them go out for the build, signed for: "The instructor is responsible. But there's one instructor and a thousand joints." The last one, the ninth, was so weak that Greg said, "All it does for me is keep the meat cold." But even that was something.

First we tested. On Corey's panel, with the protection on: if current goes through a hand, it trips before it can hurt. I went first, then Bull. Current went through the ring (faintly, like through a drinking straw) and the protection stayed quiet. Without it I wouldn't have let anybody near.

---

On Saturday, March twenty-first, at seven in the morning, there were forty people on the lot.

At nine, sixty.

By lunchtime I'd lost count. Like Ms. Pratt: "a lot."

The whole market came. The building came: Judy, old Shirley, Katie from 40, Old Pete in his hat with his ledger. Mikey's classmates came with their parents. Mikey came and brought Dylan. The same Dylan, from the next building over, whose bet had gotten Mikey to open Hank's garage on New Year's Eve. Dylan stood there scowling, watching, and then he picked up a sack of bottles and carried it off. Later Mikey told me that Dylan had told somebody about the garage after all, back then: a guy from his block, who'd apparently told his older brother. "He came to tell me himself," Mikey said. "I didn't ask him to."

Mrs. Stern came. By taxi, in an old coat, with her cane. She sat down on a crate by Mrs. Mazur's kitchen, and Mrs. Mazur gave her a hand pie and some tea, and the two of them talked all day as if they'd known each other forty years.

Goldie came, in jeans and a jacket, with a printout in a plastic sleeve: "Temporary structure. No building permit required. If anyone shows up, send them to me." Nobody showed up. Goldie hung the sleeve on a nail by the entrance and went off to haul bottles.

People I didn't know came too. Just people from town. Somebody had seen it in the Exchange chat, which was open now: Mikey had taken the password off, "let them look, we've got nothing to hide." Glen was reading it too, of course. We knew that. Old Pete wrote: "Nothing to hide. Decided by majority vote." Somebody had heard about it at the market. Somebody was walking along the bypass and stopped to see what kind of lunatics were digging around in a dump in the freezing cold.

Mrs. Mazur's kitchen stood by the entrance. A table made of two pallets, four hot plates running off Corey's panel, eight pots, and Mrs. Mazur in the middle of it all, in a headscarf and apron, with a ladle, like a general on a hill. The hand pies went out in waves. Stefan the baker brought three sheet pans and set up next to her. Two women I didn't know joined them; they said they were from the building next door, that they baked too, and "Mrs. Mazur, teach us."

"I teach in the open," said Mrs. Mazur. "Dough's over there. Wash your hands over there."

---

We raised the frame all day.

I'm not going to tell you about every joint. There were a thousand of them. I'll tell you how it looked from above, from the roof of the truck depot, where I climbed at lunchtime to understand what we were doing.

Down below lay a skeleton. Long, a hundred and thirty feet by sixty-five, made of rusty pipe, rails and beams from the dump. I'd straightened the pipe the day before, on a table, on Corey's line. Here all it needed was to be stood up and joined. Slide one into the other, crimp it, the way Ashot's heel tips are crimped on. Bending is cheap. But a thousand joints is a thousand joints, and that was work for everybody.

Bull worked the joints along the left wall, the north one. With his ring, at one of Corey's portable lines with its own protection, in rubber boots. He took hold of each joint the way you take hold of a sick engine, listened, then squeezed, and the pipe grew into the pipe. It didn't fuse. It crimped, tight, like a nut nobody will ever get off again.

Ms. Pratt worked the right wall. Every one of her joints was even. Perfect. Ruler-straight. Before each one she said it out loud, like reading a report into the record: "Joint forty-seven. Crimp. Edge: the sleeve." And crimped. Mikey stood next to her, holding a flashlight and handing her pipe.

Ashot made seals. Out of the sidewalls of old tires, the part with no cord in it, which Hank cut into strips. Ashot would take a strip and work it in his hands, and the rubber went soft as the leather on a boot he'd repaired forty times. It cost him two hand pies a strip. He laid them under the seams between panels, to keep out the drafts.

Greg cut sheet iron. You don't cut iron with a butcher knife. Greg cut it with Hank's angle grinder, on Corey's line, and used the ring only to feel where the metal was thin. Thirty years with knives: he knew thin better than anybody.

Hank poured glass. He'd found an old kiln at the truck depot, for firing some kind of parts, and hooked it up to Corey's line. Lottie's clear glass went in there. Hank poured it into molds he'd welded himself years ago, "back when I still had a welder." What came out weren't windows. They were thick cloudy squares that let light through but not faces. They cooled in the kiln till morning; in the cold, they'd have cracked. Roxanne said that for flowers they were perfect: light, but no sun to scorch them.

And I walked up and down past all of it and did what nobody else could. The heaviest joints, at the corners, where four pipes came together. And the plastic. Two hundred panels, six and a half feet on a side, half a finger thick, twenty jerry cans apiece, which we'd made over the two weeks, had to be set into the frame and their edges woven into one another, like felt, so they'd make a roof. Only I could do that. Val followed me around with a notebook, saying, "This one's polyethylene. This one too. Not this one, that's an antifreeze jug, it has additives, put it aside." I put it aside.

That Saturday I ate forty-two of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies. Old Pete stopped writing them down at thirty.

The rings worked for eight hours. Then they gave out. Not the rings: the people. Ms. Pratt sat down on a crate and said, "Unbudgeted expense. I can't do any more." Bull lay down right on the tarp and fell asleep. Ashot sat on his shoe-stool, which he'd brought "for the booth," and ate his fourth hand pie in a row. Mikey slept by Mrs. Mazur's kitchen with his head on a sack of bottles.

By nightfall the frame was standing. Bare, rusty, no roof. Like an enormous whale that had washed up on a vacant lot.

I stood under it and looked up. There were no stars. The sky was low and gray, a March sky.

"Tim," Val said beside me. "It's standing."

"It's standing," I said.

"Do you realize that this is…" She didn't finish.

I did realize. Alone, I'd have been building it for forty years; I'd done the math. And here it was: one day. Sixty people. Nine rings. One thimble. And Mrs. Mazur's kitchen.

---

On Sunday we put up the roof and the booths.

Sunday was the same kind of day, only faster. People knew what to do by now. Ms. Pratt marked out the spaces in chalk. Mr. Abrams walked around with a folder writing down whose booth went where, and didn't argue with anybody even once, and that was, if you ask me, the real miracle of the weekend. Ashot got the spot by the entrance, "because I'll be the first one tapping," and he did tap, too. With his hammer, the one that won't break, on his new counter: Greg's iron, Hank's glass on top. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

Lottie hung up her husband's sign. FISH. FRESH. ALWAYS. Over a counter that still smelled of the dump.

By five in the afternoon the plastic panels had gone onto the frame. A roof. Cloudy, white, woven together at the edges like felt. Under it, it was as bright as an overcast day. Roxanne's carnations stood in a bucket by Mrs. Mazur's hot plates, where it was warm.

And then, at six, as it was getting dark, the frame creaked.

---

At first I didn't understand. I was standing out in the middle of things, by Mrs. Mazur's kitchen, and had just taken a hand pie from her. Hadn't had time to bite into it. And suddenly the thimble on my finger went warm. On its own. The way it only ever goes warm for one reason.

The other one was close.

I looked up. The frame was creaking. Not all of it: the left wall. The one where Bull had worked all day. The pipes he'd crimped were starting to give. Slowly. As if somebody were asking the steel to rest. As if somebody were doing to each joint what Mikey had done to the spring on New Year's Eve. Only to a hundred of them at once.

I put my hand on the nearest post. Through solid metal I can feel a long way. The frame wasn't one piece, but it was crimped so tight that, for the power, it was almost the same thing. Like the riser through its threads. I could feel all of it, down to the farthest corner.

And I could feel where it was coming from.

From the north corner. From behind the truck depot, out of the dark. Under the north wall we'd laid an old rail as a footing. One end of it stuck out of the snow beyond the depot fence, a good fifteen feet farther than it needed to. I'd meant to cut it off on Friday. Somebody was standing out there with a hand on the rail, asking the steel to rest. Through the rail, into the whole frame.

Even, cold, perfect handwriting. Without a single pore.

The joints on the left wall let go one after another. The steel was going soft. The frame was leaning. Slowly. Half an inch. Another half inch. People were standing under it. Lottie by her sign. Ashot by his post. Mrs. Mazur's kitchen. Mrs. Stern on her crate.

Frost spread over the pipes. White, fast. It was taking heat from the frame, from the air under the roof, from the people next to the pipes. Lottie cried out and snatched her hand off a post: the post was ice-cold.

He was taking heat from living hands. That was something I had never once allowed myself.

I could have run over there, behind the depot. I could have tried to overpower him: my thimble against his. I knew that was wrong. The ninth volume: "If there are ever two of them, and they are in different hands, let those hands know each other. Otherwise it is not science. It is war." If I fought him, he'd be the stronger. He'd had twenty years. I'd had since October. He understood steel down to every face. Me, "roughly."

One on one, I'd lose.

But I wasn't alone.

"Everybody with a ring!" I shouted. "To the frame! Each of you to your own joint! Hold it! Don't change anything—hold! Ask for it to stay the way it is! Out loud! Mr. Abrams, get everybody out from under the roof! Mrs. Stern first!"

And they went. And the rest went the other way, toward the exit, behind Mr. Abrams and his folder.

---

Bull went to the left wall, to his own joints. He took hold of a pipe with both hands, ring and all, and said out loud, hoarsely: "My joint. Hold." And the joint stopped giving.

Ms. Pratt went to the corner where the left wall met the end wall. She took hold of a post and said, "Joint one hundred twelve. Hold. Edge: the whole wall." And her joint, even, ruler-straight, set like it had been driven in.

Ashot grabbed his post by the entrance. "This is my booth. Don't touch it." His voice was the one he uses to name the price of a heel tip: final. The post stopped.

Mikey, with the little-finger ring, the weakest of the first ones, took hold of a pipe and shut his eyes. "Hold. Just hold. The edge is this pipe." The pipe went still.

Greg. Hank. Stefan, who didn't wear a ring, just held a post by his oven with his bare hands and said something through his teeth. Mrs. Mazur. Without a ring. She went to the nearest post, wiped her hands on her apron and laid both palms on the cold pipe. Her own palms, cold as always. And said, "Now then. None of that."

And the frame stood fast.

Not right away. For thirty seconds or so it went on creaking, like two people pulling one rope in opposite directions. Through the post I could feel him pulling from the other side. Hard. Precise. Alone. And from this side, nine rings and one Mrs. Mazur were pulling. Every one of them far weaker than him. But there were a lot of them.

I kept my own hand on the post, idle. Just listening. If I'd taken hold myself, it would have turned into one on one again. And each of them held their own joint. Not the whole frame: their own. Their own piece. Their own booth. Their own pipe.

Val had told me that in December, in the lab, about hydrogen bonds. One bond is nothing. A thousand is a thread you can't break.

The frost stopped spreading, then started to melt. Every joint had somebody's warm hands on it now, and the heat was going into the steel from its own people, not out to him. Pulling it down the rail through ten other people's palms was like drinking through a straw somebody had pinched shut in ten places.

The creaking stopped.

The frame was standing.

---

I took my hand off the post and walked across the lot toward the truck depot. Not running. Walking. Along my own tracks from the day before, past the heaps of bottles, past Val's sign, DO NOT HEAT. POISON.

Around the corner of the depot, by the rusty fence, by the rail, stood Glen.

In his gray sweater under a long coat. In white gloves. No hat. One hand still lay on the rail. His face wasn't angry. It was lost. Like a man who'd spent twenty years on one problem and found the answer in a different textbook.

He looked at me. Then past me, at the lot. At the frame under its white plastic roof, lit by Corey's lamps. At the people who were still standing there, holding their joints with their hands, though there was no need to hold them anymore. At Mrs. Mazur in her apron. At Ms. Pratt with her folder. At Mikey with his flashlight. At Mrs. Stern on her crate.

He looked at Mrs. Stern the longest.

"How many of them are there?" he asked quietly.

"I don't know," I said. "A lot."

Glen took his hand off the rail.

"Nine," he said. "Rings. I felt them. Every one. Weak as a whisper." He paused. "And one woman with nothing at all. In an apron. She held harder than three of them."

"That's Mrs. Mazur," I said. "Thirty years on puff pastry."

Glen looked out at the lot.

"I'm stronger than any one of them," he said. "Ten times over. Twenty."

"Yes," I said.

"And I couldn't do it."

"No."

"I didn't want it to fall," he said. "I wanted you to see what I can do."

He was silent for a long time. Then he said, so quietly I could barely hear:

"For twenty years I thought knowledge is something you have only as long as other people don't." He looked at his gloved hand. "And they don't have knowledge. They have rings and hand pies."

"They have themselves. Each other," I said. "That's knowledge too."

Glen looked at me. Straight at me, for the first time in the whole conversation.

I wanted to say something else. About the notebooks. About "Don't search." About the report, about Keyes, about Lenny. I didn't say anything. None of that was for now.

I held out the hand pie to him. The one I'd been holding the whole time, ever since the creaking started. Mrs. Mazur's. Cabbage. Whole. A little cold.

Glen looked at the hand pie. Then at me.

He didn't take it.

He turned and walked toward the road, where a big black car stood on the shoulder with its lights off. He walked slowly, evenly, leaving good, even size-eleven-and-a-half tracks in the March snow. The driver, big and quiet, got out and opened the back door. Glen got in. The car drove away. The third time this winter he'd left his house.

I stood by the fence with the hand pie in my hand.

Then Val came up. Stood next to me. Looked at the road.

"Was that him?" she asked.

"That was him."

"What did he say?"

"That he's twenty times stronger than any one of us. And he couldn't do it."

Val was quiet for a while.

"That can be measured," she said at last. "The strength of one against the strength of many. I'm going to measure it."

"You will," I said. "You measure everything."

---

Late that evening, when most people had gone home, we sat under the new roof on crates by Mrs. Mazur's kitchen. Me, Val, Mrs. Mazur, Mrs. Stern, Mikey, Bull, Ms. Pratt and Old Pete.

Under the roof it was quiet, and bright from Corey's lamps. On the post by the entrance hung Ashot's sign, written in chalk: "SARKISIAN. SHOES. TAP-TAP." Over the next counter, Lottie's FISH. FRESH. ALWAYS. In the corner stood Roxanne's carnations.

Old Pete opened the ledger and wrote in it for a long time. Then he showed me.

"March twenty-second. Market built. Expenditure: hand pies, see D. Mazur. Bags, see Corey's meter. People: a lot. Balance: in the black."

He thought it over. Added:

"Came out even without me. Verify."

Mrs. Stern sat next to Mrs. Mazur, holding a mug of tea in both hands. She was looking at the road the black car had driven off on.

"I saw him by the fence when you went over," she said quietly. "I hadn't seen him in twenty years. He's gone gray."

"He didn't take the hand pie," I said.

"No, he didn't," Mrs. Stern agreed. "That's all right. Ephraim never took anything the first time either. You had to offer three times." She sipped her tea. "Glen always took it the third time."

I'd brought the hand pie back and set it down on the crate beside me. Cold. Cabbage.

I didn't eat it.

I wrapped it in a napkin and put it in my pocket. I don't know why, exactly.

Maybe so there'd be a third time.
