# Chapter 27. The Handbook

Keyes called on Wednesday, April eighth, at nine in the morning. I was sitting in the new booth straightening the earpiece on somebody's glasses, so I pinned the phone with my shoulder.

"Nagel? Keyes. The analysis came back."

"And?"

"Gold," said Keyes. "Ninety-nine point nine nine. Four nines. The lab guy called me separately. Not for the record, just because. Asked me where I got it. I said it was seized from a man who claims he collected it himself. The lab guy was quiet for a bit, and then he said, 'Then have him collect me some more. I make reference standards at that purity, and I have to have them shipped in from the capital.'"

"Is that good or bad?" I asked.

"For you, neither," said Keyes. "Large quantities starts at about sixty times your six grams. You're about as close to it as I am to retirement. There's no crime. I'm closing the case. The pea gets returned to you, signed for, and the silver too. No civil fine either: six grams, for personal use. As for your expert: his attorney sent in a statement saying the tip came from his client. That's a knowingly false report, if you want to go after him. Filing a complaint is your right."

"I don't want to," I said.

I put the glasses down.

"Thank you."

"Don't thank me. That's the law, not me." He paused. "But I've still got a question. Not about the case. Just one person to another."

"'Reshapes'?"

"'Reshapes,'" said Keyes. "Your attorney says you've got an open workshop on Sundays. A sign that says WE TEACH IN THE OPEN. That true?"

"It's true."

"Then I'll come," said Keyes. "As a private citizen. Sunday's my day off. My wife says I need a hobby."

---

He came. On Sunday the twelfth, at ten in the morning, in a plain jacket instead of a suit, with a paper sack of store-bought hand pies, because that, in his words, was "what you do when you're somebody's guest."

Mrs. Mazur looked at the store-bought hand pies the way Ms. Pratt looks at a tax return that's been filled out wrong. But she took them. And she set them on the table next to her own. An hour later nobody had eaten the store-bought ones, and hers were all gone. Keyes noticed and wrote something in his notepad. I don't know what.

He didn't ask anything. He sat on a crate by the wood stove and watched. He watched Ms. Pratt straighten a paper clip, saying out loud, "Paper clip. Straighten. Edge: the whole wire." He watched Mikey straighten a bent fork and then eat two hand pies in a row. He watched Bull listen to a piston with his palm on it, the hand with the ring, and say, "Ring's stuck. Second one from the bottom. I'll free it up." And then, more quietly, to himself: "If I had my own shop, I'd do this every day." He watched Ashot work a piece of leather until it was soft as a glove.

After two hours Keyes got up, came over to me and said quietly:

"Can everybody do this?"

"No. Not yet. A lot of people can. A little. If somebody teaches them."

"And me?"

I gave him a glass of water and a thermometer. He held the glass in his palms for three minutes. He wanted it to get colder. Zero. Exactly zero, like Val.

"So, no," said Keyes. And it seemed to me he wasn't disappointed; he was relieved. "Seventeen years of listening to liars. I guess I used up all my heat on them."

"Val's a zero too," I said. "And she understands more than anybody."

He looked at Val, who was standing by the workbench with one of Glen's notebooks, arguing with Greg about tempering temperatures. He nodded.

Before he left, I took a pen out of my pocket. Heavy, metal, with an engraving: "To Scott. Happy anniversary."

"This is yours," I said. "The cat took it. During the search. He keeps a stash in an old felt-lined boot. I found it that same evening. I meant to bring it back the next day, and I kept meaning to for a month. You'd have decided I'd reshaped it."

Keyes took the pen and looked at the engraving for a long time.

"I waited a month," he said. "I told my wife it was at the office. I told the office it was at home." He slid the pen into his inside pocket and buttoned it. "Tell your cat that a seizure without witnesses present is not permitted."

"I'll tell him. He won't listen."

"Nobody listens," Keyes agreed. And left.

At the door he turned around.

"When you write up how it's done," he said, "send it to me. I want to read where the edge is."

---

"When you write up."

That was the big question of April. Not "how": that much we were starting to know, a little at a time. But "whether to write it up at all." And if so, for whom.

On my table lay fifty-one notebooks. Professor Stern: thirty-one years of fear and formulas. Glen: twenty years of practice without a single "I'm afraid." Val: forty pages of excerpts and an outline for the paper. Ms. Pratt: instructions, eight points long. The Exchange chat, open now: three hundred people already. And my graph-paper notebook, with "Everywhere" underlined twice.

I did the math on a napkin. If everybody learns, my knives will sell for half as much. But then my neighbor will sharpen my knife for me himself, Greg will fix Mrs. Mazur's sheet pan himself, and half of what I work to pay for, I won't need to buy. I hadn't stopped being afraid. Cochran, Lenny, people I don't even know. But what three hundred people know, you can't carry out of a garage in two plaid shopping bags.

All of it could go in a safe. It could go to a journal, and then forty people with doctorates would read it, and everybody else would hear about it ten years later, on TV, in somebody's retelling. Or we could put it out there. All of it. For free. So that anybody in any town could open it up and read how to hold the glass and where the edge is.

We spent two Sundays deciding. At the end of the workshop, when the rings were coming back onto my hand and the wood stove was burning down.

---

The first to object was Ashot. And he objected in a way I couldn't find an answer to right away.

"If everybody can do it, who needs a cobbler?"

He said it without resentment. The way he'd name a price.

"I spent thirty years learning," said Ashot. "My father taught me, I taught Arsen, till he went off into medicine. It's mine. It's what I've got when I've got nothing else. And now anybody can open his phone, read it, squeeze a shoe in his hands, and he won't come to me."

The garage went quiet. Because this wasn't Glen with his fear. This was Ashot. One of ours.

Mrs. Mazur brushed the flour off her hands.

"Ashot," she said. "Everybody knows how to bake a pie. The recipe's in every newspaper. People still come to me."

"Because yours taste better."

"Because I listen to the dough," said Mrs. Mazur. "And newspapers don't write about that. And your handbook won't either. It'll say, 'Hold the glass for three minutes.' But how you hear leather, nobody's ever going to write that down. That's thirty years."

Ms. Pratt raised her hand, as if at a meeting.

"I will look at this as a revenue auditor," she said. "At present, shoe repair is expensive, because there are few cobblers and everybody's shoes wear out. If many people learn, simple repairs will get cheaper. That is true. Ashot Sarkisian will lose on the simple jobs. But the complicated ones, the ones that require precision, will get more expensive. Because precision equals understanding. And understanding cannot be downloaded to a phone." She adjusted her glasses. "That's Professor Stern. Volume thirty-one. He worked out there what would happen if everybody learned. He was afraid to publish it. In my view, it is simply economics."

Ashot thought about it.

"So heel tips get cheaper, and custom boots get more expensive?"

"More or less."

"I haven't made custom boots in ten years," said Ashot. "There was nobody to make them for." He paused. "All right. I'll make boots."

---

The second to object was Mikey. And that was worse.

"Glen's right," he said. Everybody turned around. Mikey went red, but he didn't look down. "About the bridge. On New Year's Eve I opened a garage. On a bet. Without any ring. With my bare hands. If I'd read all of it back then, it wouldn't have been a garage I opened."

"What, then?" asked Bull.

"I don't know," Mikey said honestly. "That's the whole thing. At fifteen you don't know what you'll open. You only know that you can."

Val closed Glen's notebook.

"Mikey," she said. "Letting a joint ease off and holding a joint are the same skill. With a different want behind it. You can't publish one without the other. If we keep quiet, then ten years from now some second Glen will find a third thimble, and he'll have 'ease off,' and everybody else will have nothing. If we publish, everybody will have 'hold.' On March twenty-second Glen tested it himself. On us."

"And what if somebody doesn't have both?" said Mikey. "Just 'ease off'?"

"Then nine hundred ninety-nine others will hold," said Val. "But only if they've been taught. That's why everybody has to be taught. Not just the reviewers."

Mikey thought for a long time.

"Then it goes first," he said. "Point one. Not how. Why. And when not to. The way Tim told me after the garage. Otherwise I'm against it."

"That will be point one," said Ms. Pratt. "I'll see to it."

---

The third was Corey.

He didn't object. He came on the second Sunday, looked over the draft Val had printed out at the department on written-off paper, found the section on energy and turned pale.

"Who wrote this?" he asked.

"I did," I said.

"'You can draw energy from a wall outlet if you're wearing rubber boots,'" Corey read out loud. "Timothy. Do you understand that the first genius to read this will try it in the bathtub? Standing in water? Holding on to the faucet?"

"It says 'in boots.'"

"He'll be in boots. In the bathtub." Corey sat down on a crate. "No. The electricity part, I'm writing. Myself. And the first line is going to be: 'Never draw current without ground-fault protection. Never. Not once. Not even if you really need to.' The second: 'Both wires. One wire only if you'd like to find out how fast they can get you to the ER.' The third: 'Without a thimble or a ring, don't draw current at all. A hand is not a thimble.' And the fourth: 'The bare tips touch the metal of the thimble only, never skin. The protection saves you from the ground. If the current goes from one wire to the other through your hands, nothing will save you.' And a diagram. I'll draw the diagram."

"Corey, that's science fiction," said Bull. "There are only two thimbles."

"There are nine rings," said Corey. "And everybody's got outlets. In a year there'll be ninety rings, and every last one of them will want to do it 'like Timothy.' I'm writing this section. Otherwise my name doesn't go on it."

It took him four evenings to write. It was the shortest section in the whole handbook, and the easiest to understand. Ms. Pratt said it was the first text in her twenty years of service in which she hadn't found a single error, and that it even worried her a little.

---

We wrote it at Mrs. Mazur's. Every evening after the market, at her kitchen table, between the jam and the sugar bowl. Mikey with the laptop, Val with the printouts, Ms. Pratt with a red pen. Other people came and went, but those three were always there. And me.

Writing turned out to be harder than teaching. And teaching is harder than doing. That much I already knew.

Because everybody wrote in their own way.

Ms. Pratt wrote like a report: "Point five. Before acting, the student states out loud the object, the intent and the edge. Example: 'Paper clip. Straighten. Edge: the whole wire.'"

Val wrote like a paper: "The observed decrease in water temperature in the control group, in the absence of any external source of cold, suggests…"

Ashot dictated a chapter on leather. Mikey typed. The chapter was called "How to Haggle with Leather," and its first sentence was: "Leather isn't the enemy. Leather's a customer. First listen to what it wants, then tell it what you can do."

Mrs. Mazur refused to write. She said, "I don't know how to write, I know how to bake." So Mikey set his phone in front of her, recording, and she talked for an hour and a half about how to mix the dough for a napoleon. Not one word about the power. Only about dough: when it's cold, when it's nervous, when it "asks for a rest." Val transcribed it afterward, read it, and said it was the best chapter.

Bull wrote one page. About how to listen to an engine. The page ended like this: "If you can't hear it, don't touch it. The engine will wait. The engine always waits. You're the one in a hurry."

And I wrote about what you must not do. About the faucet I'd sealed shut the very first night. About the buttons that flew off without asking. About the chair whose leg turned to charcoal. About the latch on Mikey's phone. About the stairwell with no lights and the speck of gold. About the bridge Glen is afraid of. And about the living. Above all, about the living. Rule Six. Mine. Never on a person or an animal, not even with consent, until you understand what you're doing. And we won't understand for a very long time yet.

And next to it, Glen's entry. From twenty years ago. The yeast dough that stopped rising. "Do not repeat." Underlined twice.

We put it in the section whole. With the numbers.

Old Pete worked out what the handbook had cost. Paper from the department: written off. Laptop: Mikey's. Electricity: D. Mazur's. Hand pies: D. Mazur's. Tea: everybody's.

"Cost: zero," he wrote. "Price to the reader: zero. Profit: not anticipated."

---

That left one question. The smallest and the hardest.

Glen.

Val wanted him as a coauthor. Not because it would look nice, but because it was honest: half the tables in the "Energy" section came from his notebooks. The welding that's three times cheaper. Sapphire, stage by stage. Kilowatt-hours he'd been counting back when I was still counting in pierogi.

"I won't put his name on it without his consent," said Val. "That's against the rules. And it would be exactly what he did with the notebooks: taking without asking."

Glen's number had been in my notebook since December. Since that bolt.

I put it off for two weeks. On Monday the twentieth I texted him:

"Glen, this is Timothy. We're publishing everything. Openly, for free, for everybody. The handbook and the paper. We'd like to use your notes (energy, sapphire, the dough) and put your name on it. Without your consent we won't do either one. Please answer yes or no."

He didn't answer.

Not on Monday, not on Tuesday, not on Wednesday. I checked my phone so often that Val finally took it away from me and put it in the freezer with my pierogi, "to cool off."

On Thursday the twenty-third, a paper sack was hanging on my door handle. No stamp, no address. Somebody had hung it there by hand. Inside was a strip of heavy paper. The same kind.

Small, even handwriting.

"Yes. Last."

Val read it and held the strip between her fingers for a long time.

"Last," she said. "For twenty years he wanted to be the only one. And now: last."

"Is that good?"

"It's honest," said Val. "He knows he got here after everybody else. Even though he knows more than everybody else."

Under the strip, in the sack, there was one more thing. A notebook. Black, thick, the twenty-first. This year's. About a third of it filled. The last entry was from Tuesday:

"Tried holding the joint instead of letting it ease off. My own fence. One post. Difficult. Unfamiliar. It worked. Cost lower than for easing off. Strange. Verify further."

---

We decided to publish on the night before Monday, April twenty-seventh. Why at night, nobody could remember. I think Mikey said the server was less busy at night. I think Val said the paper had to go to the journal before Monday anyway. I think Old Pete said, "So nobody jinxes it."

By midnight everybody was sitting in Mrs. Mazur's kitchen. The ones who'd written, and the ones who'd held joints. Mrs. Mazur's kitchen seats six, and there were nineteen of us. Mrs. Stern on the only real chair, everybody else on stools, on the windowsill, on the floor; Mikey sat on top of the fridge. Goldie stood in the doorway, because there was nowhere else.

Goldie hadn't come just to watch. She'd come with paperwork.

"Before anybody clicks," she said, "the legal side. Three things."

Everybody went quiet. Goldie didn't say much, and what she said was to the point, and when she spoke, even Old Pete put down his pen.

"One. We're putting the handbook out under an open license. That means anyone can read it, copy it, translate it and teach from it. For free. Nobody, not us and not anyone else, can turn around later and say it's theirs and demand money for it."

"What about a patent?" asked Bull. "I heard you take out a patent on inventions."

"A patent is a monopoly for twenty years," said Goldie. "Twenty years for one owner. We've already seen what that looks like. Out in the pine forest. No. And once something is published, it isn't new anymore. Nobody can patent it. Not even my father. Especially not my father."

"Two," she went on. "Responsibility. At the very beginning, on the first page: Mikey's point one, 'Why, and when not to.' And Corey's section. And Ms. Pratt's eight points. If somebody reads this and does something stupid (and somebody will), we have to know that we warned them. Clearly, on the first page, not in the fine print."

"Three," said Goldie, and looked at Mrs. Stern. "The notebooks are Timothy's; you gave them to him. But the rights to what's written in them are yours: you're the heir. I need your signature."

Mrs. Stern took the pen. Looked at the paper. Didn't bother reading it.

"He was afraid for thirty years," she said. "That's enough to go around. Where do I sign?"

She signed. In big letters, with a flourish.

"And a fourth," said Goldie. "Michael is a minor. Lana, I need your signature too."

Mikey's mom, Lana, who'd come straight from her shift in her jacket from the bread plant, took the pen.

"Under rule four," she said. "You answer for him under rule three."

Val hadn't said anything this whole time. Then she said, to nobody in particular:

"The journal might not take it. They'll say it's already been published."

"And then what?" I asked.

"So be it," said Val. "Somebody has to print the zeros too. And the ones."

---

At five to two, Mikey set the laptop on the table, between the jam and the sugar bowl. On the screen was a page. Plain, white. At the top was the title, which we'd argued about for a week and finally picked the most boring one, because Ms. Pratt said nobody steals boring: *Reshaping Matter by Hand. A Practical Manual. First Edition.*

Below it, the authors. Mikey had lined them up in a column, like names in a teacher's grade book:

"Ephraim Stern, Valerie Finch, Timothy Nagel, Nina Pratt, Dorothy Mazur, Ashot Sarkisian, George Bykowski, Michael Kravitz, Corey Lapp ('Electricity' section), Glen Rusk."

Glen last. The way he'd asked.

At the bottom, one button. "Publish."

"Who's going to click it?" asked Mikey.

Everybody looked at me.

"No," I said. "Not me."

"Why not?" Val was surprised.

"Because if I click it, it'll be 'Nagel published.' And this isn't Nagel. This is everybody."

"Then who?"

I looked at Mrs. Mazur. She was standing by the stove in her apron, wiping her hands on a dish towel. Her hands are always cold.

"Mrs. Mazur," I said. "It's your kitchen."

Mrs. Mazur looked at the laptop the way she'd look at a dough she didn't know yet.

"Where do I poke it?"

"Right here," Mikey showed her. "On the button."

Mrs. Mazur came over. She looked at the screen. She thought. Then she said, not to us, but to the world in general:

"Well," she said to the laptop, the way she'd talk to dough. "Go on. Rise."

And she clicked.

With a finger that still had flour on it even after the dish towel. It left a white mark on the touchpad.

The page blinked. A message came up: "Published."

The clock said two exactly.

Nobody said anything. For about ten seconds it was completely quiet, except for the fridge humming under Mikey. Then Old Pete opened his ledger and wrote something. He showed it to me later.

"April twenty-seventh, two a.m. Published. Income: everything. Expenditure: everything. Balance: everybody's."

---

Val sent the paper to the journal at two-twenty. From the same laptop, with the flour mark on the touchpad. Stern, Finch, Nagel, Rusk. Mrs. Stern in the acknowledgments, in big print, the way I'd told Val in February at the bus stop.

At two-forty the first comment came in. Mikey read it out loud:

"From the other side of the map, where it's already morning. Read it. Held a glass. Minus two tenths. What just happened???"

"Three question marks," said Ms. Pratt. "Unprofessional."

"It's a start," said Val.

By three there were eleven. Saltport. Highridge. Some little town out in the country, where a man had gotten a zero and was asking whether that was normal. Mikey answered him himself, without asking us: "It's normal. One of our coauthors got a zero too. And understands more than anybody."

Val read it over his shoulder and didn't say anything. She just took off her glasses.

---

People started leaving around four. Mrs. Stern went home by taxi. Goldie walked her down to the car and didn't come back. Mikey said later he'd seen them out front of the building: they were standing there talking about something, and Goldie was laughing, and Mrs. Stern had her arm through Goldie's.

Mrs. Mazur kicked us out last.

"Go to bed," she said. "Both of you. I'll do the dishes myself."

We went down to my floor, the fourth. One flight. Val went first, holding the railing, and I watched her back.

She had on her blue sweater with the wide neck, stretched out at the elbows: the one she'd been wearing in the lab in December, only now it had flour on it too, from Mrs. Mazur's. She'd pinned her hair up with a pencil to keep it out of the way, and the pencil was still sticking out. Jeans, old sneakers. Nothing special. Just ordinary Val at four in the morning.

At my door she stopped.

"Still haven't fixed the stool?"

"Still haven't."

"Fix it."

"Now?"

"No," said Val. "Not now."

We went in. Lugnut looked at us from the top of the fridge, decided we hadn't brought anything shiny, and turned away.

Val pulled her sweater over her head and got stuck: the pencil had caught on the neck. We got it off together, like a panel off the frame. There was flour on her eyelashes. Under the sweater she had on a white T-shirt with the logo of some chemistry conference from ten years ago, and under the T-shirt (I saw when she raised her arms) a dark blue bra. Plain, cotton, no lace, with a little bow in the middle that somebody had obviously sewn on, and not at the factory: the thread was a different color.

I looked at the bow. Val looked at where I was looking.

"I sewed it on myself," she said. "The old one came off. It's from a different one. Don't ask."

"I'm not asking."

"You're looking."

"I'm looking."

The thimble on my finger was warm, but only with my own warmth: a hand is a hand. The snaps on my shirt didn't click. I checked, in my head, one at a time. The first time, in this kitchen, against the wall, everything had come raining off her blouse and her jacket, mine had let go as powder, and Lugnut had even added one of his own to the pile, from his stash. Now they held. Because I'd learned. Not to do: to hold.

She yawned in the middle of my silence and said, "That's not you. That's two o'clock this morning." For the first time in half a year I had nothing to hide from her and no valve to hold.

"Tim," said Val. "Do you want to?"

"I do," I said. "And you?"

"I asked first," she said. "That means yes."

She took off her glasses and set them on the windowsill. Next to my graph-paper notebook, with "Everywhere" underlined twice.

And the rest I'm not going to tell. Because that was ours too. Not everything has to be published.

---

I woke up at eleven. Val was asleep facing the wall, in my T-shirt, and on the pillow next to her lay the pencil.

Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill. In front of him lay Val's glasses.

He was looking at them. They were shining.

I got up, took the glasses before he could make up his mind, and put them in my shirt pocket. Lugnut gave me a reproachful look and went off to the top of the fridge.

I took off the thimble and set it on the windowsill. For the first time in half a year: just like that, not to hide it. Right where the glasses had been a moment ago. Lugnut came back down from the fridge, sniffed the thimble and didn't touch it. The glasses had interested him more. I figured he was probably offended.

The stool wobbled. The landlady's stool, in the landlady's apartment: what's really mine here is the notebooks and the cat. One of its legs had been cracked since October, and I'd never once put the thimble to it, because I knew how that went with wood. The old chair's leg had turned to charcoal, light as a cracker, and you can't do a thing with that: what's gone off as smoke isn't in it anymore. I knocked out the cracked leg, took a block from one of Mikey's pallets, whittled it with a knife, fitted it, drove it in. By hand. Forty minutes, not a single hand pie. The leg stood firm. A little lighter than the others.

And I had the feeling that there was one less debt. Pen returned, stool fixed. Bull: Fridays, as always.

Then I checked my phone. Under the handbook there were four hundred comments. A school a couple of counties over, where a chemistry teacher had repeated the glasses with her whole class: three out of twenty-eight, minus three tenths, like Mikey in December. Without their parents' permission. Ms. Pratt said the point had been violated on the very first day, and wrote it down. There were others too: "Scam. Where do I buy a ring?", "Cult.", "This is an ad for thermometers." Old Pete read that last one and said, "They do it on purpose." A retiree from somewhere down south: "Held a paper clip. Nothing happened. But thank you for the section on electricity, now my wife can stop worrying."

Mom called. "Tim, Aunt Wanda showed me some kind of manual on her phone. Your name's on it. Third." A pause. "What, you didn't sleep at home last night?"

I told her I had. That was the truth. Val was home too.

I turned off my phone. I looked at Val. At Lugnut. At the stool, standing on four legs.

I opened the graph-paper notebook and wrote under "Everywhere":

"Gave it away. Still have it all."
