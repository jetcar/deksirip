# Chapter 8. The Lab Tech

At six o'clock on Wednesday evening I was sitting on a bus across the street from the Poly, looking at my watch.

I didn't go in. I should say that right away, so it won't be awkward later. I'd worked all day at the market and made eight bags, which left me forty-three short for Friday, and at five-thirty I pulled down the shutter, grabbed my backpack and got on the bus that went out toward the Poly.

I rode it to the Polytechnic stop. I looked out the window at the peeling columns. At the lit windows on the third floor: somewhere up there, at the end of a hallway that smelled like rotten eggs, a woman with a pencil in her hair was setting a beaker of fresh solution on her bench and checking her camera.

And I didn't get off.

The bus shut its doors and pulled away. I sat by the window and hated myself with that calm, familiar hatred you feel for yourself when you've once again failed to call your mom on Wednesday like you promised.

I rode to the end of the line, transferred to a bus going back and got home a little after seven. I boiled some pierogi. Ate them. Sat down on my iron chair and started reading the ninth volume of Professor Stern's notebooks, so I wouldn't have to think.

I had to think anyway.

Here's what I thought. If I'd gone in, she would have seen me. On camera. On the record. And after that I wouldn't be a man with a secret anymore. I'd be a man whose secret somebody else knew. Professor Stern had spent thirty years making sure that happened as rarely as possible. Glen had spent twenty years the same way. Probably. And I, eight days in, had very nearly caved to the first lab tech who told me, "You're lying."

On the other hand, I'd written it in my own notebook: "do it where people can see." And then I hadn't gone to the place where people would see.

So my rules were braver than I was. That stung.

---

At nine that night the doorbell rang.

I figured it was Mrs. Mazur with hand pies. But Mrs. Mazur knocks; she doesn't ring. And she knocks like the door owes her money. This was the bell. Once. Long. Insistent.

I opened the door. It opened on the first try, by the way, smoothly, the way doors do for normal people. That still made me happy.

Val was standing on the threshold.

No lab coat. A jacket, jeans, a scarf wound up to her chin. The pencil in her hair was where it belonged. Over her shoulder hung a big bag with a tripod sticking out of it. In her hand was a thermos.

"You didn't come," she said.

"I didn't come," I agreed.

"I waited until seven-thirty. Then I made coffee. Then I drank it. Then I decided you were a coward."

"I am a coward."

"That's what I decided," said Val. "And then I thought: a coward wouldn't have carried a lead ball in a sock into a chemistry department. A coward doesn't carry anything anywhere. So you're not a coward. So you're afraid of something. Those are two different things. And your ball, by the way, is cooling down. Slowly. I like that even less than I like you."

She looked past me, into the apartment. At the chair made out of a bed. At the shelf of library books. At the knives gleaming on the magnetic strip.

"May I?" she asked. "I won't film anything without permission. For now."

"How did you find me?"

"Mrs. Nicholls at the library," said Val. "She's my aunt. Not by blood, but close enough. I called her at home and told her you owed me a book. She looked you up by your library card. She said you're the first person in years who's come back to read and not just to look something up. She had nothing but nice things to say about you."

"They're not supposed to give out readers' addresses."

"They're not," Val agreed. "That's why I said you owed me. Aunt Tess never forgives a debt." She paused. "And I really do think you owe me, by the way. A crystal, for starters."

I stepped aside, and she came in.

---

Lugnut appeared from the other room immediately, as if he'd been waiting. He circled Val, sniffed her bag, sniffed her thermos, sniffed her boots, and then sat down in front of her and stared up at her face. Or not exactly her face. A little higher.

"He's looking at my pencil," said Val.

"He looks at anything shiny."

"A pencil isn't shiny."

"It's got a metal band around the eraser," I said. "Lugnut thinks that counts."

Val looked at the cat. The cat looked at her. It was like two people meeting who'd realized at first sight that they didn't trust each other but did respect each other.

"Good cat," said Val. "Smart. Another one who steals things that don't belong to him."

"I didn't steal anything."

"You stole three weeks of crystal growth from me in two seconds," she said. "That's worse than stealing. That's devaluation."

She set her bag on the table, unzipped it and started taking things out. One at a time, carefully, the way a surgeon lays out instruments. A beaker wrapped in a dish towel. The thermos, which turned out to hold not coffee but copper sulfate solution, warm and deep blue. A little electronic scale, more precise than my kitchen one, good to a hundredth of a gram. Two thermometers, not the kind you take your temperature with but real lab thermometers, long, with a thin thread of liquid inside. An alcohol burner. Some glass tubes and a coil of steel wire. An old phone and a tripod for it. A thick graph-paper notebook. And three spare pencils. Lugnut followed every one of them with his eyes.

"I figured," said Val, "that if the test subject won't come to the lab, the lab will come to the test subject. In a thermos."

"That's not how the saying goes."

"I'm a chemist, not an English major."

---

We ran the experiment in my kitchen, under my lamp, in front of her camera.

She poured the solution into the beaker. She lowered a thread into it with a tiny seed crystal on the end, a little blue speck. She set the phone on the tripod, started recording and told the camera the date, the time, and "Experiment number one. Witness, Valerie Finch. Subject, Timothy Nagel, Mr. Fix-Everything." Then she looked at me.

"Go ahead."

I put on the thimble. Val was watching my hand very closely, but not the thimble; I don't think she'd even noticed it yet. She was watching my fingers. Waiting for the trick.

I dipped the edge of the thimble into the solution, the same as last time.

The solution was warm and supersaturated, full of molecules that wanted to fall into line. I thought, "Okay, go on." And they went.

In two seconds the crystal grew to the size of a cherry. The solution went pale.

Val didn't move. Then she slowly reached out, stopped the recording and scrolled back to watch it again. She watched it. Then she looked at the crystal. Then at me.

"Again," she said quietly.

"The solution's done. Everything extra already—"

"I know. I brought more."

She took a second thermos out of her bag.

---

The next four hours were the strangest of my life. Stranger than the night of the spoons. Stranger than the chase through the garages.

Val was testing me.

She did it the way, I imagine, people test a new drug or a new alloy: methodically, point by point, writing everything down in her notebook. She had a list. She'd written it ahead of time, while she was waiting for me at the department: three pages in small handwriting, numbered.

Number one was mass. She weighed the beaker with the solution and the seed crystal before the experiment. Afterward she took out the crystal and weighed it, then weighed the beaker with whatever was left in it. She added the numbers up. They matched to a hundredth of a gram.

"Good," she said. "You're not violating conservation of mass. That's a relief. I was afraid I'd have to rewrite the textbook."

Number two was touch. She sealed a piece of steel wire inside a glass tube. She'd brought the alcohol burner along, and she did it right there in my kitchen in half a minute, and I thought: she can do things with her hands that I can't. Then she held the tube out to me.

"Bend the wire. Without touching it."

I took the tube. Through the glass I could feel the steel, faintly, the way you feel something through a glove. But I couldn't bend it. I pulled, I asked, the thimble got warm, and the wire didn't budge.

"I can't," I said. "Through glass I can only feel it. I can only reshape what I'm touching. Or, well, whatever's connected to what I'm touching by one solid piece of metal."

Val wrote it down. She wrote fast, without looking at the page.

"That's good," she said. "That's very good."

"Why?"

"Because it's a limitation. Tricks without limitations are just tricks. You've got a limitation. So it's real."

Number three was energy. That was the most interesting part, because I'd thought about it a lot myself. Val tucked one of her lab thermometers under my arm, like a fever thermometer, and asked me to weld two spoons together. I welded them. My temperature went up almost a degree, and I got so hungry I could have eaten the spoons. Then she filled a big pot with cold water from the tap, stuck the second thermometer in it, put my left hand on the pot and asked me to weld two pieces of copper wire, thin as hairs.

"Take it from here," she said. "If you know how."

I knew how. The water in the pot went from tap-cold to nearly freezing; a thin skin of ice formed around the edge, and the outside of the pot fogged over. My temperature didn't go up. I was barely hungry at all.

"Three liters, about fifteen degrees, plus a little ice," Val muttered over her notebook. "Seventy calories or so. That's plenty for the wires. For the spoons it'd take a bathtub." She tapped her pen on the page. "But the spoons don't add up. To melt the seam between two spoons, a couple of calories would do it. You're paying fifteen hundred. They're not in the spoons. They're not in you; the thermometer would have caught that. So where did they go?"

I didn't know. I wrote it down for myself too: "Where to?" For the second time.

Val sat over her notebook, working something out. For a long time. Then she looked up.

"Timothy," she said. "Do you understand what you just did?"

"Welded some spoons."

"You took heat out of water that was colder than this kitchen, and you turned it into work. With no engine. Not even a refrigerator."

"I've got a refrigerator," I said. "A quiet one."

"Not that kind! In thermodynamics, the refrigerator is the cold side, the place you dump heat. Heat flows from hot to cold all by itself, and you can only squeeze work out of it along the way. But you squeezed it out of the cold side. That's a perpetual motion machine of the second kind. The second law of thermodynamics. You can't break it. Not ever. Not anywhere. And you…" She looked at me with completely crazy eyes. "Maybe you're not breaking the law. Maybe this… this thing of yours… is dumping entropy somewhere. Someplace. I don't know where. But if I find out, that's the top prize in physics. No. Three top prizes. Never mind, it doesn't matter, I'm joking. Timothy. What's that on your finger?"

She'd finally noticed.

---

I spent a long time deciding what to tell her.

I sat on my iron chair across from a woman I'd seen exactly twice in my life, a woman with gray eyes, purple stains on her fingers and a notebook with three pages of questions. On the table stood thermoses, beakers, the scale, crystals, the welded spoons and two copper wires. Lugnut was asleep on the shelf, holding down one of her spare pencils with his paw. It was past midnight.

I thought: if I tell her, there's no going back. If I don't, she'll leave and maybe never come back. And I'll be left with thirty-one notebooks I only half understand, a thimble I use by guesswork and questions nobody can answer.

I needed somebody who knew chemistry. Val knew chemistry. I needed somebody who wouldn't want to sell it. Val, by all appearances, wanted only one thing: to understand.

And one more thing. I just didn't want her to leave.

I took off the thimble and set it on the table between us.

"This," I said. "It came to me from a man. Or rather, from his widow. That man studied it for thirty years and kept a journal. I have the journal. Thirty-one notebooks."

Val looked at the thimble. She didn't touch it.

"What was his name?" she asked.

"Stern. Professor Ephraim Stern."

Val slowly raised her eyes.

"Stern," she repeated. "From solid-state physics. He taught an elective for us senior year. On defects in crystals. People said he was nuts. That he had a lab at home nobody knew about. That he'd turned down three grants because he didn't want to answer to anybody." She paused. "In his class I got the only B of my life. He said I jump to conclusions."

"Was he right?"

"Of course he was right," said Val, annoyed. "He was always right. It was unbearable."

She reached for the thimble. Stopped. Looked at me.

"May I?"

"Go ahead."

She picked it up. Put it on her finger. Waited.

I could see her waiting. Listening. I watched her face go from concentration to hope, then to bewilderment, then to disappointment.

"Nothing," she said.

"Nothing," I said. "Mrs. Stern doesn't get anything either. The professor wrote that it's like an ear for music. Some people have it, some don't."

Val took off the thimble and set it back down. Carefully, the way you give back something that belongs to somebody else and has hurt your feelings, even though that's not your fault.

I put it back on. Without it I felt like I'd lost my glasses.

"It's not fair," she said. "Ten years of chemistry. I know more about matter than you'll learn in your whole life. And that thing picks a repairman from a booth."

"It doesn't pick," I said. "It just… hears. But you're the one who knows. Those are two different things. And it looks like you need both."

Val gave me a long, careful look.

"There," she said. "That was smart, what you just said. I didn't see that coming."

---

We made our deal at one in the morning, in my kitchen, over cold thermoses.

The idea was hers, but I could tell right away it was the right one. Val knows chemistry. I don't. I need chemistry to understand what I'm touching, and understanding, as Professor Stern wrote and as I'd checked for myself on the paper clip and the watch, makes every miracle cheaper. So every chemistry lesson saves me pierogi. Directly, literally. You can do the math.

And Val's department has a graveyard. That's what she called it. An analytical balance under its glass cover that broke five years ago. A fume hood whose fan doesn't work. A rotary evaporator (I didn't even know what that was) that leaks. A drying oven with no thermostat. A centrifuge that shakes so hard people are afraid to turn it on. There's no money for repairs. There's even less money for new equipment. The department runs on whatever's left over from the last century, and every year there's less of it left.

"Every broken instrument is experiments nobody will run," said Val. "Papers nobody will write. Students who'll never see how it really works. It all just gathers dust in a corner under a cover. Like money sitting in a safe that you can't spend."

"Like gold without papers," I said.

"What?"

"Nothing. I'll tell you later."

The deal was simple. I fix the department's equipment in the evenings, when nobody's around. She teaches me chemistry on those same evenings, at the same table. No money. She doesn't have any, the department doesn't have any, and I have less than none.

"An hour of repairs for an hour of chemistry?" asked Val.

I thought about it. Then I shook my head.

"No. That's not right."

"Why not?"

"Because your hour of chemistry is worth more to me than my hour of repairs is to you. If I understand how steel works, I'll fix things three times faster and three times cheaper. Everything, for everybody, forever. But if I fix your balance, that's one balance."

Val looked at me like a student who'd suddenly said something on an exam that wasn't in the textbook.

"You're bargaining against yourself," she said.

"I'm bargaining honestly."

"Two hours of repairs for one hour of chemistry," I said. "And the first balance is on the house. For the crystal."

"Same thing," said Val. "Fine. Two for one. But you still owe me a crystal. Grown slowly. So I have time to watch." She reached her hand across the table. "Deal."

I shook her hand.

Her hand was warm and dry, with a little callus on the middle finger from a pen, like everybody who writes a lot. For some reason I held on to it longer than I needed to. She noticed. And she didn't pull it away.

---

I don't know which of us started it. Probably her. No. Probably me. No. Honestly, I don't know.

We were sitting at the table, holding hands over the thermoses, and suddenly I saw that she had a small purple spot on her cheekbone, like the ones on her fingers. She must have rubbed her face while she was working. I said, "You've got something…" and pointed at my own cheekbone. She said, "Where?" and rubbed the wrong spot. I said, "No, a little higher." She rubbed the wrong spot again. I reached out with my free hand, the left one, the one without the thimble, and wiped the spot away with my thumb.

And I didn't take my hand back.

Val was looking at me. Very close. Her eyes were gray, with a dark rim, and there was no suspicion in them and no mockery. There was something else entirely. Something that suddenly made my mouth go dry.

"Timothy," she said quietly. "Are you going to kiss me, or is this another experiment?"

"May I?" I asked.

It was a dumb question. But it seemed important to ask it.

Val tilted her head a little, as if she were listening for something, the same way she'd listened to the thimble.

"You may," she said. "In fact, you should. For the sake of experimental rigor."

And I kissed her.

---

At first it was a careful kiss. The kind you give when you're not sure yet. Her lips were warm and a little bitter from the coffee. Then she put her hand on the back of my neck, and the kiss stopped being careful. She kissed the way she worked: focused, hungry, serious, with no ceremony at all. I forgot about the thermoses, the thimble, the forty-three bags by Friday. I stood up; she stood up. The iron chair scraped back with a crash. We ended up against the wall. Her hands were under my shirt, on my back, cool. Mine were on her waist, under the jacket she'd never taken off.

And then I heard a sound.

Quiet, dry, rapid. Like somebody spilling a handful of dried peas.

Click. Click-click. Click.

Val pulled back. We both looked down.

There were buttons on the floor at our feet. Hers, from her blouse: small, mother-of-pearl, whole and clean, as if they'd just come from the store. From her jacket, two metal ones, their little loops pried open. And of mine, white and plastic, all that was left was white powder on my shirt, like the latch on Mikey's phone. I still didn't understand plastic. Everything between us had let go of whatever was holding it.

And Lugnut came out of the other room with a button in his teeth from his stash. He added it to the pile, like somebody chipping in.

Then the hunger hit me, as if I'd welded another two spoons. The power had taken what it wanted, on its own and without asking.

My shirt fell open. So did her blouse.

We stood there looking at the floor.

And then Val started to laugh.

She laughed so hard she slid down the wall to the floor, right into the buttons, and sat there holding her stomach, her blouse hanging open, the pencil slipping out of her hair and dangling from one strand. I stood over her and laughed too, even though at the same time I was horribly embarrassed.

"Was that…" she gasped. "Was that you?"

"Me."

"On purpose?"

"No! No, of course not. I didn't even… I didn't ask for anything!"

"It works on its own?" Val asked, instantly done laughing. The scientist in her always woke up faster than the rest of her.

"Before, only when I asked."

"Then you asked," said Val. "Just not in words."

"I don't know. I didn't do anything. I just…" I stopped.

"Just what?"

"Just really wanted you to be wearing a little less," I said honestly. And I turned so red you could probably see it from the building across the street.

Val looked up at me from the floor, sitting in a heap of buttons. She looked for a long time. Then she said:

"Timothy. That's the strangest compliment anybody's ever paid me. And the most honest."

She held out her hand. I helped her up.

---

Afterward we stood in the middle of the kitchen, both of us with our shirts hanging open, and talked. That was strange too, but it felt very right.

I said this wasn't okay. That I hadn't asked. That the power had done it without her consent, and that was wrong, even if it was only buttons. That I have a rule: don't touch the living. And even more so, don't touch what a person is wearing, not without permission.

Val listened very seriously.

"That's a good rule," she said. "Keep it. Please."

"I will."

"As for the buttons…" She looked at the floor and almost laughed again. "The buttons I'll allow. Retroactively. Once. But in general, yes. Ask. It matters to me."

"I'll ask."

"And one more thing," said Val. She came up very close and put her palms on my chest, where the shirt had fallen open. Her fingers were cool, and I could feel my heart pounding under them. "I want you to know something. I really want to keep going. Really. You have no idea how much."

"But?" I said.

"But first I want to understand who I'd be keeping going with," she said. "I jump to conclusions. Professor Stern gave me a B for it. I don't want to jump with you. You're too interesting an experiment to ruin by rushing."

I laughed. So did she. And then she kissed me one more time, slowly, for a long time, until I stopped being sure where the kitchen ended, and stepped back.

Nothing fell. I was holding the valve in my head with everything I had.

"See?" said Val. "You're learning."

---

She didn't leave.

It was two in the morning, the buses had stopped running, and a cab to the other side of town would have cost her a day and a half of a lab tech's pay. We sat back down at the table. I gave her one of Professor Stern's notebooks, the first one, the gray one. She read. I gathered the buttons into the coffee can, the same one the lead ball used to live in. Lugnut sat on the shelf and glared at the can with the hatred of somebody whose collection has been confiscated.

Around four, Val fell asleep right over the notebook, her head on her arms. The pencil slid out of her hair and rolled across the table.

Lugnut jumped down from the shelf, walked over, picked the pencil up in his teeth and looked at me.

"No," I whispered.

Lugnut thought it over. He put the pencil back. He walked off.

It was the first time in his life that Lugnut had ever returned something shiny. I decided he'd understood too: this one wasn't his. It belonged to somebody.

I covered Val with my work jacket. The one without buttons. And I sat down across from her on the iron chair and watched her sleep and thought.

I thought that today, for the first time, I hadn't gone where I'd promised to go and had still ended up where I needed to be. That the knowledge I didn't have had come to me on its own, in a bag with a tripod sticking out of it. That there wasn't a single bag of pierogi in this deal, and somehow I'd come out ahead. And so, it seemed, had she. How can both people come out ahead if nobody paid anybody? I couldn't come up with an answer, so I wrote down the question.

And I thought about the buttons. About how the power listens not only to what I ask for but to what I want. And that is much more dangerous.

I looked at my hands. Ordinary hands.

I felt very scared and very happy, both at once.

I think that's what they call falling in love.

I wrote in my notebook, quietly, so I wouldn't wake her: "Deal with Val. Chemistry for repairs. Buttons: from feelings, without asking. Why? Ask Professor Stern." Then I looked at the last words. Professor Stern was dead. But I had thirty-one notebooks and a woman who knew how to read them.

I crossed out "Professor Stern" and wrote above it: "Val."

Then I thought for a while and added, at the very bottom, in tiny letters, the way you write things in the margins at school that aren't meant for the teacher:

"Gray eyes. With a dark rim."

In the morning Val woke up, looked at me, then at the jacket on her shoulders, then at the can full of buttons, and said:

"I hope you know how to sew."

"I do," I said. "By hand."

"Good," said Val. "By hand, I like that better."
