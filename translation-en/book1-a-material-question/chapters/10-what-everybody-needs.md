# Chapter 10. What Everybody Needs

On Saturday I didn't open the booth.

Okay, technically I opened it: I raised the shutter, the pigeons took off, everything the way it's supposed to be. But I didn't sit down behind the counter. I flipped the sign on the counter to BACK IN AN HOUR, picked up my notebook and went for a walk around the market.

It was a strange feeling. For twelve years I'd sat in that booth, and the market had come to me on its own: one person at a time, one broken thing at a time. I knew the market the way a cashier knows it: by faces, by hands, by what's in the shopping bag. But I'd never once walked through it to ask.

I started with the meat aisle, because it was closest.

---

Greg the butcher is huge and bald, in a white apron that never has any blood on it, because, as he puts it, he "works clean." I'd fixed his scale a hundred times and never talked to him about anything but the scale.

"Greg," I said. "Can I ask you something? In your work, what breaks the most? Or gets in your way?"

Greg looked at me over the hog carcass he was cutting up.

"What, you're taking a survey?"

"Sort of."

"For the tax office?"

"For myself."

Greg thought about it. Then he set his cleaver down on the block and wiped his hands.

"Knives," he said. "Knives go dull. I sharpen them every morning. Every morning, half an hour. Whetstone, honing steel, strop. Then by lunch it's dull again. So I sharpen it again. That's not breaking, that's just…" He hunted for the word. "Life. Knife goes dull, you sharpen it. Like shaving."

"And if it didn't go dull?"

Greg gave me the look you give a kid who's asked why people can't just live without sleep.

"There's no such thing," he said.

"But if there were?"

Greg was quiet. Then he said, very seriously, in a completely different voice:

"Tim. I've been sharpening knives half an hour a day for thirty years. You know how much that is? That's…" He started counting, moving his lips, and gave up. "That's a lot. If a knife didn't go dull, I'd spend that half hour sleeping. Every day. Thirty years."

I wrote in my notebook: "Greg. Knives. If they didn't go dull: half an hour of sleep every day." And I thought that you can't count sleep in pierogi.

---

Next came the flowers. Roxanne from the flower stall, skinny, in fingerless gloves, was trimming rose stems with a pair of pruning shears that squeaked every time she squeezed them.

"Pruners," she said right away, before I'd finished the question. "This is my third pair this year. They rust. From the water. I've got my hands in water all day. You buy a new pair, three months later it's rusty and the spring snaps."

"And if they didn't rust?"

"Then I'd love them," said Roxanne. "Like family."

The fish aisle. Lottie, the fish lady.

"Fish scaler," she said. "This thing here, see? The teeth wear down. A month later it doesn't scale anymore, it just pets the fish. I buy them ten at a time."

The bread stalls. Stefan, the bearded baker.

"Sheet pans," he said. "They warp. From the heat. Six months in they've got a hump like the roof on an old shed, and the bread bakes crooked."

The old newspaper man by the entrance.

"Glasses," he said. "My glasses get scratched. I can't see what I'm selling anymore. New ones cost too much. I'm on a fixed income."

A woman with shopping bags who was walking by and heard what I was asking:

"Pot handles! They're always falling off. And the pot's still good. It kills me to throw out a whole pot over a handle."

A boy of about twelve with a scooter:

"The wheel squeaks."

His mom, next to him:

"The zipper on his coat. Every winter."

I walked, asked questions and wrote things down. In an hour I'd been around the whole market: meat and fish and bread and clothes and the junk stalls at the far end, where people sell old stuff. The notebook ran out halfway through the junk stalls. I started writing on receipts.

---

When I got back to the booth I had forty-three notes. I spread them out on the counter and looked at them for a long time.

And I saw something I hadn't expected.

Almost nobody had told me "it broke." Almost everybody had said "goes dull," "rusts," "wears down," "warps," "gets scratched." In other words, people weren't talking about breakdowns. They were talking about things that go bad slowly, little by little, every day. Things that don't break. They wear out.

When something breaks, people come to me. When something wears out, they don't. Wear isn't an event; it's weather. You live with it. Greg has been sharpening knives for thirty years and doesn't consider that a problem. It's "life." Roxanne is on her third pair of pruners this year and doesn't call that a breakdown. Lottie buys fish scalers ten at a time.

For twelve years I'd waited for people to bring me their broken things. And the biggest thing they needed, they'd never brought me. Because they didn't think it could be fixed. Because it can't be fixed. All you can do is make it so it doesn't wear out.

On the back of a receipt I wrote: "People bring in breakdowns. What they need is for things not to wear out. Not the same thing." I thought about it and underlined "not to wear out."

And then I thought of something else, and I didn't like it.

If I made Greg a knife that never went dull, Greg would never come to me about a knife again. If I made Roxanne a pair of pruners that didn't rust, she'd never buy a fourth pair. Make everything last forever, and I'd have nothing left to fix.

I sat with that thought for a long time. It was like cold soup: you don't want it, but you have to finish your bowl.

And I remembered Professor Stern. The tenth volume. "Or to make a thing that breaks after exactly one year and sell it again and again. A monopoly on matter." That was exactly what he'd been afraid of. He'd been afraid that a man with the thimble would start making things that wore out right on time, so people would keep coming back.

I looked at my forty-three notes and realized I was standing at a fork in the road.

I could make Greg a knife that went dull in a month instead of by lunch. Then he'd come to me once a month, and I'd touch it up for half a bag. Six bags a year. Sixty over ten years. Good, honest, steady income.

Or I could make a knife that hardly ever went dull, charge twelve, and never see Greg again.

The first road paid five times better. I worked that out in a minute.

The second road was the right one. That I knew in a second.

I picked the second one. Not because I'm such a good guy. Because I pictured Greg ten years from now. He comes to me once a month, pays his half a bag and has no idea I could have made him a knife once and for all. And I felt sick. Not for Greg. For myself. For the me of ten years from now, who'd know he was taking money for something he'd ruined himself.

I wrote: "Make things that don't wear out. Even if they don't come back." And underneath, in small letters: "Others will."

That last line I wrote out of stubbornness. I didn't know yet whether it was true.

---

I made Greg's knife that same day.

It was the finest work I'd done so far. Not harder than the valve: finer. The valve had needed patching; the knife had to be remade from the inside. At home I already had a knife made from a saw blade hanging on my magnetic strip, but there the good steel had come to me ready-made. Here I had to make good steel out of bad.

That was where Val came in. Or rather, her iron-carbon diagram, the one she'd walked me through on Thursday "as an advance." Back then I'd understood why one steel snaps and another bends. Now I understood something else: why one steel goes dull and another doesn't.

A knife goes dull because its edge, thin as paper, gets bent over. With every blow against bone, against the board, against gristle, the edge rolls over a tiny bit, like the corner of a page. Greg straightens it with his honing steel and sharpens it on his whetstone, but every day it rolls over again. Because the steel at the edge is soft. Make it hard, and it'll stop rolling. But hard steel is brittle: the edge will chip, like glass.

So the steel had to be hard only at the very edge, in a thin strip. And farther in, in the body of the blade, soft and springy, so it could take a blow. Blacksmiths have been doing this for hundreds of years: they harden the edge and leave the spine soft. But they do it roughly, by eye, by the color the steel turns as it heats.

But I had a diagram. And the thimble.

I laid Greg's knife on the counter, put on the thimble and spent a long time, twenty minutes or so, just feeling it. The steel was simple carbon steel, honest but soft: it was a cheap knife, Greg had bought it at this same market. The carbon sat in the steel few and far between, like raisins in a cheap fruitcake. Under my left hand I had two thermoses (these days I brought three to the market, full of boiling water), and slowly, a sliver at a time, I started gathering carbon toward the edge: from the body of the blade and from the spine, where nobody needed it. That made the spine even softer. All the better: a soft spine takes a blow.

Not all of it, just a little. Exactly as much as it took to make the steel at the very edge, in a strip as thin as a hair, as hard as a file. And then, even more slowly, I reshaped the lattice in that strip: the same rearrangement that's called hardening, which people usually do with fire and water. I did it with my fingers, holding in my head the picture from Val's explanation: needles, thin and tangled together like straw in a haystack. That's what hard steel is. And then, just barely, I let it ease off, let the needles relax a little. Val had said that without that, a hardened edge is glass.

The two thermoses went cold. I ate the three hand pies Mrs. Mazur had sent along with me that morning. And I was still hungry.

When I took the knife back to Greg, at first he didn't get it. He looked at it, felt it, laid it across his palm.

"So you sharpened it," he said. "Thanks."

"No," I said. "Try it."

Greg tried it. Right in front of me he broke down half a hog carcass, and with every cut his face changed. At the end he stopped, held the knife up to the light and looked at the edge. The edge was the same as when he'd started. Not a dent.

"What is this?" Greg asked quietly.

"A knife," I said. "It won't go dull. Well, almost. Once every six months, run it over the honing steel. That's it."

Greg looked at the knife for a long time. Then at me.

"How much?" he asked.

I named my price. Twelve bags. That was what he spent on whetstones, honing steels and new knives in a year. I'd worked it out ahead of time, from what he'd told me. No more and no less.

Greg thought about it.

"I don't have that much on me," he said. "I've got six. And meat. Will you take meat? For the rest?"

I thought about my pierogi. About how pierogi are dough and meat, and how the meat in my pierogi is the most expensive and the most mysterious part. About how I was eating twice as much these days.

"I'll take it," I said.

Greg handed me six bags in cash and a sack with six and a half pounds of pork shoulder in it. To be honest, that was more than six bags' worth. I didn't argue. Greg, I think, knew it too.

I walked back to the booth with a sack of meat in my hand and thought: so there it is. I'd gotten paid in food for a repair. Not by Mrs. Mazur, but by somebody else entirely. And once again I had no way to figure out whether it was fair. What's meat worth in pierogi? What's a knife worth in meat? It turned out that every thing was worth something different depending on what you traded it for.

I wrote on a receipt: "Knife: six bags and meat. What's meat in bags? Don't know. Greg knows. I don't. How do you count it?"

So every thing had its own price, depending on what you traded it for.

---

On Sunday the market got by without me, and I got by without the market. I boiled a piece of Greg's pork shoulder (no pierogi, just meat, like a rich man) and ate it with bread, slowly, sitting at the table instead of standing at the stove. Then I took the pea out of my father's tackle box.

Six grams. On paper, a hundred and eighty bags. In ten days it hadn't bought me a single fried meat pie. It sat there among the bobbers, heavy and useless, like a rich relative who never comes to visit.

And a cheap knife from our own market, which I'd reworked yesterday in an hour, had brought me six bags and six and a half pounds of meat.

I set the pea on the table next to the plate that still had a piece of pork on it, and looked at the two things for a long time. Gold is valuable because there's so little of it. The knife was valuable because Greg needed it. The rare thing, nobody would take off my hands. The needed thing, he'd bought without a second thought.

Lugnut jumped up on the table and looked first at the pea, then at the meat. He thought it over. And reached for the meat.

Even the cat understood.

I moved the plate out of his reach, put the pea back in the little case of hooks and wrote: "Value isn't in the metal. It's in who needs it." Then I ran my column again. A month from now, Bull: twenty-nine, plus interest. Starting the first, rent on the booth up twenty percent, as Mr. Abrams had promised. I already had six bags. If two more people with knives came tomorrow…

I didn't know if they'd come. But just in case, that evening I boiled the kettle, filled all three thermoses and lined them up by the door like soldiers on parade. Next to them I put a lump of rosin. If people came, the soldering iron had to smell right.

By Saturday evening the whole meat aisle had heard about the knife.

By Sunday lunchtime, the whole market.

On Monday morning there was a line at my booth.

It was the first line in twelve years. My booth had never had a line before: two people at most, and one of them was usually Ashot. Now there were eight. Then twelve. Butchers with knives. Roxanne from the flower stall with her pruners and two more pairs from her friends. Lottie with her fish scaler. Stefan the baker with his sheet pans, four of them, stacked up, heavy. The old newspaper man with his glasses. The woman with the pot. The boy with the scooter.

I worked like never before in my life.

I switched on the soldering iron first thing and set a lump of rosin on the tip: for Ashot, for the line, for myself. The booth smelled of pine, like a sauna. I carried each thing to the back shelf, turned my back and counted to a hundred before I handed it over. Somebody asked how I did it. "Cold hardening," I said, and tapped a thermos. "New technology." The man nodded: the thermos on the counter explained everything. People are happy to believe in technology if it's sitting out where they can see it and you pour boiling water into it. Still, turning toward the shelf for the twentieth time, I remembered Bull: "You'll be the payment." And Lenny Kurgan's container out back of this same market. You can see a line from a long way off. A line is an advertisement too, only you never know who's reading it.

Roxanne's pruners: the steel was stainless, but cheap. It had tiny specks of something foreign in it, soft, like crumbs in butter, and the rust started right there, from the inside, like a cavity. Val told me that evening what they're called: sulfides, sulfur; they add it so the steel is easier to cut on a lathe. I squeezed those specks out to the surface as a yellowish film and wiped it off with a rag. Not all of them, as many as I could. It was like pitting cherries. Then I made the spring over again, out of the same steel, but with the right lattice.

The fish scaler: I built the teeth back up with metal from the back of it and hardened them along the edge, like Greg's knife.

The second knife cost me half a thermos: I already knew where the carbon sat in bad steel and what the edge was supposed to be like. Understanding had turned out cheaper than food again. But cheaper doesn't mean free, and there were thirty things.

The sheet pans warped because the metal in them was uneven: thicker in some places, thinner in others, and when they heated up, the thin spots stretched more. I evened out the thickness. That was cheap, nearly free: just moving metal from the thick spots to the thin ones. Like smoothing out a tablecloth. I got through two of the four.

The old man's glasses were glass, and I understood glass worse than metal but better than plastic. The scratches on them were fine ones, and glass, as Professor Arkin had said right after smashing a bottle on the lectern, is a liquid that forgot how to flow. I reminded it a little. On the surface. The scratches closed up. That took almost a whole thermos: glass always remembers grudgingly. The old man put on his glasses, looked at his newspaper and said, "Oh." Then he took the glasses off, wiped them, put them back on and said "Oh" again. He didn't say anything else. He paid the half a bag I charged him and walked away holding the newspaper out in front of him as if he were seeing letters for the first time.

The pot, the scooter, the zipper: small stuff. Five minutes apiece. I fastened the pot handle back on with an iron rivet and left the plastic alone.

By lunchtime on Monday I'd made twenty-two bags. Almost three ordinary days.

And by lunchtime on Monday it dawned on me that I was dying.

---

Not really, of course. But I was so hungry my hands were shaking. The thermoses had run out by ten in the morning. I ran to the meat-pie stand by the gate for boiling water (they filled my thermoses out of pity), and that ran out by eleven. The boiling water was the pie man's, and so was the gas; I promised to take a look at his vent hood. Mrs. Mazur's hand pies had run out by nine. The pork shoulder was sitting at home, raw, forty minutes away by bus.

I ate four fried meat pies. Then two fried meat buns. Then a package of cookies from the kiosk. Then I drank a quart of sweet tea.

The line didn't get any shorter.

People stood there, shifting from foot to foot. Some had waited an hour. Some left and came back. Somebody started arguing about who was ahead of whom. The woman with the pot told the man with the knife he was cutting in line. The man with the knife said he had meat sitting out. Stefan the baker said he still had two sheet pans and could wait, but not till evening, because in the evening he had to bake.

Suddenly something became clear to me, something I hadn't figured into the plan for my new life at all.

I was one man.

One man. Two hands. One thimble. One stomach that holds about four fried meat pies at a time. Eight hours of work a day, if I didn't keel over. And a line that grew faster than I could keep up with.

Before, I'd had a little work and a lot of time. Now I had a lot of work and no time at all. I'd traded one kind of poor for another. I used to be short on money. Now I was short on hands.

I looked at the line, then at my shaking hands, then at the last fried meat pie, half eaten, going cold on the counter.

And for the first time in twelve years I flipped the sign to CLOSED in the middle of a working day.

"I'm sorry," I said to the line. "I can't do any more. Not today. Tomorrow, first thing."

The line grumbled. Not too loudly (people at our market have known me a long time), but it grumbled. Somebody said, "Typical." Somebody said, "I've been standing here an hour." Stefan the baker came up without a word and set his two remaining sheet pans down behind my counter.

"Tomorrow," he said. "First thing. I'm first."

And he left.

The line thought it over and did the same. Five minutes later, behind my counter there were eight knives, three pairs of pruners, two pots and somebody's meat grinder. Each one tagged with a slip of paper and a name. Like a coat check.

I looked at that pile and understood that tomorrow would be the same. And the day after. And that I wasn't going to be able to keep up.

---

That evening Val called. On Thursday, at the door, "Timothy" had somehow quietly turned into "Tim." I couldn't even remember which of us had started it. Over the phone, "Tim" still sounded new, and very nice.

"So," she said. "How's our iron-carbon diagram? Come in handy?"

I told her about Greg's knife. About hardening with my fingers. About the needles, like straw in a haystack. She listened without a word, and then she said:

"That's not magic."

"Then what is it?"

"It's carburizing and hardening. You pulled the carbon to the edge; in a factory that's hours in a furnace. And then you made martensite. No heat and no water. You just went and reshaped the lattice." She paused. "Tim, on Saturday you did by hand what a factory needs a furnace, a quench tank and a three-man shift to do."

"What I know is, I did it in an hour and two thermoses," I said. "And then thirty more things. And now I've got a line I can't keep up with, and I've eaten so many fried meat pies that I think they could sell me back to the pie stand for parts."

Val laughed.

"That's what's called a bottleneck," she said. "You're the bottleneck. The whole system runs up against you. The better you do, the more people come, and the faster you run out."

"So what do I do?"

"I don't know," said Val. "I'm a chemist, not an economist. But in the lab, when one instrument can't keep up, you set up a second one."

"There's no second thimble."

"I don't mean the thimble," said Val. "I mean maybe not everything you do has to be done by you."

"Then who?" I asked. "There's only one thimble."

"I'm a chemist," said Val. "I ask the questions. Answers are your department. You practical types."

I hung up and spent a long time thinking about who, besides me, could do what I do. The answer was nobody. I didn't understand yet that I was asking the wrong question.

---

On Tuesday morning I got to the market at seven, an hour before it opened. So I could get at least something done before the line.

Ashot was already sitting in his booth. He always gets there before anybody else except the sunflower-seed lady. He was sitting on his stool, looking at my booth. At the CLOSED sign behind the glass.

There was nobody in front of his booth.

He didn't say good morning. Neither did I. I raised the shutter (the pigeons took off), and there behind the counter was yesterday's pile of knives and pruners. Ashot looked at it. Then at me working. Without a word. For a long time.

By eight there was a line at my booth again. Stefan with his sheet pans, first, as promised.

There was nobody at Ashot's booth.

Then a woman came up with a boot. I recognized her: she'd brought me an umbrella once. The boot had its heel tip torn off. Usually she took things like that to Ashot. That was his work. His boots, his heel tips, his twenty years.

She walked right past Ashot's booth and got in my line.

"I heard you do heel tips now," she said to me, over the head of a man with a knife. "The kind that don't wear down?"

I'd never done anything of the kind. Not once. I didn't understand rubber: the same spaghetti as plastic. But somebody had evidently decided that if I could make a knife that didn't go dull, I could make a heel tip that didn't wear down. At the market, rumors travel faster than knowledge.

I looked across the aisle at Ashot.

Ashot was sitting on his stool with a hammer in his hand, looking at the woman. Then he slowly turned his eyes to me.

He didn't say a word. But I saw his face. And I understood that yesterday, while I was so happy about my line, I'd been taking the food off his table. Not on purpose. Not with my own hands. But I had.

The woman with the boot was standing in my line.

Ashot set his hammer down on the counter and came out of his booth.

All of a sudden I felt, very sharply, that a conversation was coming that I wasn't ready for.
