# Chapter 17. The Second Bearer

On Saturday I told Val everything.

We were at her place: the first time I'd ever been to her place, a small room on the other side of town. On the windowsill, copper sulfate crystals were growing in jars, blue as the evening sky, and over the bed hung a periodic table with notes on it in red pen. Val sat on the bed with her feet tucked under her. I sat on a stool across from her. Like an interrogation. I'd picked that seat myself.

I told her about the twelve-bag steak. About "thirty times." About "nobody, ever, only you, forever." About the hand on mine, and the snaps. About Cochran, the foundation pit and the corkscrew spoon. About the expert in alloys who'd seen the bracelet. And about Glen: I'd never told her about the line in the notebook that said "Don't search." Now I did. And about the folder on the shelf, next to the business card.

"You didn't say no," said Val.

"I didn't say no."

"Why not?"

"I don't know," I said. "I thought about it all night. I really don't know. Maybe because of the thirty times. Because of the analyzer for you. Windows for Mom. Because I've never once in my life held a piece of paper that said somebody owed me something, instead of me owing them."

Val was quiet for a long time. The crystals on the windowsill caught the light from the streetlight.

"Do you want me to tell you to say no?" she asked at last.

"No."

"Good. Because I won't." She took off her glasses and wiped them on the hem of her sweater. "If I tell you to and you say no, you'll think you said no because of me. And a year from now, when the silicon's getting made at night for next to nothing and we still don't have an analyzer, you'll look at me like this." She showed me how. It was a very good impression. "Decide for yourself."

"What about you?" I asked. "What do you want?"

Val put her glasses back on.

"I want you not to turn into Dr. Andrews," she said. "Thanking everybody who helped, in fine print." She paused. "And I want to know who this expert is."

"I think you know."

"I think you do too," said Val.

We didn't say his name out loud. As if a name could overhear us too.

Then she came over to sit on my stool with me, which meant on my lap, since there was only one stool, and laid her head on my shoulder. The snaps on my shirt didn't stir. I was holding the valve, and she noticed.

"Holding it," she said.

"Holding it."

"Tonight, keep holding it," said Val. "Tonight I want it to be just hands. No science."

It was just hands. I felt better than I had at the Summit, or anywhere else. All day Sunday, though, I thought about the folder.

---

On Monday morning I walked up to my booth and didn't recognize my own bolt.

The bolt on the shutter was mine. I'd put it up myself, eight years or so ago: a steel bar, a hasp and a padlock. The bar was rusty, the hasp was bent. The shutter creaked. Everything just the way it should be.

Now the bar was shining.

It had been polished to a mirror. Smooth, without a single scratch, as if it had just come off the machine. No rust. Not a flake. I looked at it and saw my own face: pale, unshaven, mouth hanging open.

Over the reflection, thin and deep, in even letters that might have been engraved by a laser, someone had written:

"Your work is sloppy. Let's talk. Glen."

Under that, a phone number.

I stood in front of the booth and couldn't move. Not because of the words. Because of how it had been done. The rust hadn't been scrubbed off. It was gone: it had turned back into iron, all of it, atom by atom, and settled into the bar so that not a seam was left. I couldn't do that. I could turn rust into iron, but only "roughly." With pores. With bubbles. This was a mirror.

And then the letters. They hadn't been scratched in. The metal had simply parted and lain down in ridges along the edges, as thin as a hair. As if the bar had decided for itself what would be written on it.

He'd stood right here in the night. At night, when the market's empty and the only light is the streetlight by the meat-pie stand. He'd touched my bolt. A man who doesn't like leaving his house had come in person: he'd needed to touch it.

The heat he'd used belonged to somebody else. There was frost on my shutter and on the back wall of the meat-pie stand, and nowhere else. That's where they keep a pot of broth all night on a low flame. In the morning the broth was cold, and the pie man was cursing loud enough for the whole aisle to hear. Heat always belongs to somebody. This heat had been the meat-pie stand's. Through the bolt, he could have felt the whole shutter, every hinge, every nail and bracket. My booth. From the inside.

Ashot came over, hammer in hand. He looked at the bolt. He looked at me.

"I'm not asking," he said. "I'm reading. What is this?"

"A letter," I said.

"To you?"

"To me."

Ashot read it. His lips moved. He was silent for a very long time. Then he tapped the bar with the butt end of the hammer handle, gently.

"Good steel," he said. "Better than it was." He paused. "But bad handwriting. Angry handwriting. I know people by their heel tips. The way a man writes is the way he walks."

I copied the number into my notebook, and then I smoothed out the letters myself, for half a thermos: the market had no business reading my mail. It didn't come out a mirror. It came out "roughly."

I called at lunchtime.

He picked up right away, on the first ring. As if he'd been sitting there with the phone in his hand, waiting.

"Nagel," said the voice. Not a question. It was a quiet voice, polite, a little tired. The voice of a man who for a long time now has mostly been talking to himself. "Thank you for calling. Tomorrow at seven a car will come for you. Out front of your building. 17 Factory Street, stairwell three. Yes, I know. Don't bring anyone. And please leave your phone at home."

"And if I don't come?"

A pause.

"Then I'll come to you," said Glen. "But I'd rather not. I don't like leaving the house."

He hung up.

---

The car was black, big and quiet. The driver was big and quiet too, in a black jacket, and in forty minutes he didn't say a single word. I sat in the back seat with a paper sack of Mrs. Mazur's hand pies on my knees, thinking that maybe they were taking me somewhere to kill me. Then I figured nobody would take you to get killed in a car like this. They'd mess up the upholstery. Then I figured they probably had another car. For that.

I'd left my phone at home, the way he'd asked. The hand pies I'd brought just in case: the power eats a lot, and I had a feeling this would be a long conversation. All I'd told Mrs. Mazur was that I was going to a meeting. She packed eight and posted in the chat: "For Timothy, for a business meeting. Cabbage. Let them see we're not starving." Old Pete logged me minus eight and opened a new account called "Travel Expenses."

The house stood outside town, in a pine forest, behind a high fence. The gate opened by itself. The yard was lit with lamps. The path from the gate to the door was as dry as in summer. On either side of it, under the lamps, lay snow, smooth as a tablecloth, without a single track. Not a person's, not a dog's, not a bird's. As if nobody had ever crossed that yard at all.

It was a big house. Gray stone, dark glass, a flat roof. Huge windows, floor to ceiling, and not one of them lit, except one on the second floor.

A security guard met me at the door. Also big and quiet. He ran a handheld metal detector over me. It beeped at the thimble.

"Take it off," said the guard.

"No," I said.

The guard looked at me, then at the thimble, then said something into his radio. The radio answered in a quiet, tired voice: "Let him keep it. It's fine."

The guard looked into the sack.

"What's this?"

"Hand pies," I said. "Cabbage."

The guard looked into the sack for a long time. Then he said something into the radio again. The radio was silent for a while. When it answered, for the first time there was something alive in the voice, something almost flustered:

"Let him bring them."

---

Inside it was warm, quiet and empty.

I'd never seen emptiness like that. Not the poor kind. The rich kind. An enormous living room, white walls, a stone floor where footsteps sounded like a gym at night. No rug, no couch, no TV, not a single photograph. Along the walls stood glass display cases, lit from inside, like in a museum.

I went over to the first one.

In the case lay a crystal. Clear, blue, the size of a fist, with faces so even you could use them as mirrors. A sapphire. Without a single bubble. One night in November I'd tried to make a sapphire out of an old grinding wheel from the dumpsters. Three bags I hadn't told even Old Pete about. What came out was a cloudy drop full of bubbles, like a lollipop somebody had dropped in the sand. This was a sapphire.

In the next case, a diamond. The size of a walnut. Clear as water. I knew what it had cost. Graphite to diamond means breaking every bond and making a new one. For a walnut like that, at my rates for welding, a hundred bags, if not two hundred. Val told me later that the diamond itself costs almost nothing: graphite and diamond hold almost exactly the same energy. The expensive part is getting over the hump in between.

After that, metals. An ingot that looked like mercury but was solid. A wire as thin as a hair, coiled into a spiral; the little card read: "Holds 35 lb." A glass sphere with another glass sphere floating inside it, and inside that one, a third. Without a single seam. A thing like that can't be made by hand. Can't be made by a machine. Only by the power. And only by someone who understands glass the way I don't understand anything.

"Do you like them?" asked a voice from above.

He was standing on the stairs. Thin, tall, stooped, in a gray sweater. Gray hair, cut short. A narrow, pale face, as if he hadn't been out in the sun in a long time, which was probably true. Long hands with slender fingers, in white cotton gloves. Like a museum conservator's. Later he explained: so he'd never touch anything without asking. Not even his own skin.

On the middle finger of his right hand was a thimble. The finger of the glove had been cut off underneath it.

Dark. Nearly black, matte, worn smooth like an old coin. Mine was lighter. I recognized it at once, though, the way you recognize a relative at the train station when you've only ever seen him in photographs.

The thimble on my own finger grew warm. I felt it distinctly: the warmth spread from my fingertip into my palm, soft, like the warmth of a mug of tea. As if two instruments separated by twenty years had recognized each other before the people did. Heat always belongs to somebody. Whose this was, I didn't know. A question for Val.

"You feel it too," said Glen. He came down one step. "It's getting warm. So is mine. The professor wrote about it in the ninth volume. Have you read the ninth volume?"

"I've read it."

"Read it, or leafed through it?" asked Glen. "You skipped the formulas. I can tell from the bolt. That's where all the interesting parts are." He came down another step. "Hello, Timothy."

He'd gone from "Nagel" to "Timothy" the moment he saw my thimble.

"Hello, Glen," I said. "Want me to use your last name too?"

"No need," he said. "Nobody here will hear it. Nobody here hears anything at all. Come on up."

---

We sat in a study on the second floor. The one lit room in the house. The desk was a single solid slab of something clear and faintly smoky, a hand's breadth thick. I laid my hand on it and knew what it was. Quartz. Fused quartz: glass made from pure sand. A whole slab, without a single bubble.

"Out of sand," I said.

"Out of sand," Glen agreed. "River sand. You make things out of sand too, I know. Silicon. Solar panels. One panel the size of your hand, half a volt."

I didn't ask how he knew. He knew.

I set the sack of hand pies on the desk. Glen looked at it the way the guard had: for a long time, without understanding.

"They're from Mrs. Mazur," I said. "My neighbor. She bakes. Cabbage."

"I know who Mrs. Mazur is," said Glen. He didn't reach for the sack, though. "You do realize I could check what's inside them?"

"Cabbage."

"That's not what I mean."

So he checked. At first I didn't understand what he was doing. He laid his gloved hand, the one with the thimble, on the sack, closed his eyes and sat like that for maybe half a minute. He was feeling through the hand pies. The dough, the filling, the crumbs. Searching for metal, for wires, for anything foreign. He was checking Mrs. Mazur's cabbage hand pies for bugs.

Then he opened his eyes.

"Cabbage," he said. And for the first time that evening he smiled a little. With one corner of his mouth. "And egg. And a little dill. And she puts sour cream in the dough."

"You figured that out with the thimble?"

"I figured that out by the smell," said Glen. "With the thimble, I figured out there are no bugs."

He took a hand pie. With two fingers, through the glove. He took a bite. Chewed slowly. His face looked strange. Like the face of a man remembering something from very long ago and not quite able to remember all of it.

"I haven't eaten anything made by someone I know in about five years," he said. "My food comes in boxes. From a restaurant. They leave it at the gate. Security brings it in."

There are hands in a restaurant too, I thought. Just without names.

"Why?"

Glen finished the hand pie. He neatly pinched the gloved fingers together and brushed a crumb onto the desk. The crumb landed on the quartz and stayed there, the only thing lying on that desk.

"Because hands are people," he said. "And people are questions."

---

Then he showed me what he could do.

It took a minute. He took a jar of gray powder from a shelf. Corundum, aluminum oxide. He poured a handful into his gloved palm. And simply closed his fingers.

When he opened them, the study had gotten colder. Frost appeared on the inside of the dark window, and somewhere downstairs a boiler clicked on. In his palm lay a lens. Clear, faintly blue, perfectly round, with a surface so smooth I couldn't see it: all I could see was the light it gathered into a point on his white glove. A sapphire lens. From a handful of powder. In ten seconds.

Out of an old grinding wheel I'd made a lollipop dropped in the sand. In half an hour, for three bags.

"How?" I asked. My voice came out hoarse, and I was ashamed of it.

"I don't burn lights in this house," said Glen, following my glance to the window. "I need the house warm for something else. My own substation, my own boiler. I buy heat like everybody else. Just more of it."

"Understanding," said Glen. "You know that. Precision equals understanding. You work by 'roughly.' You don't know how the atoms stand in corundum. You don't know why it wants to become exactly this and nothing else, why a face runs at this angle and not some other. You ask, and it does the best it can. Sloppily. I know. I spent twenty years learning it. Crystallography, thermodynamics, the physics of defects. I know every face by name."

He laid the lens on the desk next to the crumb.

"The professor knew even more," he said. "He knew everything. And he did almost nothing. In the ten years of notebooks I saw, a couple of hundred experiments. A couple of hundred. And every time, he was afraid. Experiment one hundred seven, the eighth volume: frost in the room, and he got scared and didn't touch the object for six months. After that, I'd guess, nothing at all." He looked at me. "What's in the last volumes?"

"This and that," I said. And said nothing more.

"I do a hundred experiments a day," said Glen. "And I'm not afraid."

He was lying. I knew it right away, from the way he said "I'm not afraid." He was afraid. He was so afraid he checked hand pies for bugs. He was just afraid of something else.

---

"Why did you take it?" I asked.

Glen didn't pretend not to understand.

"Because it was sitting there," he said. "Object Two. In the safe, in the lab, in an old cigar box. The professor never touched it. He worked with the first one: a lot at first, and then, in my last years there, once every six months. He let me use the first one. In his presence, on Fridays. The second one just sat there. Ten years he had it. And some time before that, his teacher had it. Just sitting." Glen looked at his thimble. "A thing should work, not sit around. He said that himself. He said it about everything. About microscopes, about furnaces, about students. About everything but this."

I went still. It was Mrs. Stern's line. No: his first. She'd only been repeating him, and I'd been repeating her. My line. The line that had me pinching a crumb off Grandma's silver for a solar panel. Glen said it the same way I did. In the very same words. And for him it meant something else entirely.

"Did you tell him?" I asked.

"No." Glen was quiet for a moment. "I left. I said I was going into business. He knew, of course. He had to have known. He never called. Not once. In twenty years."

"He wrote something," I said.

Glen looked up. Fast. Too fast for a man who doesn't care.

"Wrote what?"

I thought it over. I could have kept quiet. I could have lied. I told the truth, because it wasn't my truth, it was the professor's, and I had no right to change it.

"'Object Two is gone. Glen has left. Don't search,'" I said. "It's in the notebook. One line. On a page by itself."

Glen looked at me for a long time. His face didn't change. Only the fingers of his white glove slowly closed on the edge of the quartz desk. A thin, barely visible crack ran out across the quartz from under them. It went about an inch and stopped. He didn't notice it. I did. The power running on emotion, which in five minutes he'd be calling unprofessional.

"'Don't search,'" he repeated. "Well, sure. Of course."

He unclenched his fingers. Looked at the crack. Ran the finger with the thimble along it, and it was gone. The quartz grew back together, smooth, without a trace. Then, without a word, Glen took a second hand pie.

"He was afraid I'd show up," said Glen. "And I was afraid he would. So that's how we spent twenty years. Being afraid. Split two ways."

"He died in April," I said.

"I know," said Glen. "I was at the funeral. I stood at the back, behind the trees. Mrs. Stern didn't see me." A pause. "How is she?"

"The lamp at her place is on around the clock," I said. "The professor's watch is running. I fixed it."

Glen nodded. Slowly. As if this were very important news. Maybe it was.

---

Then he got down to business. The same way Goldie had: quickly, precisely, forgetting nothing. Except that behind Goldie's words there'd been thirty times and a steak, and behind Glen's there was fear.

"You're glowing," he said. "Do you understand that? You sit in a booth at the Central Market and fix everything in sight in a minute. Knives that don't go dull. Heel tips that don't wear down. The revenue auditor's glasses. You do it in front of people. For hand pies. The whole market knows you've got some 'new technology.' Your whole building knows about the frost on your windows. Your Mr. Palmer already has three theories about you, and all of them are wrong. So far. You're lit up like the lamp on Mrs. Stern's desk. Around the clock."

"So?"

"So sooner or later somebody's going to ask: how?" said Glen. "Not Mr. Palmer. Not Goldie. Somebody real. Somebody who knows what 'how' means. And he'll start searching. And he'll find you. And through you, me."

There it is, I thought. That's what he's afraid of. Not me. The fact that people can see me.

"For twenty years I've lived so that nobody can see me," said Glen. "I have three clients. Construction, aviation, medicine. I make them alloys that nobody else in the world makes. Wire as thin as a hair that holds thirty-five pounds. Steel that doesn't fatigue. Titanium for implants that the body doesn't reject. They don't know how I do it. They think I have some secret technology. And they pay whatever I tell them. Because nobody else can do it."

"Cochran's company," I said.

"One of the three," said Glen. "Goldie sent me her bracelet through my security. She herself never got past the gate; I've never once met her. I touched the spring and understood right away. It wasn't a seam: it was growth. As if the steel remembered being whole. Only the object does that. And then she told me about the spoon." He smiled slightly. "A corkscrew. At the Summit. In front of her father. You were angry."

"I was angry."

"That's unprofessional," said Glen. "The power running on emotion costs three times as much and is ten times as dangerous." He steepled his fingers.

"How do you know all this?" I asked. "About my building. About Mrs. Mazur. About the half a volt."

"Anybody with the link can get into your building's chat," said Glen. "The boy who set it up didn't even put a password on it. And as for the half a volt, your Mrs. Mazur posted that herself: 'Timothy and Valerie made electricity out of sand, half a volt, congratulations.' With three exclamation points." He paused. "Also, one of my people spent three weeks drinking coffee at your meat-pie stand. The coffee there is bad."

He went on.

"Here's what I'm proposing. You give up the market. You give up the Exchange, the hand pies, the old ladies' irons. You take Goldie's contract. I've read it; it's a good one, no traps. You work quietly. In a building, behind a door with a keypad. We split the clients: you take repairs, I take production. We hold the prices together. High prices. There are two of us. Nobody else."

"Professor Stern's teacher said there were more than two," I said.

"Rumors," said Glen. Something crossed his face, though, quick as the shadow of a bird. Nobody can do what we can. Which means we set the price.

"And what do I get in return?"

"I'll teach you," said Glen.

I realized that this was the main thing. Not the money. Not the thirty times. This.

"Everything," said Glen. "Crystallography. Defects. Phase diagrams. Alloys. Polymers: I know you don't understand plastic; your cobbler does the rubber for you. I'll teach you. In a year you'll be making lenses out of a handful of powder. In two, things that aren't in any textbook. You'll be able to understand. Really understand. Like the professor. But actually do things. Like me."

He was looking at me. His face was almost alive. Almost warm. He wanted very badly, I realized, for me to say yes. Not because of the prices. Because for twenty years he'd had nobody to talk to about the ninth volume.

"And nobody can know," said Glen. "Not one word. Not to the lab tech, not to the cobbler, not to the neighbor with the hand pies. Nobody will find out that this is possible. Knowledge is something you have only as long as other people don't. Once everybody has it, it's worthless."

---

I sat there without saying anything. For a long time.

It was hard. Harder than the Summit. Goldie had offered me money, and I couldn't find anything I wanted to buy with it. Glen was offering what I actually wanted. To understand. To stop working sloppy. To know every face by name. To stop eating three bags on a lollipop dropped in the sand.

I thought: he's right, though. If Ashot learns, if Mrs. Mazur learns, if Mikey learns, my booth won't be the only one anymore. The line will melt away. Everybody will have a knife that doesn't go dull. And it'll cost what an ordinary knife costs.

Then I thought: so what?

I thought about Greg, whose knife doesn't go dull. He doesn't care how many knives like that there are in the world. I thought about myself. From Greg I get pork shoulder for soup. From Mrs. Mazur, hand pies. People call me when something breaks: an iron, a frying pan, a pair of glasses. In this house, nothing will ever break. And nobody will ever call.

Then I looked around.

At the cases of sapphires and diamonds, lit from inside. Lit for whom? Who comes here to look at them? At the quartz desk with one single crumb from Mrs. Mazur's hand pie on it. At the white gloves. At the guard outside the door, who isn't allowed to hear anything. At the food in boxes by the gate.

At a man who for twenty years couldn't call his teacher, while his teacher couldn't call him. Both of them afraid. Split two ways.

I thought: this is what "nobody, ever, only you, forever" looks like. This is what it looks like when you're the only one who knows. An empty house with lights inside the display cases. Snow in the yard without a single track.

And I understood that I didn't want it. Because of the snow without a single track.

"No," I said.

Glen wasn't surprised. Not at all. As if he'd known in advance and was just curious how long I'd take to think.

"Why not?" he asked. Politely. With real interest.

"Because you don't have anybody," I said. "In twenty years you haven't had anybody over. I'm the first. And even so, you checked my hand pies for bugs."

Glen was quiet for a moment.

"That's true," he said. "It isn't an argument, but it's true."

"For me it's an argument," I said. "In my building somebody comes to my door every day. With an iron, with a frying pan, with hand pies. I need that more than I need lenses. I only just figured that out. Here, at your place. Thank you."

Glen stood up. He went to the window. Outside was the forest, black, and the snow, white, and the fence.

"You'll change your mind," he said, without turning around. "Not now. Later. When they come for you. They will come, Timothy. Whoever glows always ends up drawing the people who are looking for light. Lenny Kurgan was just the beginning." He paused. "When they come, call me. You have the number. It's on your bolt."

"And if I don't call?"

"Then there'll be nothing I can do to help you," said Glen. "I'm sorry. I mean it."

Strangely enough, I believed him. He really was sorry. Not for me, though. For himself. Sorry that the conversation about the ninth volume was over.

---

They drove me back in the same car. The driver still didn't say anything. Only when we pulled up at my building, as I was getting out, he turned around and spoke quietly, for the first time all evening:

"The hand pies. He's never eaten anything a guest brought before."

"He's never had a guest before," I said.

The driver thought about it.

"That's true," he said, and drove off.

At home, Lugnut was sitting on the shelf, between *Crystal Physics* and the third volume of Professor Stern's notebooks. Next to Goldie's folder. He was looking at me as if he were guarding it.

I took the folder down from the shelf. Opened it. Ten pages of fine print. "The Contractor shall not provide similar services to any third party." "The Contractor shall not disclose the technology." A good contract. No traps. Goldie hadn't lied. The trap wasn't in the contract. The trap was in what I'd seen tonight.

I picked up a pen. On the first page, at the very top, above the word "CONTRACT," I wrote in big letters: "No."

I thought about it. Underneath, in small letters, I added: "Thanks for the steak. I've seen what it looks like. It's not for me."

I put it back on the shelf. I'd take it to her myself. There were still two weeks until New Year's. Or I could have Ashot pass it along. No. Myself. This one I had to do myself.

Then I sat down in the kitchen and opened the ninth volume. The one with "all the interesting parts." This time, formulas and all. I read until two in the morning. About how two objects grow warm near each other. About how Professor Stern had had them together only once, on a table, back in his teacher's day, and got so scared that he locked the second one away in the safe. And never took it out again. Where his teacher had gotten the second one: again, not a word.

On the last page of the ninth volume, in his handwriting, unsteady, it said:

"If there are ever two of them, and they are in different hands, let those hands know each other. Otherwise it is not science. It is war."

I looked at that line for a long time.

Then I wrote in my own notebook: "Glen: has everything, has nobody. Snow without a single track. Me: the other way around."

I thought about it. Added: "But he's right about one thing. I glow. And somebody will come."

Lugnut came down off the shelf, climbed onto my lap and fell asleep. He'd never done that before. I guess he'd decided too that it's cold being alone.
