# Chapter 4. The Gold Question

I woke up at two-thirty in the morning with a thought sitting on my chest, heavier than Lugnut.

Fifty-six bags.

That's how far short I was for Friday. Not for the whole debt, just for the one Friday meeting with Bull. I lay in the dark and honestly tried to get back to sleep, but the thought wouldn't leave. It shifted around, got comfortable, and spoke to me in Professor Arkin's voice: "Nagel, you have in your pocket a tool alchemists dreamed about for two thousand years. And you're fixing zippers."

What the alchemists dreamed about was gold.

I sat up on the couch. Lugnut slid off my feet in disgust and stalked away into the dark, muttering something catlike and insulting.

I should say here that I'd known from the very first day that gold was a bad idea. I'd even written it down in the notebook: "Gold — test it. But carefully. Hard to sell." But it's one thing to know an idea is bad in the daytime, on a full stomach, when Friday is still a long way off. It's something else at two-thirty in the morning, when Friday is one day away and bad ideas start to look like good ones, the way a cat can pass for a dog in the dark.

I told myself: just test it. Don't sell anything. Just find out whether it can be done.

And I went looking for lead.

---

I had lead. My father's tackle box sits in the closet, between the shelter for broken things and the vacuum cleaner I've been promising myself I'd fix for three years now. My father went fishing every summer while he was healthy, and the box was just the way he'd left it: hooks in their little boxes, line on spools, bobbers like fat little toy soldiers, and sinkers. Lots of sinkers. He used to cast them himself, in a spoon, on the gas stove, and my mom would complain that nobody could eat out of that spoon anymore.

I took a handful of sinkers. Heavy, gray, soft: you can scratch lead with a fingernail. The closet floor is bare concrete and ice-cold, and I'd surrendered my slippers to Lugnut back in September, so I'd gone in after the box wearing my father's knee-high rubber fishing boots. I sat down at the table in them just like that. Put the sinkers down, sat down and put on the thimble.

Lead felt lazy. I don't know how else to say it. Steel was strict, like the army; brass was fussy; silver was soft, like butter. But lead was lazy, heavy, sleepy. Its atoms lay in the lattice like retirees on a beach: packed in tight, shoulder to shoulder, without the slightest desire to go anywhere.

I tried making one of the sinkers into a ball. It worked right away, almost without effort. Lead was happy to change shape, as long as nobody bothered it about anything serious.

And then I tried bothering it about something serious.

I need to explain a little here. I'm not a physicist, but I'm not a total idiot either. I remembered from school (and a little from college) that everything is made of atoms, and atoms differ from one another by the number of protons in the nucleus. Lead has eighty-two. Gold has seventy-nine. The difference is three. Just three. It's as if you had a house made of eighty-two bricks and you needed a house made of seventy-nine. Pull out three bricks and you're done. Except there are two kinds of bricks in there, protons and neutrons, and for the gold to hold together instead of falling apart a minute later, you'd have to pull out another eight or so neutrons. That night I didn't think about them.

The trouble is, those bricks are held in the nucleus by a force that made everything I'd done before (bending, welding, stretching) look like a finger flick next to a pile driver. I remembered that too. It was called "binding energy," and it's the reason atomic bombs go off.

I thought about that. And I'll be honest: it scared me.

Then I thought: but I'm not planning to blow anything up. I'm planning to very carefully take three little bricks out of one tiny nucleus. One. Just to see.

I focused on a tiny point inside the lead ball and tried to feel it.

---

At first I didn't feel anything. The lattice, the atoms, the retirees on the beach. I went down deeper and deeper (not with my hands but with my attention, the way you let a bucket down a well), and at some point the feeling changed.

I'd found a nucleus.

It was like running into a wall. No, not a wall: a mountain. Everything above it, up in the lattice, was loose, soft, obedient. But down here was something so dense and so compressed that I felt it not as a thing but as pressure. As if I'd pressed my finger against a spring compressed as far as it would go, and the spring had answered: "Don't even think about it."

I thought about it.

I asked three little bricks to come out.

The first thing I understood was that I didn't have the energy. Not a drop. I felt it right away, the way you can tell a car isn't going to make it up a hill before it's even lost its momentum. Me, all of me (all my food, all my sleep, all my hand pies), was to this nucleus what a match is to an iceberg.

The thimble understood that too. And it started looking for somewhere to get more.

I didn't have time to stop it. I didn't even know you could stop it. It pulled the way it had pulled from the radiator this time last night, only not heat this time, something faster and meaner. On the table stood the kettle, plugged into an extension cord, and the extension cord (the one wrapped in electrical tape, with a bare copper spot I've been promising myself to tape up for three years) was lying under my left palm. Bare spot up. The live wire.

I felt current running through the cord. Not like a shock: like water through a hose, if the hose had suddenly turned into one of your own veins. The current came out of the outlet, out of the wall, through the old fuses in my front hall, out of the panel on the first floor, out of the cable under the street, and all of it came rushing through my left palm, through my chest, through my right arm into the thimble and on into that one tiny point in the lead. The metal ran unbroken all the way to the panel and past it: the copper in the extension cord, then the aluminum in the walls. Like the riser from my faucet, only longer.

The lights flickered.

Then they went out.

Not just mine. The strip of light from the landing vanished from under my front door. On the other side of the wall, the neighbors' fridge went quiet. On the floor below, somebody gasped, a door slammed, then another. But the streetlight in the yard was still on. And the windows in the building across the way were still lit, the few where people weren't asleep yet. It was dark only in our part of the building: the whole of stairwell three, top to bottom, as if somebody had switched the building off along one vertical line.

Then I figured out why. The fuses in my front hall were old, the porcelain kind, and instead of a proper fuse, one of them had a bit of copper wire jammed in it, put there by my landlady's husband back when the fuses blew once. Copper wire doesn't blow. So the next thing in line tripped: the breaker for the whole stairwell, in the panel on the first floor. The protection worked. It just wasn't mine.

I sat at the table in total darkness and silence, quiet like out in the country. My hand was burning. My left palm was burning. There was a smell in my nose, not of something burnt but of something sharp and fresh, like after a thunderstorm. The thimble on my finger was so hot I couldn't take it off: it had stuck.

And on the table in front of me, something was glowing faintly.

Not brightly, just barely, the way the dial of an old watch glows in the dark. I thought the lead ball was glowing. Then I realized it wasn't glowing for my eyes: it was the thimble showing me what eyes can't see. The ball was warm, and inside it, at the very center, was something that hadn't been there before.

I sat and watched the glow slowly fade. About three minutes later it was completely dark.

---

The power came back a little after four. The property management company had gotten their on-call electrician out of bed in the middle of the night. He swore down on the first floor for a long time, flipped the breaker in the panel back on and left without ever figuring out what had tripped it. I silently apologized to him and promised myself I'd never do anything like that again. That was the first promise of the night. I made a few more after it.

When the lights came on, the first thing I did was look at my hand. The thimble had cooled down and come off. Under it, my finger had a red ring around it, the kind a too-tight ring leaves. My left palm was red too, but in one piece. I touched it and decided I'd gotten off easy. A man who grabs a live wire with his bare hand is supposed to end up on the floor. I wasn't on the floor.

I looked down. I had on my father's rubber boots.

And that's when I understood how close it had been. Current always looks for the ground. If I'd been sitting barefoot on a concrete floor, it would have found the shortest way: through my palm, through my heart, through my legs, into the floor. And the thimble wouldn't have had time to do anything. But in rubber boots, the current had no ground. It had nowhere to go but the thimble. The thimble became my ground. It turned the current into something else the moment it came in, right there in my palm, and pulled it through me like water through a pipe, without letting it soak the walls. My father never once in his life went fishing without his boots. So in a way he'd come along with me this time too. That was comforting and terrifying at the same time. The insulation on the extension cord had melted like a candle, and the kettle wouldn't turn on anymore, so I had to take somebody else's out of the shelter and plug it straight into the wall. The copper wire in the front hall had turned into a black blob. In the morning I screwed in real fuses.

Then I looked at the lead ball.

It looked like an ordinary lead ball. I picked it up, put on the thimble (carefully, the way you'd put on a hot frying pan, if you ever had to) and looked inside.

Lead, lead, lead, retirees on a beach. And at the very center, a tiny point, smaller than a poppy seed, that was something else entirely. Not lazy, not sleepy. Warm, even, heavy, and somehow pleased with itself. I didn't know what gold felt like; I'd never touched it with the thimble before. But somehow I knew right away that this was it.

A speck. One speck of gold. I'd asked for a single nucleus. The thimble, as always, had taken extra and made as much as the stairwell's worth of power would buy before the breaker tripped. A pump with no shutoff valve. Price: the lights out in the whole stairwell for an hour and a half, a burned palm, and two years of my life, which I'm pretty sure got scared off me.

And one more thing. Around the speck, the lead was somehow wrong. As if it had been shaken. The atoms stood unevenly, as if they'd been shoved, and here and there in the lattice were tiny gaps that hadn't been there before. Nearly everything I'd taken out, the thimble had stuffed back into the neighboring lead nuclei, and that made them not quite lead anymore. But something had flown out of there while I was pulling out my little bricks. Something I hadn't caught and didn't understand.

I put the ball in an empty coffee can, put the lid on, carried the can out onto the balcony and set it in the farthest corner, behind my old skis. I don't know what I was trying to protect. Myself, probably, from the thought that I didn't know what I'd done.

Then I sat down in the kitchen and wrote:

"Gold from lead: possible. One speck. Price: the whole stairwell without power. Never again."

I thought about it and added, in small letters: "Something flew out. What? Ask somebody who knows. Who?"

It was already the second time in three days I'd written "somebody. Who?" in the notebook. Last time it was "tell," this time "ask." I was starting to think it wasn't a question. It was a diagnosis.

---

By morning I'd had another idea. It was worse than the first one, because it was much more reasonable.

You don't have to make gold. You can find it.

Gold is everywhere. Well, not everywhere, but a lot closer than you'd think. I've been fixing electronics for twelve years, and I know perfectly well that there's gold inside any old device. Not much: a thin layer on the contacts, on the connectors, the chip pins, the edges of the boards that slide into a slot. They put it there because gold doesn't oxidize and the contact doesn't go bad. People who break down old computers for scrap have known this for ages. There are entire forums where they argue about how many grams you can get off a ton of boards.

Not much. About a quarter of a gram per kilogram. But I had the shelter.

And I also had a box of old military-surplus boards. I'd bought it about three years ago at the flea market for half a bag, because I'd meant to strip it for components, and I never did. Back then the army built its instruments as if it planned to keep fighting for another thousand years: thicker connectors, more generous gold plating, capacitors with silver inside. On those same forums, people never called those boards anything but "the golden ones," and I'd always assumed that was an exaggeration.

It wasn't an exaggeration.

I hauled the box into the kitchen, dumped the boards, the connectors and a scatter of little gold-plated pins out onto the table, filled the shelter kettle all the way, plugged it into the wall, switched it on and sat down to work.

It was a completely different kind of work from the night before. I didn't have to break any nuclei. The gold was already gold; all I had to do was gather it together. Separate it from the copper, the nickel, the tin it had been laid onto as a thin film all those years ago. And different metals, as I already knew, don't like to mix. The gold sat on the copper like butter on bread, and when I asked, it came off almost willingly.

I'd take a connector in my right hand, the one with the thimble, keep my left hand on the hot side of the kettle (the shelter's kettle, but my own electricity, my own meter; I'd made a point of checking that in this apartment it was me, not the building, who paid for the power) and gather. The gold flowed off the contacts toward the center of my palm in a thin, almost invisible film, curled up and turned into a small yellow drop.

The kettle went cold. I boiled it again. And again. Over the course of the morning I must have boiled it thirty times. The meter out on the landing, I imagine, was surprised.

By noon there was a small, bright yellow nugget on the table in front of me, the size of a pea.

I put it on the kitchen scale. The scale thought it over and showed: six grams.

Six grams of pure gold.

I knew roughly what gold went for, because I'd once fixed a scale at a jewelry shop and seen the price board on their wall. In pierogi, one gram comes to about thirty bags.

Six grams: a hundred and eighty bags.

Fifty-six bags for Friday, and enough left over for half of the whole debt.

I sat over that pea and, I think, didn't breathe for about ten minutes.

---

I know what you're thinking. I was thinking the same thing. Why not stop right there? Go to the pawn shop, sell it, get the money, pay Bull, breathe out. It's not stolen gold. It's my gold, from my own boards, gathered with my own hands on my own electricity. It doesn't get more honest than that.

I went to the pawn shop.

There's only one pawn shop in our part of town: a small one on the ground floor of a walk-up apartment building, with bars on the windows and a sign that says TOP PRICES PAID FOR GOLD, which has been hanging there as long as I can remember and has never once been true. Behind the glass sat a clerk, a girl with long nails and the face of someone who had seen everything and appraised all of it below market value.

I put the pea on the counter.

She looked at it, then at me, then at it again. She picked it up with tweezers, put a drop of something from a little bottle on it, looked at it through a loupe. Then she set the pea on a special scale and looked at me again. Differently this time.

"What's this?" she asked.

"Gold."

"I can see it's gold. Where's it from?"

"It's mine."

"Is it a piece of jewelry?"

"No. It's bullion. Sort of. This is it."

"Do you have papers for it?"

"What papers?"

The clerk sighed the sigh people sigh over someone who doesn't understand how the world works.

"Hon. Bars are sold with a certificate. With a stamp. With a serial number. And this thing of yours, nobody can even tell what purity it is. The color's like the picture in a textbook; you never see that in jewelry. And it's soft: I ran my nail over it and it left a mark. That means it's purer than anything I've seen in here in five years. Nobody brings in gold this clean. Stuff like this only gets made at a refinery. Or…" She didn't finish.

"Or?" I asked.

"Or someplace else," she said. "Someplace where they make things people don't talk about. I'm not taking it. If you want, you can sell it as scrap, at scrap prices, with ID, and I'm required to report it. If you don't want to, have a nice day."

"Report it to who?"

She looked at me for a very long time. The way you'd look at someone who had just asked why traffic lights have red.

I took back my pea and left.

Scrap at scrap prices would have been fair. But "required to report it" meant the next question would come not from a girl with long nails but from someone with short nails and long questions, and I didn't have an answer for him. "Off some old boards, with a thimble" wasn't going to cut it.

---

Out on the street, I stood for a while getting my bearings, and started thinking, out of habit, in a column.

I had gold. That was a fact. Six grams, a hundred and eighty bags. But what I had in my pocket turned out to look nothing like a hundred and eighty bags. It looked like a very small, very heavy question that everybody I showed it to was going to ask me.

Here's what I figured out. Gold isn't valuable by itself. Its value is that everybody believes: this is gold, this kind, this much, and you can trade it for something else. And for everybody to believe that, somebody has to vouch for it. A refinery, a stamp, a serial number, a certificate, a piece of paper with a seal on it. Without the paper, gold doesn't stop being gold, but it stops being money. It turns into plain yellow metal nobody wants to take, because nobody knows where it came from.

It was like my screwdrivers, only in reverse. My screwdrivers were worth a lot to me and pennies to everybody else, because nobody else needed them. Gold was worth a lot to everybody else, but only gold somebody would vouch for. Nobody would vouch for mine.

On a receipt I wrote: "Value = the thing + trust. Without trust, gold is just yellow." And I underlined "trust."

It was the right discovery. That's where I should have left it.

I didn't leave it there.

---

Lenny Kurgan ran a shipping container out behind the market with a sign on it: BUY & SELL. SCRAP METAL. BATTERIES. Everybody at the market knew Lenny took anything and asked no questions. Everybody at the market also knew Lenny paid a third of the real price, and sometimes didn't pay at all, if you were too weak to argue with him. I sold him copper wire from an old motor once. He weighed it on a scale that had clearly been fixed by me and clearly been ruined by somebody else, and paid me for a pound when there were four and a half.

But a third of a hundred and eighty is sixty bags. And I needed fifty-six.

I told myself: just this once. Hand over the pea, get sixty, give fifty-six to Bull, and the rest goes to pierogi. And never again.

That was probably the fourth "never" in twenty-four hours. I was starting to notice that my "never" worked about like the cheap washer in my faucet: it holds until there's real pressure on it.

Lenny's container smelled of rust, acid and tobacco. Inside it was dark and cramped. On the floor lay old batteries, coils of wire, copper pipe, and in the corner, behind a table, sat Lenny himself: as wide as his container, bald, with a gold tooth he'd supposedly made himself out of rings people had sold him.

"Timmy," Lenny said, unsurprised. "Brought me copper?"

"Not copper."

I put the pea on the table.

Lenny looked at it, and I saw his eyebrow twitch. Just barely. He picked up the pea in his thick fingers, turned it over, then took some kind of gadget with a little screen out of a drawer, held it to the pea and pressed a button. The gadget beeped. Lenny looked at the screen and said nothing for so long that I started to get uncomfortable.

"Where'd you get it?" he asked at last.

"Found it."

"Found it," Lenny repeated. "Timmy. You know what it says here?"

"What?"

"Ninety-nine point nine nine. That's not jewelry. That's bank grade. Nobody in Granton has stuff like this. Not even me."

He set the pea down on the table, covered it with his palm as if it might run away, and looked at me with completely different eyes. Not like he was looking at the guy from the repair booth. Like he was looking at a man who had something Lenny didn't.

"How much more?" he asked.

"None. That's all of it."

"Don't lie to me. Stuff like this doesn't come one at a time. Where's it from?"

"Circuit boards," I said, honestly, because it was the truth. "Old boards. I took the plating off."

Lenny laughed. His laugh was short and joyless, like a cough.

"Circuit boards," he said. "You get it off boards with acid. Comes out dirty. Dark. You're telling me you got four nines with acid in your little booth? Timmy. I'm forty-three years old. I was pulling gold off boards when you were still in school."

He turned toward the door of the container and called, not loudly:

"Sarge."

A man I'd never seen before appeared from around the outside wall. He was younger than Lenny and thinner, but he looked the way people look when they get called quietly and come right away. He stood in the doorway. The container had only one door anybody used.

"So here's how it is," Lenny said kindly. "I'll take the pea off your hands. I'll give you… let's say fifteen bags. Since we're neighbors. And you tell me where it came from. And you bring me more. We'll work together. I'm your market, you're my supplier. Good deal."

"Fifteen?" I said. "That's a twelfth of what it's worth."

"So go to the bank," Lenny said. "Go on. Tell them about your boards."

I looked at Sarge in the doorway. Sarge looked at me. He wasn't doing anything. He was just standing there.

And that's when I understood that I'd made my mistake not today but right at the start. From the moment at two-thirty in the morning when I told myself "just test it." The gold wasn't a solution; it was bait. Only it wasn't Lenny who got hooked. It was me.

"I changed my mind," I said, and reached for the pea.

Lenny didn't move his hand.

"Too late to change your mind, Timmy," he said. "You're already here."

---

I'm not a hero. I want that to be clear. I can't fight, I can't run, and the one time I tried to do more than twenty push-ups, I pulled something in my back and slept on the floor for a week. If Sarge had wanted to stop me, he would have stopped me.

But I had the thimble, and Lenny had a table.

The table was metal: welded together from square tubing, with a sheet-iron top painted green. And Lenny's palm was lying on that top, pressing down on my pea.

I put my hand on the edge of the table. I'd put the thimble on back at the pawn shop (I seemed to have stopped taking it off at all), and I felt the tabletop, thin and ringing, and Lenny's palm on it, heavy and warm. And the pea under the palm, dense and indifferent to whose it was.

I didn't touch Lenny. Even in that moment I remembered: don't touch the living. But the tabletop wasn't alive.

I asked it to sag. Just a little, right under the pea. The steel under Lenny's palm gently gave way, a dimple formed, the pea rolled into it, and the dimple sank further, right through the tabletop, and the pea dropped to the floor at my feet with a ring, like a coin.

Lenny looked at his palm. Under it, in the tabletop, was a hole: neat, round, with smooth edges.

"The hell is that?" Lenny said.

I picked up the pea and ran.

Sarge was standing in the doorway. I didn't try to get around him; there was no getting around him. I ran the other way, to the back wall of the container, where there was a second door I'd noticed earlier: old, rusty, shut with a bolt that, judging by the rust, hadn't been opened since the fall of some empire or other.

The bolt was steel, as thick as my wrist. You probably couldn't have budged it with a crowbar.

I grabbed the bolt with both hands and asked it to go soft.

The bolt went soft. It went as soft as a noodle: a hot, overcooked, long noodle. It sagged in my hands and slid out of its brackets, coiling up as it went. I shoved the door; it screeched and swung open, and I shot out into a narrow passage between the garages.

Behind me Lenny shouted. Not at me: at Sarge. Something like "Get him!" Sarge ran.

I ran between the garages the way I'd never run in my life. The garages behind our market are a whole city, a maze of rusty boxes built over half a century by people who each thought their spot mattered more than anybody else's. The alleys run at odd angles, dead-end without warning, and then suddenly come out where nobody expects. I knew that maze better than Sarge did, because I'd fixed padlocks for half the owners in there.

I turned left, then right, then ducked under somebody's chain strung across the alley. Sarge was coming after me. I could hear his footsteps, heavy and fast. He was younger than me, and he was in sneakers, and I was in winter boots that let in water and are still on my list under a number I don't remember.

Up ahead was a dead end. The garages came together at an angle, and the alley ran straight into the metal wall of garage number forty-seven. I remembered that one: the owner kept a motorcycle in there that he hadn't ridden in twenty years.

I pressed my palms against the wall. Thin sheet iron, corrugated, rusty. I asked it to part.

The wall parted like a curtain. I slipped inside, into darkness that smelled of gasoline and old rubber, and right away asked the iron to close up again.

It closed. Almost without a sound. Just a soft sigh, like the sound a fridge makes when the door swings shut. The iron under my palms instantly went white with frost, and I started shaking so hard my teeth chattered. I'd taken everything there was in me, and a little extra from the wall.

I stood in the dark of somebody else's garage, my back pressed against a motorcycle nobody had ridden in twenty years, and listened to Sarge run past outside. He stopped at the dead end. Stood there. Then he swore (long, and with real surprise) and went back the way he'd come.

I stayed in the garage another twenty minutes or so. In that time I managed to promise myself three times never to get mixed up with gold again, and once to fix the motorcycle for the owner of garage number forty-seven, by way of apology for walking through his wall without asking.

I did fix that motorcycle later, by the way. But that's another story.

---

I got home as it was starting to get dark. The hunger was terrible. I ate two bags of pierogi, then another half, then all the bread in the house, and I still had the shakes until bedtime. While I ate, I thought.

In my pocket was the pea. Six grams. A hundred and eighty bags I couldn't take anywhere.

The pawn shop wouldn't take it without papers. Lenny would take it for fifteen bags, with a side of questions I didn't want to hear. And I wasn't going to a bank, because people at a bank have very good questions and very good memories.

And on top of that, Lenny knew my face now. Lenny knew that Timmy from We Fix Everything had something Lenny didn't. Lenny had seen the hole in his table. Lenny was the kind of man who thinks slowly, but for a very long time.

I wrote down in the notebook everything I'd figured out that day. It didn't come to much:

"Making gold: possible, but the whole stairwell pays. Never.

Gathering gold: possible. Six grams. Honest.

Selling it: impossible. Money isn't metal, it's trust. My gold has no trust.

Lenny knows. That's bad.

The bolt: a noodle. The garage wall: a curtain. Works when I'm scared. Why? And costs three times as much."

That last question nagged at me. The bolt had been as thick as a wrist, and the garage wall was a whole wall. Normally something like that would have taken me a long time, a lot of strength and a lot of pierogi. But in the garages it all happened instantly, almost without thinking. As if fear itself had opened some door inside me, and the power came through it faster.

I didn't know what to do with that. I needed to remember it. I needed to tell somebody. Who?

I looked at the pea, lying on the table and glinting dully in the lamplight. Pretty. Heavy. Useless.

Then I remembered the speck out on the balcony, in the coffee can, behind the skis. And whatever had flown out of the lead while I was making that speck. And "ask somebody who knows."

All my questions over these three days ran into the same wall. I didn't understand what I was doing. I knew how to do it (with my hands, by feel, the way I always had). But I didn't understand it. Which meant that every time I did something new, I risked doing something that couldn't be undone afterward. Breaking Mikey's phone was one thing. But blacking out a whole stairwell? And whatever flew out of the lead?

I needed books. I needed somebody who knew matter better than I did. To be honest, I needed Professor Arkin.

Professor Arkin probably wasn't in the department anymore. But the department was still there.

The Poly.

And only then did it hit me that I'd never made it to the market today. Minus eight bags. Tomorrow was Friday. Bull would get whatever was left of yesterday's eight bags instead of eighty, and an honest face, since that's the only kind I've got. Mrs. Stern would get her lamp and maybe a question I didn't know how to ask yet. And then the Poly.

I wrote: "Tomorrow: Bull. Lamp. Then the Poly. The library. Reference books. Maybe somebody."

And then I hid the pea. Not on the balcony: somewhere nobody would think to look. In my father's tackle box, in the little case of hooks, between the bobbers.

My father would probably have laughed. He always said fishing is when you sit there with your rod knowing nothing's waiting for you at home but a frying pan. And here it was: gold among the bobbers.

In the morning, when I woke up, the case of hooks was open, and the pea wasn't in it.

Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill, chewing a bobber with a look of disgust.

I found the pea an hour later: under the couch, in his stash, next to three buttons, a half-inch nut and a wrapper from a Polar Bear candy. The ring wasn't there. Which meant this wasn't his only stash.

I took the pea. I left the wrapper.

A cat ought to have something of his own.
