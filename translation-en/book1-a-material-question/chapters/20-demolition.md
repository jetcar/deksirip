# Chapter 20. Demolition

The news came on Thursday, January fifteenth, at ten thirty, along with a meat pie.

The pie man brought it over himself, the way he had every Thursday ever since I'd started looking after his vent hood in exchange for boiling water. Only this time he didn't say "Enjoy." He set the meat pie on the counter, wiped his hands on his apron and said:

"That's it, Timothy. It's decided."

"Decided what?"

"The city council. Last night." He pulled a folded newspaper out of his pocket: the local one, the free one they stuff into everybody's mailbox. "Here. Page three."

On page three, under a photo of Cochran holding a shovel beside some other foundation pit, it said: "Central Market to make way for modern shopping and entertainment complex. By decision of the city council, the land lease with the market's operator will not be renewed. Demolition is scheduled to begin in April."

Below that, in small print: "Based on an assessment finding the market's structures to be in unsatisfactory condition." And below that, in very small print: the market would stay open through the end of March.

I read it twice. Then I looked at my booth. At the shutter that creaks on purpose. At the bolt Glen had polished to a mirror and I'd smoothed back down to "roughly." At the counter I'd knocked together myself, years ago, which hadn't budged in twelve years.

Unsatisfactory condition.

"Nobody ever looked at my booth," I said.

"Nobody looked at anybody's," said the pie man. "I've been here sixteen years. Nobody came to see me. With paperwork or without."

He stood there a while longer. Then he said, more quietly:

"Eat it while it's hot. Who knows how many more there'll be."

And he went back to his stand.

The meat pie was hot. I ate it without tasting it. The second time in my life. The first was the day I found the thimble. That time it was from happiness. This time, the opposite.

---

By lunchtime the market was buzzing like a beehive somebody had poked with a stick.

Nobody was working. That is, everybody was standing behind their counters, but nobody was selling. Customers walked up and down the aisles asking whether it was true, and people told them it was, and they left without buying anything, the way people leave a wake.

Greg the butcher stood at his counter holding a knife, the one that doesn't go dull, and didn't cut. Lottie was crying by the fish counter, quietly, wiping her eyes with her sleeve. Roxanne sat on a crate among her carnations, staring at nothing. Stefan the baker went up and down the aisles with the newspaper, showing everybody page three, as if once everybody had seen it, it would stop being true.

Ashot sat in his booth and wasn't tapping.

That was the scariest part. For twelve years I'd heard that tapping: tap, tap-tap, tap. Ashot taps when he works, when he's angry, when he's thinking, when he's haggling. Even when he isn't saying anything, he taps. And now he sat there with the hammer that doesn't break and stared at the sole he was holding in his other hand.

I went over.

"March," said Ashot, without looking up. "Arsen's got three more years. He's in his last year of medical school, then residency. I figured I'd make it. With your heel tips, I'd make it. I even started putting a little aside."

"Ashot…"

"Rent in the new center (I asked, back when the rumors started) is five times as much. Five, Tim. Per square foot. And you pay for the build-out yourself. And nobody's going to let a shoe repair in there anyway. It's going to be coffee shops and phones. A cobbler's not on-brand. That's what they told me at their office. 'Not on-brand.'"

He finally looked at me.

"I've been not on-brand for twenty years. Now it's official."

I couldn't find anything to say. I set half of my second meat pie on his counter. Ashot looked at it. Picked it up. Took a bite. Chewed slowly.

"At least something in this town doesn't change," he said. "Meat pies."

---

That evening everybody gathered at the meat-pie place.

Nobody planned it. Nobody called anybody. It's just that at seven, when the market closes, nobody went home. Everybody drifted over to the meat-pie place, the way people drift into the kitchen when there's trouble in the house. The pie man slid the bolt on the door, set three kettles out on the tables and said, "Tonight it's on the house. Tomorrow we'll see."

There were forty or so of us. Half the market. Greg, Lottie, Roxanne, Stefan, Ashot. The old newspaper man in the glasses I'd fixed for him once. Two women from the produce aisle. A guy who sells batteries and shoelaces, whom everybody calls Double-A, though nobody knows why. And Mr. Abrams.

At first I didn't recognize him. He was standing in the corner by the window without his usual leather folder, in an old coat, and he didn't look like a man who owned five booths. He looked like a man who was having five booths taken away from him.

"Mr. Abrams," I said. "You too?"

"What did you think?" he said. "The land is the city's, the booths are mine. Were. They say I'm owed compensation. For each booth, about what you'd get for firewood. My wife asks me: what now? And I don't know. Thirty years I've been renting out booths. I don't know how to do anything else."

So it turns out landlords stand in line too. Their place in line is just further back.

There was a lot of talking. And shouting. Somebody wanted to write to the district attorney. Somebody wanted to go out to the square with signs. Somebody wanted to go to the newspaper, even though the newspaper was the very one with Cochran and the shovel. Stefan the baker proposed that nobody leave their booths when the machines came, and let them knock everything down with the people inside. Lottie said she wasn't giving up her booth, because her husband had sold fish in it for twenty years, and now she did, and her husband would never have forgiven her.

"The assessment," said the old newspaper man. He said it quietly, but everybody stopped talking. "They write 'unsatisfactory condition.' Who did it? Who signed it? If it isn't true, it can be challenged."

"Challenged," Mr. Abrams repeated bitterly. "Do you know what a lawyer costs, going up against Cochran? Do you know how long it would drag on? A year. Two. And by March we'll already be gone. They tear it down in April."

Nobody answered.

Without thinking, I looked over at the little corner table by the window. The one where one of Glen's people had spent three weeks drinking bad coffee. Now Double-A was sitting there, crying into his tea.

I sat in the corner with my tea and did the math. I always do the math when I'm scared. On a napkin. In bags. What a booth costs. What a lawyer costs. What rent five times higher in the new center costs. What three more years of Arsen's school would cost Ashot.

It came out to a lot. It came out to so much that I ran out of bags before I ran out of questions. And my own line at the bottom: Bull, every Friday. With no booth, out of what?

---

Goldie came to see me on Friday, late in the evening.

I'd already gone to bed. Lugnut was asleep at my feet. The doorbell rang once, short and uncertain. I opened the door.

She was standing on the landing in her navy coat, no hat, snow in her hair. No hat, at four below. So she really had come without thinking. No folder. Not even a purse, just her phone in her hand. Her face looked as if she'd walked all the way across town and argued with somebody the whole way.

"May I?" she said.

"How did you find me?"

"I asked Ashot," said Goldie. "He said, 'If a coat like that is headed to Tim's at night, there's trouble.' And he gave me the address."

I stepped aside to let her in. She came in and stopped in the hallway. She looked over my apartment: the iron chair made out of a bed frame, the shelf, the knives on the strip with magnets out of old speakers, *Crystal Physics* and Professor Stern's volumes. The cat, who had come out of the main room and was looking at her the way he looked at everybody who smelled like strangers.

"Is this the house?" she asked. "The one you saw?"

"No," I said. "That one was empty. This one's mine."

She nodded. She took off her coat without asking where to hang it and hung it on the peg I'd made out of an old bolt. She went into the kitchen and sat down on the iron chair. She put her phone on the table, face down.

"I signed the assessment," she said.

I didn't follow.

"What assessment?"

"On the condition of the market." She was looking at her phone, not at me. "Dad has an inspection firm. Granton Structural Survey. Licensed, with engineers, all by the book. Three years ago he put it in my name. For tax reasons, he said. I'm the director. On paper. In November, right after the first time I came to your booth, he brought me the assessment. The engineer signs, and the director has to approve it. Dad said it was for the insurance company: before construction they always survey the neighboring buildings. Ordinary paperwork. 'Sign it, Goldilocks, you're the director.' I signed it. Without reading it. Because it was Dad."

She was quiet for a moment.

"I'm a good attorney, Timothy. I check every word. I wrote you a contract without a single trap in it. And I was a director on paper. And the assessment they're using to tear down the market, I approved without reading."

I sat down across from her, on the stool.

"You found out yesterday?"

"Today," she said. "Yesterday I read the paper and thought: well, there it is, I knew it would happen someday. This morning I pulled up the city council's decision. The whole thing, with the attachments. One of the attachments is the assessment. With my signature. And it says: 'Limited serviceability; continued operation not economically justified.' Not a single measurement. The engineer who signed it was never at the market: I called him today, and he admitted it himself. Just his signature, my signature and the seal."

She raised her eyes.

"I went to see Dad. I asked him. He said, 'Goldilocks, it's just a formality. The market has to come down anyway. And you did great, you helped.' He said I'd helped him a lot. That without my signature they'd have had to wait for a real inspection, and that's six months. And that another partner of his had asked very nicely for it to go faster. And he also said the contract with you was a good idea. That if the best craftsman at the market went off to work in the building, the rest wouldn't put up a fight. That it was smart of me to think of that too."

Lugnut walked over to her. He sniffed her boots. Then he sat down at her feet and looked up at her. He'd never done that with a stranger.

"I didn't think anything up," said Goldie. "I really believed I was saving you. And he believed I was getting you out of the way. And we both believed it was the same thing."

---

I put the kettle on. Not because I wanted tea. Because I needed to do something with my hands.

While the kettle heated, I thought. I thought the way I'm used to: what follows from this, and what to do about it.

Goldie wasn't lying. I could see that. She hadn't lied at the Summit either, or in the lobby with her "If you ever need a lawyer." She just lived in a house where people didn't tell the whole truth. Like Glen, only the other way around: Glen hid things from everybody, and they hid things from her.

"Why did you come?" I asked.

It came out rude. I hadn't meant it to.

Goldie didn't take offense.

"Because I don't know what to do," she said. "I can go to the district attorney. They'll throw out the assessment and investigate Dad, and me right alongside him. Dad also voted on his own project. That's a conflict of interest, another violation. But that won't save the market. The city isn't obligated to renew the lease, and it runs out in March. The assessment was only needed so the operator couldn't demand a renewal. We'd win a year in court and lose the market. I can go to the newspaper. The newspaper is Dad's. You're the only person I know who doesn't go to court or to the newspaper. You just do things. I came to ask: what can be done?"

She didn't ask, "What can you do?" She asked, "What can be done?" I noticed that.

The kettle clicked off. I poured two mugs. I put one in front of her. She wrapped both hands around it, the way Mrs. Mazur had held her glass, and out of habit I checked whether the water was getting any colder. It wasn't. Ordinary warm hands.

---

I sat there and thought. And for some reason I wasn't thinking about courts, or newspapers, or Cochran. I was thinking about what Ashot had said to me: "I've been not on-brand for twenty years."

And about what Mr. Abrams had said: "I don't know how to do anything else."

And about what Judy had said on the stairs about watchmen.

And all of a sudden I understood something. Something so simple that I was ashamed I hadn't understood it before.

The market isn't the booths.

Booths are boards, sheet metal, nails and corrugated roofing. You can tear a booth down in an hour. You can build one in a day. I knocked my counter together in one evening, twelve years ago. The market is Greg, who sharpened knives for thirty years and now stands there holding a knife and doesn't cut. It's Lottie, whose husband sold fish. It's Ashot, who reads people by their heel tips. It's the line that forms at my booth and then goes on to Greg for meat, to Stefan for bread and to Roxanne for flowers. It's people who know each other. It's hand pies and valves. It's the Exchange, only bigger.

Cochran is tearing down booths. And the market isn't the booths.

Which means the market can be carried somewhere else. The way you carry a fire: not the wood, the flame.

"Goldie," I said. "You're an attorney. Tell me something. Who owns the vacant lot behind the old truck depot? The one past the bypass, where the dump starts."

Goldie gave me the look she'd given me at my booth when I charged her half a bag for the bracelet.

"I don't know," she said. "The city, probably. Why?"

"Could somebody lease it? Cheap. In the name of a co-op. An association. Anything."

"A vacant lot by the dump?" Goldie frowned. "Nobody wants to go out there. There's no road, no power, no water. There's just the old truck depot, and everybody's forgotten about it. Land nobody wants is cheap. If it's even on the books under anybody's name. Timothy. You want to build a new market? At the dump?"

"Not at the dump. Next to it." I took a napkin and started drawing. "Out of the dump."

---

I drew. Goldie watched.

The dump has everything. I'd been out there before, for motors, for glass. The bed frames for my chair I'd found by our own dumpsters, and the dump is the same dumpsters, only for the whole city. That's where I'd been planning to go for scrap for Hank's vise. There's scrap metal out there: old cars, pipe, rebar. You can make a frame out of that. Not booths: a real frame, like a hangar's. Steel I understand. Ashot might learn. Bull's a mechanic; he knows iron better than anybody.

There's plastic out there. Mountains of plastic. Bottles, jerry cans, crates. Plastic I don't understand yet. The latch on Mikey's phone crumbled within an hour, and a roof that crumbles within an hour isn't a roof. And plastics aren't all alike: a bottle is one thing, a jerry can is another, and some kinds give off poison when they burn. Val understands plastic. So first Val teaches me, and then I teach the plastic. Or maybe one of the people who can "hear" will understand it by hand, the way Mrs. Mazur understands dough. Out of plastic: panels. Walls. A roof.

There's glass out there. Broken, cloudy, nobody wants it. Glass is a liquid that forgot how to flow, as Professor Arkin used to say. I know how to remind it. Only you have to remind it with heat, and a lot of it. And in winter, at four below, the heat in the air comes out as frost on a man's mustache, not as glass.

There's sand nearby. A quarry, people say, abandoned about twenty years ago. Out of sand: silicon. Out of silicon: panels. Light. Our own. Nobody's. Not taken from anybody.

"That's insane," said Goldie.

"Yes," I said. "I can't do it alone. And I'm not saying I'll do it alone."

I ran the numbers on the napkin. The frame of a hangar: a thousand joints. A joint isn't a spoon, it's a beam. Call it ten bags a joint. Ten thousand bags. That's a good fifteen years of my dinners. Bending is cheap; welding is expensive. So bend, and slide pipe into pipe, the way I did with the chair. And it's still thousands of bags.

"On my own I'd be building a market like that for ten years," I said, "and I'd eat every bag of pierogi in town."

"And not on your own?"

I looked at the napkin. At the scribbles that looked like a plan. Or like a kid's drawing.

"In December, a neighbor of mine cooled a glass of water by a degree and a half Celsius with her hands," I said. "Without even looking at it. A boy straightened a paper clip. With nothing at all. Five days later he opened a garage. That's bad, but it means he can."

I said it, and only then did I realize who I'd said it to. Cochran's daughter. About the garage. Goldie seemed to realize it too, and acted as if she hadn't heard. At the meeting, Judy the nurse had said that if everybody can do it, there'll be watchmen too. And what I think is: if everybody can do it, there'll be builders too.

Goldie was quiet for a long time.

"I don't understand half of what you're saying," she said at last. "What does 'cooled the water with her hands' mean?"

"It means I need more hands," I said. "And it turns out they've been around the whole time. I wrote that down three weeks ago. I just didn't know what I'd need them for."

Goldie looked at the napkin. Then at me. Then at the napkin again. She was deciding something. I could see her deciding: fast, precisely, sorting through the options. The way she'd checked the contract, word by word.

"The land," she said. "If it's the city's and nobody wants it, I'll find it. In the name of a vendors' association, so it isn't on any one person. That I know how to do. Only they won't lease it without an auction, and an auction takes a month. But nobody's going to come bid on a dump except us. And it can't be right next to the dump itself: there's a buffer zone, a third of a mile, and you can't sell meat inside it. So we put it farther out, by the truck depot. A building permit is harder. Temporary vendor structures are easier. I'll look into what to call it so it's easier." She paused. "Dad will know the same day I file the application."

"Will you help?"

"I signed that assessment without reading it," said Goldie. "The least I can do is read everything else."

---

She left after midnight. I walked her down to her taxi. It was four below outside, and the snow squeaked like packing foam.

At the taxi she turned around.

"Timothy. One more thing. That 'very private' expert. The one who saw my bracelet. I remembered today: Dad talked to him on the phone at the beginning of December, before our dinner. For a long time. And afterward he said to me, 'We need to speed up the market.' At the time I didn't understand what the market had to do with it."

I went cold. Colder than four below could make me.

"You're glowing," Glen had said to me. "You sit in a booth at the Central Market…" And also: "When they come for you." He'd already known they would come.

No booth, no light. What would be left was Cochran's building, a shop behind a door with a keypad, the way Glen had put it, and the clients he would split with me. Or the street. A quiet craftsman with no booth.

It was a guess. I had no way to check it. But it lay down flat and even, like Mrs. Mazur's layers.

For the bolt, Glen had left the house. For the market, he hadn't even bothered. He'd just made a phone call.

"Thank you," I said to Goldie.

"For what?"

"For reading it."

The taxi pulled away. I stood outside the building and watched the snow.

---

On Saturday morning I got to the market and the first thing I did was fix the shutter. The one I'd left unfixed for twelve years, on purpose, for cover. There was nowhere left to hide, so there was no reason to. The shutter stopped creaking, and Ashot looked up in the booth next door, as if somebody had switched off a radio right by his ear.

Mikey showed up at nine, the way he was supposed to on Saturdays, with his order notebook. There weren't any orders that day. He followed me down the aisles and started writing down people instead of orders.

I went down the aisles.

I didn't know how to say it. I'm no good at talking in front of people. I'm good at fixing things. So I did it the way I do when I'm fixing: one at a time. Each one separately.

Ashot. I told him about the vacant lot, about the dump, about the frame out of scrap metal. He was quiet for a long time. Then he asked:

"I'll have a booth out there?"

"You will," I said. "Whatever kind you want. Out of shoe soles, if you like."

"Not out of soles," said Ashot. "I've been breathing glue for twenty years. Make it out of something that doesn't smell like glue. How you'll do it, I'm not asking. I'll ask where my booth is. And I'll be the first one tapping away out there."

Greg the butcher. He listened, wiping his knife.

"At the dump," he said. "Meat. At the dump."

"Not at the dump. Next to it. It'll be clean there. We'll make it clean."

"We," Greg repeated. "Who's 'we'?"

I thought about it.

"Whoever wants in," I said. "We teach in the open. Whoever wants to learn, we teach. Those aren't my words. It's a rule. It's written down in our building."

Greg looked at his knife.

"You made me this knife in half a day," he said. "I sharpened knives for thirty years, and you did it in half a day. That wasn't just hardening, was it? I'll be honest, it stung a little, back then."

"Hardening too," I said. "But not only."

"So tell me: could I do that?"

"I don't know," I said. "Let's find out. A glass of water, three minutes. Val will run the test."

Greg laughed. The first time in three days, Lottie told me later. Mikey wrote down: "Greg: glass of water."

Lottie. She listened and cried. Then she asked whether she could take her husband's sign to the new place. The old wooden one: FISH. FRESH. ALWAYS. I said she could. That I'd even touch it up for her. She said, "Don't. Leave it the way it is." Mikey wrote down: "Lottie: sign. Don't touch."

Roxanne asked whether it would be warm enough out there for flowers. I said it would: first a stove, then panels. She asked what panels. I said: out of sand. She looked at me as if I were out of my mind and said, "Fine. If the carnations make it, I'm in."

Stefan the baker asked whether there'd be an oven out there. I said there would, if he showed me where to put it. He said an oven had to be set so it would draw. I said we'd make it draw. He said, "You ever built an oven?" I said no. He said, "Then you'll learn. I'll show you." Mikey wrote down: "Stefan: oven. Teaching Tim."

And I understood that this was the main thing. I don't know how to build an oven. Stefan does. I don't know how to keep flowers from freezing, or from wilting in the heat. Roxanne does. I don't know what "not on-brand" means. Ashot does. Every one of them knows something I don't. And every pair of hands knows something of its own.

---

The last one I went to see was Mr. Abrams.

He was standing by his five booths, at the first one, where his sign RENT DUE ON THE FIRST used to hang. Somebody had already taken the sign down.

"Mr. Abrams," I said. "You've spent your whole life renting out booths. You know what a square foot costs, what electricity costs, what the fire inspection costs, who pays when and who doesn't pay at all. You know everybody at this market better than they know themselves."

"I do," he said. "So?"

"The new place is going to need somebody who knows that," I said. "Somebody to do the math. Somebody to decide whose booth goes where. So people don't come to blows over it. You know how to do that. You said you don't know how to do anything else. But this you do know how to do. Better than anybody."

Mr. Abrams looked at me for a very long time. Then he said:

"Back in October I put your rent up twenty percent."

"I remember," I said.

"And you're offering me a job."

"I'm offering you a place," I said. "There'll be a lot of work. Not much money, probably. For now."

He was quiet. Then he took out of his coat that same leather folder: it turned out he'd had it with him all along, just under his coat. He opened it. He took out a clean sheet of paper. He held it out to me.

"Write," he said. "How many booths. What sizes. Who goes where. I'll run the numbers. I'll run the numbers on this, too."

---

That evening we sat at Mrs. Mazur's. Me, Val, Mrs. Mazur, Mikey, Old Pete. Goldie called and said that according to the county parcel map the vacant lot by the truck depot belonged to the city, unassigned reserve land, that it could be leased to an association, that on Monday she'd check it properly, and that she'd already started. Old Pete entered it in the ledger as "Asset. Land. In reserve."

"And the demolition, that's the property management company," he said, and underlined it. "They do it on purpose. So we'll all move."

"Forty people building," said Mrs. Mazur, looking out the window. "Think of all the baking."

Val looked at me over her glasses.

"She was at your place last night?" she asked quietly.

"Till after midnight. Tea. Snaps intact."

"I didn't ask about the snaps."

"You did," I said. And she smiled a little.

Val sat next to me, looking at my napkin from the meat-pie place, which I'd brought to show everybody.

"Tim," she said quietly. "Do you understand what this means? Not one thimble. Not just you. It means we need more thimbles. Even weak ones. Even student thimbles. Otherwise Greg will be cooling water, and you'll be bending the frame by yourself."

I looked at her.

"Professor Stern spent his whole life studying how it works," said Val. "Thirty-one notebooks. It's all in there. Or almost all. We've never once tried to understand how it's put together. Only to use it."

"You want to copy it."

"I want to understand it," said Val. "And if we understand it, maybe we'll copy it."

I looked at my thimble. Warm, heavy. Paler than Glen's. Thirty years with Professor Stern. Who knows how long with his teacher. With me since October.

The lease ran through the end of March. Demolition in April. Until the end of March: two and a half months.

I picked up the napkin and wrote at the bottom, in my crooked handwriting:

"The market isn't booths. The market is people. Booths can be built."

And below that:

"Hands: plenty. Thimbles: one."

Lugnut, whom Mrs. Mazur had let into the kitchen in honor of "a historic evening," jumped up on the table, looked at the napkin and sat down right on top of it. As if he were signing it.

Old Pete wrote: "Minutes certified by the cat."
