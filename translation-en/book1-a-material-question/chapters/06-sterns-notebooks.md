# Chapter 6. Stern's Notebooks

On Saturday morning I decided to give the thimble back.

I decided at six-thirty, when I woke up and saw the four library books on the stool next to the couch. The decision came on its own, the way you realize you're running a fever: nothing hurts yet, but you already know.

The logic went like this. The thimble wasn't mine. Nobody had given it to me. Somebody had brought me a lamp, and inside the lamp was something that belonged to someone else. Mrs. Stern had said "take it apart very carefully" and "a thing should work, not sit around," but that could mean anything. It could mean she'd wanted me to find it. Or it could mean she'd just been talking about the lamp, and I, like anybody with a miracle in his pocket, was hearing hints in every sentence.

If a thing belongs to someone else, you give it back. That was the first rule my father drilled into me, when I was about six and brought home a scooter I'd found outside our building. We spent two hours walking around the block ringing doorbells until we found the owner. The owner turned out to be an obnoxious kid named Ronnie, who didn't even say thank you. My father said thank-yous had nothing to do with it.

I put on my jacket, put the thimble in my pocket and went to the market. The market first. Saturday's the best day for business, and I was sixty-seven bags short for Friday. A miracle could be returned in the evening just as well.

I knew how that sounded. I laughed at myself too.

---

I worked at the market until two, and I worked carefully. Only what I understood. Only metal. Only with the soldering iron going, smelling of rosin. Ashot kept glancing over but didn't say anything. Apparently yesterday's show with Mrs. Stern had led him to some conclusions of his own, and he was still thinking them over.

By two I had eight bags. Fifty-nine to go before Friday.

"Ashot," I said, pulling down the shutter, "do you know where Mrs. Stern lives?"

Ashot looked at me over the shoe he was holding.

"What do you need that for?"

"I just do. About the lamp."

"You gave her the lamp back yesterday."

"I forgot to tell her something."

Ashot thought about it. The shoe in his hand turned slowly, like a planet.

"The Professors' Building," he said at last. "On Garden Street. Yellow, with columns. Third entrance, second floor. I've been putting new heels on her shoes for twenty years. Every time she brings me shoes older than I am and says, 'Same as last time.'" He paused. "Tim. Did you take something from her?"

I froze with the shutter in my hands.

"What makes you think that?"

"You've got the face my son had when he was eight and ate his grandmother's birthday cake. The whole thing. Before the party."

"I didn't take anything," I said. "Somebody gave it to me."

Ashot nodded, as if that explained everything. Maybe it did.

---

The Professors' Building on Garden Street had been built before the war, for people who were considered important back then. Yellow stucco, white columns by the entrances, tall windows, plaster moldings under the eaves: peeling, but still proud. In buildings like that the staircases are wide, the doors are tall, and the neighbors know each other by their coughs.

Mrs. Stern opened the door right away, as if she'd been standing behind it.

"Timothy," she said, not at all surprised. "Come in. Keep your shoes on; I haven't tidied up."

Her apartment was tidy the way a place gets tidy only when nobody has touched anything in it for a long time. A long hallway with bookshelves up to the ceiling. The smell: paper, old wood, valerian drops, and something else, sharp and metallic, like my booth after soldering. A kitchen with an enormous china cabinet. And at the end of the hallway, a half-open door to a study, with yellow light falling out of it.

The lamp was in there, on the desk. Lit.

"I don't turn it off," said Mrs. Stern, noticing where I was looking. "It's silly, I know. My electric bill looks like a small factory's. But it makes me feel better."

We sat in the kitchen. She poured tea into thin glasses in metal holders and put a little dish of jam on the table: cherry, dark, almost black. And a plate of cookies. And another plate, of sandwiches. And, after thinking it over, a third, with cold chicken.

"Are you always this hungry?" she asked.

"The last four days," I said honestly.

"Yes," she said. "Ephraim too. The first few years. Then he learned."

I set my glass down on the table. Carefully, so it wouldn't spill.

Then I took the thimble out of my pocket and put it down next to the jam.

It lay on the flowered oilcloth, dark and shimmering, looking like something that had no business lying on flowered oilcloth. Mrs. Stern looked at it for a long time, calmly. She didn't reach for it.

"I came to give it back," I said. "It's yours."

"No," she said.

"It was in your husband's lamp."

"It was," she agreed. "And now you have it. It isn't mine, Timothy. And it wasn't his either, if you think about it. He used to say so himself. 'I'm not the owner, Bella. I'm the keeper. And being a keeper isn't a profession, it's an illness.'"

She picked up the thimble. At last. She turned it over in her fingers, which were thin and dry, with big knuckles. She put it on her middle finger, the way I had that first day. She sat. She waited.

Nothing happened. I could see that in her face. She looked at the table, at her glass, at the jam, and nothing in her face changed.

"You see?" she said. "I've tried. Many times. It doesn't talk to me. Ephraim said I don't have it in me. That it's like an ear for music: some people have it and some don't. I don't. I've lived thirty years next to this thing, and for me it's just a thimble. It isn't even any good for sewing; it's too heavy."

She took the thimble off and put it back down in front of me. She pushed it with one finger. Half an inch. Toward me.

"Why me?" I asked.

---

Mrs. Stern stirred her tea. The spoon rang against the glass, thin and clear.

"I've been going to that market for twenty years," she said. "Ephraim never did the shopping; he believed food appeared in the refrigerator on its own, like dust. I've been walking past your booth, Timothy, ever since you turned up at that market. I've watched you work. I watched you fix that awful flowered umbrella for a woman who had no money, and took a hand pie for it. I watched you spend three hours on a watch that was worth less than the repair, because the old man said it was his wife's. I watched you give a boy his money back for his phone."

"That was Wednesday," I said.

"I was at the market on Wednesday," said Mrs. Stern. "I'm at the market every day. I have nowhere else to go."

She was quiet for a moment.

"Before he died, Ephraim told me one thing. He said: 'Give it to someone who fixes things. Not to someone who sells them. Someone who sells will hide it. Someone who fixes will use it.'" She looked at me. "He thought I'd give it to someone from the department. Some young physicists. But young physicists, Timothy, will write a grant proposal first thing. Then a patent. And then they'll hide it. I spent thirty years watching how it's done. I chose you."

"What if I hide it?"

"You didn't hide it," she said. "You came to give it back. That's your answer."

---

I don't know how long we sat like that. The tea went cold. I ate all the chicken, all the sandwiches and half the cookies, and Mrs. Stern watched me with the kind of pleasure she must have taken in watching her husband eat.

Then she stood up and said:

"Come along."

We went into the study. The lamp was burning on the desk with its warm, honest light. The walls were lined with bookshelves, like the hallway, and by the window stood a heavy iron cabinet: a safe, an old one, with a wheel for a handle, like in bank movies.

"He welded it himself," said Mrs. Stern. "In the seventies, back when that was still necessary. I never did find out where he got the steel."

She turned the wheel and dialed the combination without hiding it from me (all I noticed was that the last number was an eight), and opened the door. Inside, on the shelves, were notebooks. Thick bound notebooks with oilcloth covers, gray, green, brown. Lots of them. I counted more than thirty.

"One a year," said Mrs. Stern. "Sometimes two. He wrote every evening. Even when he wasn't doing anything. Especially when he wasn't doing anything."

She took out the first one, gray and worn, with faded writing on the cover: "Object One. Volume One."

"Object One?" I said.

Mrs. Stern looked at me. Something flickered across her face, quick as the shadow of a bird.

"There was an Object Two," she said. "It disappeared twenty years ago. Along with Ephraim's graduate student. Glen Rusk. A very gifted boy. Ephraim loved him like a son. And then Glen left the department; he said he was going into business, that science doesn't put food on the table. And Object Two left with him. Ephraim didn't tell anyone. Not the police, not the department. All he said to me was, 'Then that's how it had to be.' And he hid Object One in the lamp he'd built years before. And from then on he kept it there, on his desk, within reach, and he was afraid."

"Afraid of what?"

"Of everything," Mrs. Stern said simply. "That someone would find out. That Glen would come back. That he himself would do something that couldn't be undone. He was a very smart man, Timothy. And very smart people always see ten steps ahead, and at every one of those ten steps they're frightened."

She held out the notebook to me.

"Take it. Take all of them. I don't need them. I've read them, and I don't understand a thing in them, except that he was unhappy. You need them more."

---

Before I left, one more thing happened. A small thing. But I still think about it.

On the desk in the study, next to the lamp, lay a wristwatch. A man's watch, old, with a worn leather strap. The hands had stopped at twenty to four.

"That's his," said Mrs. Stern, noticing where I was looking. "It stopped that day. In April. I didn't wind it. Somehow I just… didn't."

I picked up the watch. Mechanical, simple, good: the kind they made to be repaired for a whole lifetime. I held it to my ear. Silence.

"May I?" I asked.

Mrs. Stern was quiet for a long time. Then she nodded.

I sat down at the desk, under the lamp. I opened the case back with my thumbnail; on a watch like that it's just a snap-on back. Inside was the movement (brass, steel, tiny ruby jewels), and I saw what the trouble was right away. The balance staff, the hair-thin axle the little timekeeping wheel swings on, was bent. Just slightly. Maybe the professor had knocked his hand against the desk. Or dropped the watch. Or maybe the staff had simply gotten tired after forty years of service.

Normally you replace a staff like that. You have to order one, hunt for it, find one that fits. That takes weeks.

I put on the thimble.

The steel of the staff was as thin as a hair. On Wednesday at the market I'd already mended one like it, in half a minute and by guesswork. This time I didn't want guesswork. I touched the thimble to the balance bridge, and the lattice of the staff answered me through the brass. The grain ran lengthwise, like fibers: work hardening, I knew the word now. At the bend, the fibers were crumpled, like a bundle of straw squeezed in a fist. I asked them to straighten out. Slowly. Very slowly. One at a time.

It took a minute, twice as long as on Wednesday. But there was almost no hunger, as if I'd eaten one more of Mrs. Stern's sandwiches. Slow turned out to be cheaper. Mrs. Stern stood next to me, not breathing.

I nudged the balance wheel with the tip of a screwdriver.

The watch started.

Tick-tock. Tick-tock. Very quietly. Like the heart of a small bird.

I set the hands to the right time, wound it and handed the watch to Mrs. Stern. She took it in both hands, held it to her ear and closed her eyes. And stood that way. For a long time.

Then she opened her eyes and said, in a perfectly dry voice:

"Ephraim would have said you did it wrong. That you shouldn't have straightened the staff, you should have annealed it and hardened it all over again. He would have given you a forty-minute lecture."

"I'd have listened," I said.

"I know," said Mrs. Stern. "That's why I chose you."

---

I carried the notebooks home in two shopping bags that Mrs. Stern had given me. The bags were plaid, made of that plasticky woven stuff, the kind people used to haul goods to the market in when I was a kid. On the bus people kept glancing at me sideways: thirty-odd notebooks weigh a fair amount, and apparently I looked like a man carrying something very valuable. I was.

At home I laid them out on the floor by year. Lugnut came over to look, sniffed the first notebook, sneezed and left. Dust. Thirty years of dust.

And I started to read.

---

Professor Stern's handwriting was small, sharp and neat: the handwriting of a man who'd spent his whole life writing formulas on a blackboard and had gotten used to every squiggle meaning something. In the first volume there were a lot of exclamation points. After that there were fewer and fewer. By the tenth volume they were gone entirely.

He used initials for everyone, as if he were afraid of names: two letters for his teacher, and later two for Glen. Where I can, I'll just use the names.

The first entry went like this:

"Received the object from my teacher. He says he doesn't know where it came from. He got it from his own teacher. Where that one got it, unknown. The chain goes back into nothing. Put it on. Felt the copper in the wiring. Hand shaking. Writing this with a shaking hand. This is impossible. This is real."

I read that entry three times. "This is impossible. This is real." Thirty years ago a man who knew everything there was to know about matter had put on this thimble and felt exactly what I'd felt. And written it down. And his hands had shaken exactly like mine.

After that came the experiments. Neat, numbered, dated. "Experiment fourteen. Copper wire, half a millimeter. Bent ninety degrees. Time: four seconds. Subjective cost: low. Mass before and after: no change." "Experiment fifty-two. Welded two steel samples together. Cost: considerable. Severe hunger. Ate Bella's lunch and my own." I laughed out loud. We ate the same way, him and me.

He'd discovered everything I'd discovered. Only thirty years earlier, and with numbers. You can't create mass. Bending is cheap; tearing is expensive. Precision comes from understanding. You can take heat from your surroundings. He'd even worked out something like a formula: the energy the object pulls doesn't depend on what you want to do, but on how clearly you understand exactly what it is you want. The muddier the understanding, the more it pulls. As if the thimble made up in force for whatever was missing in knowledge.

A pump with no shutoff valve. That's what I'd called it. Professor Stern called it "the greed of the instrument." And in the eighth volume he wrote:

"The valve is attention. If you hold a precise picture in your mind, the object takes exactly as much as it needs. If the picture is blurred, it takes as much as it's given. Checked three times. Experiment one hundred seven: chilled the room until there was frost, because I was thinking not about the lattice but about Bella, who'd promised to be home at seven and wasn't."

I looked at that line for a long time. He'd chilled a room until there was frost because he was thinking about his wife. And I'd frozen the whole riser because I was thinking about the razor-spoon: what it would be like, not how it was put together inside. For him, Bella had blurred the picture; for me, it was not knowing. But neither of us had shut the valve. It was like finding out that you and a great-grandfather you'd never met have the same birthmark.

---

And then, around the tenth volume, the notebooks started to change.

There were fewer entries. The experiments got rarer and smaller. The professor had almost stopped trying anything new. He wrote down observations, arguments, doubts. Long paragraphs without a single number. I read them and felt a chill go down my spine.

"What happens if this falls into the hands of someone who wants power? He'll make weapons. Not necessarily a bomb. It would be enough to make a steel nobody else can make and sell it to the army. Or to make a thing that breaks after exactly one year and sell it again and again. A monopoly on matter. Worse than any monopoly on oil."

"What happens if I publish? Will others turn up who can do it? My teacher said rumor has it there are more than two objects. Two, both in my hands: for now that's a balance. Ten would be a market. A thousand would be something I can't imagine."

Where he'd gotten the second one, I never did find in the notebooks. As if one day the word "second" simply turned up on a page.

"Glen is talent without fear. He does the things I don't dare do, and does them easily, cheerfully, fast. He's twenty-five. I'm sixty. I look at him and don't know what I feel: pride or horror. Talent without fear is worse than fear without talent."

And in the same volume, two pages later, a short, dry entry:

"Object Two is gone. Glen has left. Don't search."

After that there was nothing else in that volume. Just a date above the line, and five blank pages.

---

I read until three in the morning. I boiled pierogi twice and both times ate them without tasting anything. Lugnut left and came back. Sometime around two-thirty I came across an entry in the very last volume, the thirty-first, and I read it out loud, I don't know why. Probably because I wanted somebody to hear it. Even if it was only the cat.

"For thirty years I have held in my hands the instrument humanity has dreamed of ever since it learned to hold anything in its hands. And I did nothing with it. I understood the rules. I understood the price. I understood the danger. There was one thing I didn't understand: what any of it is for, if you don't use it. Precision equals understanding. I understood a great deal, and I dared nothing.

"Bella, if you are reading this, give it to someone who fixes things. Let him dare."

Lugnut was watching me from the windowsill. I put the notebook down on my knees and looked at my hands.

They were ordinary hands. Calluses on the fingers from screwdrivers, a burn from the soldering iron, a scratch on a knuckle from the shutter. The hands of a repairman from a booth at the market, who owed almost sixty bags by Friday and had no idea where to get them.

I wanted terribly to be somebody else. Somebody who'd know what to do with thirty-one notebooks of fear and one thimble.

But there wasn't anybody else. There was me.

---

On Sunday at eleven my mom called.

She's called on Sundays at eleven ever since she moved in with Aunt Wanda. You could set your watch by her. Even Professor Stern's watch, which was running again now.

"Tim, sweetheart," said Mom. "Are you eating soup?"

"I am," I said.

"Pierogi in the water they were boiled in are not soup."

I almost dropped the phone. For ten years she hadn't known, and now she did.

"Mom, how did you…"

"Wanda," said Mom. "She read online that all single men between thirty and forty eat pierogi and call it soup. Is that true?"

"It's a question of terminology," I said.

Mom sighed. The sigh only moms can do. It had everything in it: "I knew it," and "just like your father," and "what am I going to do with you."

"You sound different," she said suddenly.

"Different how?"

"I don't know. Like you've found something."

I looked at the notebooks laid out on the floor. At the thimble, lying on notebook number one. At the cat, asleep on notebook number seventeen, as if that one were his.

"I have," I said.

"A girl?" Mom perked up.

I thought about gray eyes and a pencil in someone's hair. Then about Wednesday, six p.m., the inorganic chemistry lab. Then about how Professor Stern, in thirty years, had never shown anybody anything except his wife and Glen, and look how that turned out, and I, in less than a week, had already shown it to Mikey, to Val, to Mrs. Stern and maybe halfway to Ashot.

"Work," I said. "I think I've found work."

"But you already work."

"No, Mom. Before, I fixed things. And now…" I looked for the word. "Now I think I have to learn."

Mom was quiet. Then she said something I didn't expect from her at all:

"Your father always said you shouldn't have dropped out of college. That you'd have made a good scientist. Only you needed to keep your hands busy."

"He said that?"

"He did. Not to you. To me."

I sat on the floor among the notebooks and didn't know what to say.

"Eat some soup," said Mom. "Real soup. With beets."

And she hung up.

---

I sat there a little longer. Then I took my own notebook, the graph-paper one with "Trials" written on the first page, and on a clean spread I wrote in big letters:

"Professor Stern was afraid and did nothing. Glen wasn't afraid and took it. I'm in the middle. So: be afraid, and do it anyway. But do it where people can see."

I thought about it and underlined "where people can see." It was a new thought, and I didn't know yet what it meant. But it seemed important. Professor Stern hid it and was afraid for thirty years. Glen hid it too, and is probably afraid as well, just in a different way. Maybe if you don't hide it, being afraid won't be so scary?

Then in the corner of the page I wrote: fifty-nine bags by Friday. Thirty-one notebooks of knowledge were worth, if you thought about it, a lot more than all the pierogi in Granton. But you couldn't hand a single one of them to Bull.

At the bottom I wrote, in small letters: "Knowledge: yes. Money: no. How do I get one to help the other?"

And I went to make soup. Real soup, with beets. I didn't have any beets, but I had a can of tomato paste, an onion and a bag of pierogi.

It was a philosophically defensible borscht.
