# Chapter 23. Glen's Move

The auction was on Friday, March sixth, at eleven in the morning, in a small room on the second floor of the city council building that smelled of floor wax and other people's hopes.

I didn't go. Goldie said I shouldn't.

"You'll stand there with a look on your face that'll double the price," she said on the phone. "Mr. Abrams and I will go. He's chairman of the association. He knows how to look at officials as if they owed him rent."

Goldie had registered the New Market association back in January, a temporary one, with three members. On Monday, at a meeting at the meat-pie place, it was made permanent, and Mr. Abrams was elected chairman. Forty-two people raised their hands. Double-A raised both, because he wasn't sure how many you were supposed to. The whole market chipped in for the auction deposit, half a bag per booth, and Mr. Abrams personally counted every half.

From somewhere he'd produced his leather folder again. He looked as if he'd never lost it.

At eleven I was standing in my booth, fixing Roxanne's pruners, which didn't need fixing. My hands just had to be doing something.

At eleven forty, Goldie called.

"It's ours," she said. "The lot by the truck depot. For five years. There were no other bids. The auction was declared void, and the lease goes to the sole bidder at the starting price."

I sat down. Right on a crate. Across the aisle, Ashot stopped tapping.

"None at all?" I asked.

"None at all. Who's going to bid on a dump?" Goldie paused. "Dad found out about the application the same evening I filed it, back in January. He called me then and said, 'Goldilocks, let them play at the dump. I don't mind.' And he laughed."

"Is that good?"

"It means he doesn't think we're dangerous. That's always good. For now."

I told Ashot. Ashot told Greg. Greg told Lottie. Ten minutes later the whole market knew. Stefan the baker carried a sheet pan of hand pies out of his booth and started handing them out for free, and when they ran out he brought out a second one. Lottie cried, but a different kind of crying this time. Roxanne set a bucket with every carnation she had by the entrance and wrote on a piece of cardboard: "FREE. TODAY WE CELEBRATE."

By evening the carnations were gone. Every last one.

---

That evening we sat at Mrs. Mazur's. Eight people in a kitchen built for three. Mrs. Mazur, me, Val, Mikey and Lana, Old Pete, Ms. Pratt and Goldie. It was Goldie's first time. She stood in the doorway in her coat and didn't know what to do with it. Mrs. Mazur took the coat from her and hung it on a hook in the hall, right on top of her own.

"So you're the one with the bracelet?" asked Mrs. Mazur.

"I am," said Goldie.

"Skinny," said Mrs. Mazur. "Sit down. Eat."

Goldie sat down and ate three hand pies. Quickly, with concentration, like someone who hadn't eaten anything made by hand, for her, in a long time. I'd seen that look before. In the pine forest. It made me uneasy.

Val sat across from her. They looked at each other like two chemists over the same flask.

"Finch," said Goldie. "I know."

"Cochran," said Val. "So do I."

"Did he tell you about the steak?"

"About the steak, the snaps and the corkscrew spoon."

Goldie smiled slightly.

"Then you know everything," she said. "Except that I envied him that night. Not you. Him. That he could say no."

Val was quiet for a moment. Then she slid a fourth hand pie over to her. That said more than any words would have.

Old Pete opened the ledger and wrote, solemnly: "March sixth. Asset. Land. Ours. Five years."

Then he thought for a moment and added: "They gave it to us on purpose. So we'd let our guard down."

Everybody laughed. So did I.

---

That night, as always, the rings were on my hand. All four, since Sunday. On Sundays at six, after the workshop, the students gave them back to me, and I wore them all week so they'd keep growing. Ms. Pratt had put it into the instructions as point six: "The rings are kept by the instructor. The instructor is responsible for them."

And the notebooks were in the garage. In the drawer under the workbench. Thirty-one notebooks, in Mrs. Stern's two plaid shopping bags. I'd moved them there back in January, to read at night. And left them there. It seemed safer than my place: everybody and their brother comes through my apartment. Neighbors, students, Lugnut through the transom.

That was a mistake. You always understand your mistakes afterward.

---

Hank called on Saturday, at six in the morning.

"Timothy," he said. His voice wasn't angry. Just lost. "Come over. Now."

I was there in ten minutes. It was still dark. A streetlight hung over the garages.

The doors of Hank's garage stood open. Not broken into. Open.

The padlock, the one Mikey had hardened himself in January, lay in the snow under the hasp. The body was intact. But the shackle was gone. It had run. Thick, hardened, ornery steel had slid down like wax off a candle and fused to the bottom of the body in a heavy, shiny drop with an even, mirror-smooth edge. Not cut. Not sawed. No scale, no heat tint. The steel had simply stopped being a shackle.

I pulled the rings off my fingers, stuffed them in my pocket so they wouldn't get in the way, and touched the drop with the thimble alone.

And I recognized the handwriting.

Ashot had been right in December, when he read the mirror-smooth bolt: "angry handwriting." The power has handwriting, the same as a person. Mine is "roughly," with pores, with a ripple. Mikey's is hesitant, a hair at a time. This one was even, cold, without a single pore. Like a lens made from a handful of corundum in ten seconds.

"During the night," Hank said behind me. "I looked out the window at two; my kitchen faces the garages. The streetlight was on, everything was shut. Then I got up at five, and there it was."

I looked at the snow. Tracks ran from the road to the doors. One set. Even, unhurried, from good boots with a fine tread. There and back. A man who doesn't like leaving his house had come out in person for the second time this winter. He'd needed to touch it.

Inside it was cold. Not the air: with the doors open, the air was just the outdoors by now. It was the things that were cold. Frost on the workbenches. Frost on Hank's car, parked outside by the doors, as thick as in a freezer. The wood stove, which Hank had stoked the night before with the last of the pallets, was ice-cold. Not cooled off: ice-cold. There'd been a kettle of water on Hank's workbench. The water had frozen solid to the bottom, and the bottom had bulged out like a blister.

I did the math. A shackle is half a bag, if you understand steel. Even for me, a couple of bags. But he'd frozen everything. The car, the doors, the stove, the kettle. He hadn't needed that much. The heat was Hank's: his stove, his car, his kettle. He'd taken it so he wouldn't have to draw on himself. And he'd taken more than he needed, on purpose. So that I'd know. Like that time with the pot at the meat-pie stand.

The drawer under the workbench had been pulled out. Empty.

The plaid shopping bags were gone.

The notebooks were gone. All thirty-one. Experiment one hundred seven. The ninth volume, with "let those hands know each other." The eleventh, with the bronze socket. Thirty years of Professor Stern's life. His fear. His "Don't search."

Everything else was where it had been. Bull's torque wrench. The coolant. Ashot's shoe-stool with the cracked upper. Mikey's pallets. Hank's welding cable, with no welder to go with it. Val's jar of thermometers.

He had taken only the notebooks.

And he'd left a note. On the workbench, weighed down with my own hammer. A sheet of heavy white paper, the same kind of paper as the notes I'd seen in his study, lying beside the quartz desk. One word, in the same small, even handwriting that had been on the bolt in December:

"Don't search."

---

I stood over the note for a long time.

"Don't search." Professor Stern's words. From a notebook Glen had never seen, until I said them out loud myself, in the pine forest. Now he'd given them back to me. Like a slap in the face. Like an answer.

Twenty years ago he'd walked out on Professor Stern with the object, and the professor had written: "Don't search." Now he'd walked out on me with the notebooks and written the same thing. As if he'd closed a circle.

"Timothy," Hank said quietly. "Who is this?"

I didn't answer.

"Hands again," said Hank. "No marks. Like in January."

He didn't say "Mikey." But I saw him think "Mikey." And forbid himself to think it. And not quite manage.

"It's not Mikey," I said. "I've had all the rings since Sunday. All four. And Mikey can't do this. None of our people can. Look at the drop: not a single pore. Mikey straightens a paper clip a hair at a time. This is somebody who's been doing it like this every day for twenty years. And look at the tracks. Size eleven and a half. Mikey wears a seven."

Hank looked at the tracks. Then at me.

"You know who," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I know."

"Going to the police?"

"And tell Sommers what? That a man from the pine forest came in the night, dissolved a padlock with his hands and walked off with a dead professor's notebooks? Sommers will type 'with his hands.' For the second time."

"Then I'm not going either," said Hank. "Second time 'with his hands,' the whole station'll be laughing at me." He paused. "That's twice this winter for me. First time, the welder. Second time, your notebooks. I gave you the garage because you fixed my hinge. And now my garage is a public thoroughfare for people who don't leave marks."

That was fair.

I promised him new doors. Solid ones. Hank said there was no such thing as steel that can't be sweet-talked; he knew that now. I said I knew it too. But I'd make doors that would scream if somebody tried to sweet-talk them. So the whole yard would hear.

Hank thought about it and said, "Now that's something. An alarm that cusses you out." And smiled, just barely.

I didn't open the booth that day. When I called Ashot, he said, "Go. I'll take orders for both of us." We canceled the Saturday evening workshop.

---

Mikey came at eight. He'd heard from Hank. He stood by the doors, white-faced, in a hoodie, no hat, looking at the drop.

"Tim. It wasn't me."

"I know."

"Everybody's going to think it was me."

"Not everybody. Hank doesn't anymore. And we'll tell the rest."

Mikey looked at the drop. Then he suddenly said:

"He wanted them to think it was me. Right? He knew about January. He knew that if there were no marks, the first one they'd think of was the one who'd already opened something without leaving marks."

That hadn't occurred to me. It had occurred to Mikey. Because he was the one who'd stood in front of Sommers back then and said "with my hands."

"He read the chat," said Mikey. "Back then, before the password. Two hundred forty times. He knows everything about me." His voice shook. "Because of me."

"Not because of you," I said. "The chat's been locked since December. The chat didn't know about the notebooks in the garage. Everybody who came to the workshop knew. There were already five people in the garage on February twenty-second, and by Monday the whole market was talking about the rings. And on the first of March, Greg, Stefan and Roxanne came too. And he's got a man at the meat-pie stand. He'd have found out anyway. For twenty years he's been finding out about everybody who glows even a little. That's his work. He doesn't do anything else."

Mikey nodded. He didn't believe me. But he nodded.

---

Val got there around ten. She looked at the empty box. At the note. At the ice-cold wood stove.

At first she didn't say anything. Then she opened her oilcloth notebook. The one where, over January and February, forty pages of excerpts had piled up. Two columns: how the object was built and how it behaved.

"Not everything," she said quietly. "But the main things. Experiment one hundred seven. The ninth volume. The socket in the eleventh. Mrs. Stern's words about the pearl. The composition. The density. The funnels. The rings. Our water glasses."

Then she closed the notebook, and her face became the one I'd seen only once before, on the stairs at Mrs. Mazur's.

"Without the notebooks, it's a retelling," she said. "The reviewer will ask: where's the original? A paper with Stern as first author, and not a single page of Stern's." She paused. "He took my primary source, Tim. My reference number one."

Then she pressed the notebook to her chest, the way Ms. Pratt holds her folder.

"But our two months are right here. That he didn't take."

Glen had made a mistake. He thought the knowledge was in the notebooks. But it wasn't only there anymore. It was in Val's forty pages. In Ms. Pratt's six-point instructions. In Ashot's "Just the heel. Three-quarters of an inch." In Mrs. Mazur's "So it doesn't run. But so it stays jam." In the four rings in my pocket, which were growing and would teach the next ones.

What's spread out across a dozen pairs of hands, you can't carry off in two plaid shopping bags.

But he'd taken something else too. Twenty volumes he'd never seen. "What's in the last volumes?" he'd asked me in the forest. I'd said, "This and that."

Now he knew.

---

In the afternoon I went to Garden Street. They were her notebooks. I had to tell her myself.

Mrs. Stern heard me out without a word. The desk lamp was on. Professor Stern's watch was ticking.

"Will you file a report?" I asked.

"No," she said. "Twenty years ago Ephraim said, 'Then that's how it had to be.' I was very angry with him at the time." She paused. "Now I think he may have been right. The notebooks were written for whoever would read them. Glen will. He always read everything down to the last page. Even what he hadn't been assigned."

She looked at the lamp.

"And another thing, Timothy. Glen always returned books. Late, sometimes a year late. With notes in the margins. But he returned them."

I didn't know what to say to that. I sat and looked at the empty bronze socket in the lamp.

That evening I got out my notebook and found the number I'd copied off the bolt in December. I dialed. It rang. It rang again.

I hung up.

If I called, it would be exactly what he was waiting for. "When they come, call me." He wanted me to get scared and call. I was scared. But I didn't call.

---

On Sunday, eight out of twelve came to the workshop.

Roxanne texted that she had her carnations to think of, and carnations mustn't get upset. Stefan didn't text anything. Greg came, but said it was just to watch, and didn't take a glass. Glen's move had worked. Halfway.

Bull came. He looked for a long time at the drop, which Hank had laid on the workbench like evidence. Then he said, like a mechanic:

"Nice work. I couldn't do that." And then, in a different voice, the debt collector's: "I know the type, Timothy. That's no thief. A thief takes what he wants and goes. This one left a note. Guys like that scare you first. Then they write."

"Write where?"

"Someplace where you can't prove anything yourself," said Bull. "For three years now I've been getting letters like that. Tips on debtors. Unsigned. You know who writes them? People who are scared to come out and say it to your face."

Ms. Pratt said:

"Theft of property belonging to Mrs. Bella Stern. She is the one who has to file the report."

"She won't," I said. "I've been to see her."

Ms. Pratt pressed her lips together.

"Then at least we'll put it on record here. Point seven of the instructions: 'Keep records in several places. In different hands.'"

Old Pete wrote it down. Underlined it. Then he took off his hat, looked inside it as if the answers might be in there, and put it back on.

"I told you," he said. "On purpose. So we'd let our guard down. And then, bam."

"You did," I said. "You were right, Mr. Palmer."

Old Pete looked at me, thrown. Almost frightened. Like a man who, for the first time in thirty-one years, had been told he was right not on paper, but in real life.

"That doesn't count," he said. "That was an accident."

"It's on record," said Ms. Pratt. "So it counts."

---

That night I didn't sleep.

I sat in the kitchen with the thimble and the four rings on my hand. Lugnut hadn't left my side all evening. Cats can tell when something's missing from a home.

Why? The notebooks, sure. For twenty years he'd wanted to know what was in the last volumes. But why now? And why the note?

He hadn't waited for them to come for us. He'd come himself. Not to snuff us out. To scare us. So that twelve people in a garage would look at the drop and think: maybe we really shouldn't. Better to keep it quiet. Better to do it his way.

The workshop, the rings, a market at the dump: for him, that wasn't "we teach in the open." It was the end. Of his twenty years. Of his three clients. Of his alloys, which nobody else in the world makes. If a ring will grow on anybody's finger in nine days, then in a year half of Granton will be making alloys.

His monopoly was ending. And he'd understood that before I did.

I wrote in my notebook:

"Glen took the notebooks. Left 'Don't search.' Wanted them to think it was Mikey. Wanted us scared. Four got scared."

And below that:

"Bull: first they scare you, then they write."

---

As it turned out, he'd started writing before he started scaring.

On Monday, March ninth, at seven in the morning, the doorbell rang. Not the way neighbors ring. Long. Three times.

There were three men on the landing. Two in uniform. One in a gray overcoat, with a folder, and the face of a man who'd gotten up at five and considered it my fault. Behind them, on the stairs, was Officer Sommers. He looked at me and looked away.

"Timothy Nagel? Detective Keyes." He showed me his ID, then a sheet of paper. "We've received a report of illegal dealing in precious metals, and an investigation's been opened. This is a warrant to search your residence. Signed by a judge."

I looked at the paper. At the dates. The report had come in on the twenty-third of February. The warrant was dated the fourth of March.

The twenty-third of February. Almost two weeks before the theft.

I stood in the doorway and suddenly understood how it had all gone. He'd written first. He knew a letter like that would bring a search. And he knew that a search would turn up the notebooks: thirty-one of them, with the thimble on every page. And they'd be read. And for twenty years he'd lived so that nobody would ever read about the thimble.

That was why he'd come to the garage. Not just for the last volumes. He'd taken them so the police wouldn't. And so that they'd come to me without them.

It wasn't an impulse. It was a move. Like in chess, when a piece steps off its square and, just by stepping aside, puts you in check.

They brought in two neighbors as witnesses to the search: Old Pete and Katie from 40. Old Pete came up from the second floor in pajamas under his overcoat, with his hat in his hand, as if a hat were required of a witness. Katie came with a needle stuck in the collar of her cardigan, because they'd pulled her away from her sewing machine.

Old Pete looked at the detective, at me, at the two in uniform, and said quietly, but so that everybody heard:

"I knew it. I always knew it. I just thought it would be the property management company."

---

The search took two hours.

They turned everything upside down. The wardrobe. The shelf, where all that was left of the books was *Crystal Physics*, *The Properties of Wood* and Goldie's business card. The kitchen. My iron chair, made out of a bed frame. The fridge, which didn't hum anymore.

The detective leafed through my notebook. For a long time.

"'Hand pie: a tenth of a bag,'" he read out loud. "'Balance: minus thirty-one.' 'Science: minus twelve.' 'Travel Expenses: minus eight.'" He looked up. "Is this some kind of code?"

"It's bookkeeping," Old Pete said from the corner, as a witness, without being asked. "Mine. Between neighbors. No money involved."

Keyes looked at him. He wrote: "Notebook containing records of a household nature." And put it back.

Then he noticed the rings on my hand.

"What are these?"

"Copper. Student ones," I said. That was almost true.

He took a closer look. He wrote: "On the fingers of the right hand: rings, white and yellow metal, four, worn continuously, personal items." The thimble he didn't write down. An ordinary tailor's thimble. Dark. With dimples. It hadn't been in any report.

Glen knew you couldn't write about the thimble. Write about the thimble, and they'll come for you too.

Lugnut had been sitting on the windowsill the whole time. And when Keyes bent over my toolbox, Lugnut jumped down, slipped under his arm and made off with his pen. A shiny one, metal. Keyes straightened up and looked at the table, at the cat under the couch, at me.

"He'll give it back," I said. "Sometimes."

Keyes sighed and took a second one out of his pocket.

Toward the end, one of the uniforms opened my father's tackle box. He took out the case of hooks. Opened it.

Between the bobbers and the jigs lay the pea of gold. The same one, from October, from the military-surplus boards, from my kettle. Next to it, half of Grandma's little silver bar and the half handle that was left of Mrs. Mazur's spoon.

Keyes picked up the pea with two fingers and held it up to the light.

"Do you have papers for it?"

The same question the pawn shop had asked me in October. Word for word.

"No."

"Where did it come from?"

"Circuit boards. Old ones. Military-surplus connectors. I collected it myself."

"Collected it yourself," Keyes repeated, and wrote it down. "'Yellow metal, presumably gold, weight approximately six grams. White metal, presumably silver, two fragments.'" He looked up. "The report says you systematically manufacture items from precious metals and sell them through the market. White gold with palladium. Bracelets."

The bracelet. Goldie's. With my spring.

For a second I thought: Goldie? Then I remembered: she'd sent that bracelet into the pine forest. Through his security. And in the forest I'd been told, "Lenny Kurgan was just the beginning." Lenny had seen the pea in October. Lenny knew I'd turned down "We'll work together." Glen hadn't needed to make anything up. All he'd needed to do was put together what other people knew. The way Ms. Pratt puts together her observations.

"I fixed a bracelet," I said. "The spring. For half a bag. I rang it up in the app. There's a receipt."

"We'll sort it out." The pea and the little bar went into an evidence bag, which was sealed and signed. Old Pete signed too. His hand was shaking. Katie signed next to him and looked at me the way you look at a neighbor who's being taken away: with pity, and with a secret relief that it isn't you. "Get your things. We're going to go have a talk."

I put on my jacket. My father's rubber boots stood by the door. I looked at them and didn't put them on. Where I was going, they wouldn't help.

---

Out in front of the building stood a gray car with its flashers off.

The yard already knew. The yard always knows. Mrs. Mazur in her headscarf, Judy from the third floor, old Shirley with her knitting needles, Lana, just off her shift. And Mikey. He hadn't gone to school that day. He stood in front of everybody.

Mrs. Mazur stepped toward me. The detective started to stop her. She looked at him, and he didn't.

She stuck a paper sack in my pocket. A warm one.

"Cabbage," she said. "Let them see we're not starving."

Like that time before the forest. Word for word.

Mikey didn't say anything. He took out his phone, turned away and started typing fast. Later I found out: it was the workshop chat, the one he and Ms. Pratt had set up in February, with a password and the three rules in the description. One message:

"They took Tim. About gold. Need Goldie. Need Ms. Pratt. Need Bull. Everybody."

Old Pete read it over his shoulder and said:

"The books are open."

The car pulled away. I looked at the yard, at the building, at my own windows on the fourth floor. Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill. With somebody else's pen in his teeth. Watching the car, without blinking.

Glen had said: whoever glows draws the people who are looking for light.

He'd forgotten that light doesn't only draw the ones who want to put it out.
