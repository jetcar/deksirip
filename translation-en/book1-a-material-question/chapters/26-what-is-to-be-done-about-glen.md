# Chapter 26. What Is to Be Done About Glen

On Monday I woke up at eleven and couldn't figure out right away where it hurt. It hurt everywhere, but differently. My legs, from two days on the snow. My back, from the corner joints. My stomach, from forty-two hand pies. And my head hurt on its own account, and not from the hand pies.

Lugnut was sitting on the windowsill, looking at me the way a cat looks at a person who hasn't slept at home for two nights. Reproachfully, and with a certain calculation: had I brought anything shiny?

I hadn't brought anything. Just a hand pie in a napkin. It was still in the pocket of my jacket, flattened like a letter nobody ever mailed.

I put it in the fridge. On the top shelf, next to Mrs. Mazur's jar of pickles. Lugnut followed it with his eyes and lost interest: the hand pie didn't shine.

---

The last week of the old market was a strange one. Like the last week in an apartment you're moving out of: your things are all still in their places, but there's already nowhere to live.

Greg sold meat until lunch, and after lunch he hauled his chopping blocks out to the vacant lot. Lottie was selling her fish at a discount, "so I don't have to haul it." Double-A was selling batteries one at a time, because he'd run out of packs and had already ordered the new ones sent to the new address. Ashot tapped. He tapped all week, louder than usual, as if he wanted to finish tapping out on the old booth everything he hadn't gotten around to in twelve years.

And everybody asked the same thing.

"And what about him?" Lottie would ask, and nod somewhere off to the north, toward the pine forest, though you can't see the forest from here.

"Who?" I'd ask, though I knew.

"The one in the gloves."

I didn't know what about the one in the gloves. So on Tuesday evening we got together to decide.

---

We got together under the new roof, by Mrs. Mazur's kitchen. The kitchen was a permanent fixture now: Mr. Abrams had put it into the plan as a "food service facility, noncommercial," and Ms. Pratt said the wording was debatable, but she wasn't going to get into it.

Everybody who had held a joint came. And about ten more people who hadn't held anything but had opinions.

There turned out to be a lot of opinions.

"Hit him in the wallet," said Old Pete. He was sitting on a crate with his ledger on his knees, like a presiding judge. "He owes us. The frame, that's one. The notebooks, two. Our nerves, three. I've added it up."

"How much?" asked Bull, with professional interest.

"Nerves can't be added up," Old Pete admitted. "But I wrote them down."

"We should do it our way," said Bull. Everybody looked at him, and he put up his hands. "Not like that. For three years I've been going to see people who owe. And for eighteen before that, cars that won't run. You know what I figured out? Not once did it help that I'm scary. What helped was showing up on time. Every Friday. The way Timothy did with me."

"And what exactly are you proposing?" asked Mrs. Mazur.

"Don't know," Bull said honestly. "Go see him on Fridays."

Ms. Pratt was quiet for a long time. Everybody was looking at her, because she had something nobody else had: the right to show up at a person's door with an audit. I thought: years of alloys, three big clients. His books were surely clean. But books are only clean until the audit.

"No," said Ms. Pratt. "The Revenue Department is not a blunt instrument. If I go after him because he broke our frame, I'll be just like him. I will go to him when his turn comes up on the schedule. If it comes up."

It was the longest thing she said all evening, and nobody argued.

Goldie went over the legal side. No notes, ticking the points off on her fingers.

"The theft of the notebooks," she said. "Earlier this month Ms. Pratt said they were Mrs. Stern's property. But Mrs. Stern gave them away herself: 'Take them. Take all of them.' That's a gift; the property was handed over in person. So the injured party is Timothy, and he's the one who can file the report. They'll take it. Then they'll ask what's in the notebooks. Timothy will tell them. Detective Keyes will write down: 'Reshapes.' He's already fond of that word."

"And the frame?" asked Ashot.

"The frame is standing," said Goldie. "Damage: zero. Witnesses saw frost. The report would read like this: 'Mr. Rusk stood behind the fence with his hand on a rail, which caused our frame to begin to bend.' The desk officer will take it. Then they'll decline to open a case, and the whole department will be reading it out loud. And I'm not sure I want them to take it."

"Why not?" asked Lottie.

"Because then how we held it goes on the record," said Goldie. "And for now, that's ours alone."

Val was sitting next to me and not saying anything. Then she spoke, without raising her voice, but in a way that made everything go quiet:

"He's been practicing for twenty years. Every day. We've been at it since October, and only Tim, at that. Everything we know, we know from Professor Stern's notebooks, and Professor Stern, in his own words, 'understood a great deal, and dared nothing.' This man dared. Twenty years of experiments nobody has ever seen. If he goes to prison, that knowledge goes with him. If he's ruined, he'll burn it. I don't need him punished. I need what's in his head."

"Pragmatic," said Old Pete, and wrote it down.

"Scientific," Val corrected him.

Everybody looked at me. I understood that this was what "deciding" meant: everybody has had their say, and you have to choose.

I thought about the hand pie in the fridge. About the way Glen had looked at Mrs. Stern. About "I didn't want it to fall." About how he was many times stronger than any of us and still lived in a house with no tracks in the snow.

"I'm going to go see him," I said. "I'll invite him to teach."

It got completely quiet. Even Old Pete stopped writing.

"Teach," Greg repeated. "Him. Us."

"Him teaching us. And maybe us teaching him. He doesn't understand us either. He said so himself."

"He nearly dropped a frame on your head," said Bull.

"Well, he didn't."

Mrs. Mazur wiped her hands on her apron.

"Well, if you're going," she said, "then not with a hand pie. Third time, you bring a pie. I'm telling you that as a cook. A hand pie says, 'Here, have a bite.' A pie says, 'Sit down, let's talk.'"

---

Mrs. Mazur baked the pie on Friday, starting at six in the morning, and she baked it in a way I had never seen her bake.

Slowly.

Usually her dough flies. Kneading, rolling, shaping, all on the go, arguing with Old Pete and not even looking. This time she kneaded in silence. Twenty minutes. Then she laid her palms on the dough and just stood there.

"Mrs. Mazur, what are you doing?"

"Listening," she said. "It's nervous today."

"The dough?"

"You're nervous too. It's picking it up from you. Out."

I went out onto the stairs. Val came out after me. We stood by the window on the fifth-floor landing. From down below, from the second floor, we could hear Old Pete pacing behind his door with the DON'T RING. I KNOW. sign: he was nervous too.

"Don't try to talk him into it," said Val.

"Then what?"

"Invite him. That's all. You only talk someone into something when you need it more than he does. I need it more. You don't. You just feel sorry for him."

"I don't feel sorry for him."

"You do," she said. "You've felt sorry for him since winter. Ever since you came back from that house and said, 'A very beautiful one. Empty.' I heard you. That wasn't an assessment. That was 'poor guy.'"

I didn't argue. When Val is right, arguing with her is like cussing out the riser.

An hour later Mrs. Mazur brought out the pie. Round, golden, in her enamel pie pan with the chipped rim, wrapped in a dish towel with roosters on it. Cabbage and egg. Heavy: four pounds, give or take.

"Bring the pan back," said Mrs. Mazur. "It was my mother's."

---

An hour after that, Bull drove me out to the pine forest.

"I'm not going in," he said in the car. "I'll wait by the gate. If you're not out an hour after you go in, I start making calls. Not to the police. To Goldie."

"That's the right way to do it," I said.

"I know it's the right way. That's how I've been working for three years."

The gate was shut. Back before New Year's it had opened by itself; now it stood there like a wall: dark metal, seamless, no handle. I pressed the button on the intercom. The intercom answered in the guard's voice, big and quiet even through the speaker:

"The owner is unwell."

"I know," I said. "I'll wait."

"You'll be waiting a long time."

"I've got a pie," I said. "It'll stay hot for another hour. After that it'll be cold, but it'll still taste good."

The speaker was silent for a moment, then clicked off.

I waited.

By the calendar, spring was a week old already; by the weather it hadn't begun. The snow had settled and gone black at the edges, and the pines were dripping. I stood by the gate with a pie in my hands, in my father's rubber boots, and I had to laugh: a man who'd built a market in a weekend with sixty neighbors, standing outside a fence like a vacuum cleaner salesman.

Through the gap under the gate you could see the yard. And I noticed something.

In December the snow in the yard had lain smooth as a tablecloth, without a single track. Now it was melting, and there were tracks in it. Lots of them. Size eleven and a half, back and forth, from the front steps to the fence and along the fence, in circles. Somebody had been walking around his own yard. Every day. For a long time. The way people walk when they're thinking.

Forty minutes later, the gate opened.

---

Inside, everything was almost the same. The empty living room, the stone floor, the display cases. Only one case stood empty. The one where the sphere inside a sphere had sat before New Year's.

Glen was waiting for me on the stairs. In a gray sweater, in his gloves. His face was as gray as the sweater. He hadn't been sleeping. You can always tell with people who don't sleep: the skin under their eyes goes as thin as paper.

"What happened to the sphere?" I asked instead of hello.

"I broke it," said Glen. "On Sunday, when I got back. First time in twenty years I've acted on emotion. Unprofessional." He paused. "You came after all."

"Mrs. Mazur said the third time calls for a pie."

Glen looked at the dish towel with the roosters.

"Mrs. Stern says that too," he said. "Used to say it. Twenty years ago. Come in."

On the quartz desk in his study lay Professor Stern's notebooks.

Not in bags, not in a box. Laid out. All thirty-one, fanned out by year, like cards in a game of solitaire. Several of them were open. There was pencil in the margins. Small, even writing, as if it had been typeset, next to Professor Stern's sharp handwriting, which got more and more uneven toward the last volumes.

I set the pie down on the edge of the desk and took off the towel. Steam rose from the pie, and the smell of cabbage was so out of place in that study that I almost apologized.

Glen didn't look at the pie. He was looking at the notebooks.

"I read them," he said. "All of them. In three weeks. I wanted to know what he'd written in the last volumes. I left during the eleventh volume. And he wrote twenty more."

"And what did he write?"

Glen picked up one of the notebooks. The twenty-ninth: I recognized it by the tea stain on the cover. He opened it at a bookmark. Handed it to me.

It was in Professor Stern's handwriting, uneven, with no formulas and no numbers:

"Eighteen years since Glen left. I did not report it then. Bella said I should have. I think the fault is my own. I taught him alone, on Fridays, behind a closed door. I taught him the most important thing I had learned myself: that this must never be shown to anyone. He is the best of all those I have taught. That is exactly what he learned."

Below that, small, in pencil, was Glen's handwriting. One word.

"Yes."

I closed the notebook and put it down on the desk. We were both quiet for a while.

"Twenty years," said Glen. "For twenty years I thought he'd cursed me. That I was a thief. I am a thief. But he didn't curse me. He cursed himself. In the twenty-ninth volume. And I only found out now. Because I stole again."

He said it evenly, without any self-pity. Like a report on an alloy: composition such-and-such, defect such-and-such.

"The report to the police. Was that you?" I asked.

I hadn't meant to ask. It asked itself.

"It was," said Glen. "Not personally. But it was me. It can't be taken back; it isn't a book, you can't return it with notes in the margins." He looked at me. "Lenny mentioned a pea. If you made it with that"—he nodded at my thimble—"it'll come back four nines. Nobody gets that at home."

"I know."

"Your lawyer will think of something. She's smart. She sent me her bracelet through my security and never once asked who I was. That's a rare quality. And then she filed a statement against me. Signed. That's rarer still."

---

I sat down. Without being invited, on the same chair I'd sat in back in December.

"I came to invite you," I said. "To teach."

Glen didn't answer. He was looking at me the way he'd looked at the hand pies in December: for a long time, without understanding.

"On Sundays we have the workshop," I said. "In Hank's garage. There's Ms. Pratt, the revenue auditor: she makes her joints ruler-straight and says each one out loud, like reading a report into the record. Bull, the debt collector. Ashot, the cobbler. Mrs. Mazur: you felt her. Mikey, who's fifteen. We do everything 'roughly.' You do everything precisely. Without a single pore. I saw your bolt. I can't do that. None of us can. You've spent twenty years doing what we've only just started."

"And you want me to teach that to Mikey," said Glen. "A fifteen-year-old who opens garages on a bet."

"He doesn't anymore."

"Yet."

"Yet," I agreed. "That's exactly why he needs teaching. Not just how. Why, too. And when not to."

Glen got up and went over to the window. It ran from the floor to the ceiling, and beyond it stood wet pines.

"You don't understand what you're asking," he said. "I'll tell you what you don't understand. On Sunday I stood by that rail and did what I know how to do. And I could feel every one of you. Nine rings. Weak as a whisper. But every one of them works. In a month there'll be twenty. In a year, two hundred. In ten years, someone out of those two hundred will want to do what I did. Only not let the joints ease off: dissolve them. Not a frame: a bridge. Have you thought about that?"

"I have," I said. "Since New Year's. Since Mikey and that padlock spring."

"And what did you come up with?"

"That on Sunday you showed us yourself," I said. "One strong one against many weak ones. If there's one like you in a thousand, and the other nine hundred ninety-nine know how to hold, he won't be able to do a thing. A bridge won't fall if every joint has its own person standing on it. I didn't come up with that. You tested it. On us."

Glen stood with his back to me and was silent for a long time.

"That's beautiful," he said at last. "That's naive. That may be true. I don't know."

"So we'll test it. All of us together."

"No," he said. And turned around. "No, Timothy. I won't come."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know how to be around people." He said it as evenly as he'd said it about the report. "For twenty years I've talked to my driver in gestures. To my clients, through my security. I've talked more with you just now than with everyone else put together in the past year. I'll come to your garage, Ms. Pratt will say 'Joint forty-seven,' and I'll tell her she's doing it wrong. And she'll cry. Or I will. I don't know what will happen, and that frightens me more than two hundred rings."

I wanted to say that Ms. Pratt doesn't cry. That at the Revenue Department she'd heard worse. That Mikey hadn't known how to be around people either, and he'd learned. That six months ago I hadn't talked to anybody myself except customers and my cat.

I didn't say it. Val was right: you only talk someone into something when you need it more than he does.

"All right," I said. "The invitation stands. Sundays, ten in the morning, Hank's garage. Every week."

"I won't come."

"Then it'll just go on standing."

Glen looked at the pie.

The pie had gone cold, but it smelled even stronger. Cabbage always smells stronger as it cools.

He took off his right glove. The hand was as pale as his face. It was the first time I'd seen his hand without a glove. An ordinary hand. Long fingers, dry skin, the knuckles chapped from his own frost.

He broke off a piece of the pie. By hand, not with a knife. He sat down across from me and ate it. Slowly, the way he had in December.

"Cabbage," he said. "And egg. Dill. Sour cream in the dough." He paused. "And she listened to it today. For a long time."

"Twenty minutes or so," I said. "She said the dough was nervous. From me."

"It isn't nervous," said Glen. "It's very even. I couldn't have done that."

He broke off a second piece.

The first time, before New Year's: through a glove, and after checking for bugs. The second, by the fence: he didn't take it. The third: by hand, with no glove. Mrs. Stern, as always, was right.

---

When I was leaving, Glen walked me to the stairs. He didn't go any farther.

"Leave the pan," he said. "I'll finish the pie."

"That pan was Mrs. Mazur's mother's," I said. "It has to go back to her."

"I'll return it," said Glen. "I always return things. Late. But I return them."

Bull was waiting by the gate. He was leaning against his old blue sedan, looking at his watch.

"Fifty-eight minutes since you went in," he said. "Two more, and I'd have called."

"He said no," I said.

"And the pie?"

"The pie he took."

Bull thought about it.

"Well," he said, "it's like with a debtor. First he doesn't pick up the phone. Then he picks up and says he hasn't got the money. Then he pays. Not right away. But the first time he picks up, that's halfway there."

We drove off. I looked out the rear window. The gate closed. Behind it, in the yard, on the melting snow, somebody was walking along the fence again. In circles.

---

On March thirty-first, the Central Market closed.

I'm not going to say it was sad. It was sad and it wasn't. Sad, because twelve years. And not sad, because everybody was already set up at the new place, and the old market was like an old coat: you hate to throw it out, but nobody's going to wear it anymore.

Ashot took the sign that said SARKISIAN. SHOES. down off his booth. The old one, tin, which he'd cut out himself his first year. He held it in his hands for a while. Then he gave it to me.

"Reshape it," he said. "So it won't rust. I'll hang it inside, over the counter. Outside, let it be chalk. Chalk's more honest. You have to write it fresh every day."

I reshaped it. The rust back into iron, a thin layer, "roughly," at the price of a mug of tea from my thermos, which went as cold as Keyes's coffee. The letters came through. Ashot wiped the sign off with his sleeve and said it was "good as new, only better, because it's old."

Old Pete walked the aisles one last time with his ledger. He wrote:

"March thirty-first. Central. Closed. Balance: not struck here. To be struck there."

And an arrow. Pointing toward the bypass.

On the first of April the new market opened. On the first of April: April Fools' Day. Ms. Pratt said that Mr. Abrams had picked the date and that it was "formally permissible, but imprudent." Mr. Abrams said it was on purpose, so Cochran would think we were joking.

We weren't joking. On the first day there were more people under the plastic roof than the old market had ever seen on its best Saturday. Ashot tapped. Tap. Tap-tap. Tap.

On Sunday the fifth, at ten, the workshop met in Hank's garage. Twenty-six people came. Hank put in a second wood stove and said he wasn't "a garage anymore, he was a school with a car in it."

Glen didn't come.

At ten I looked at the door. And at five past. And at a quarter past. Val saw and didn't say anything. She just took my hand under the table for a second.

---

But two days before that, on Friday, April third, there was a notice in my mailbox. I'm telling this out of order, because that's the order it sits in my head.

"Package. Two pieces. Total weight: 36 lb 2 oz. Pick up at the post office. Photo ID required."

The sender wasn't given. Or rather, it was given, but the way a person gives it who doesn't want to be found and doesn't want to lie either: "Rusk, G., General Delivery."

I wasn't going to haul thirty-six pounds on the bus. I called Bull. Bull showed up twenty minutes later and said this was his second trip "on this case" in a week, and it was time he sent somebody a bill.

"Who?" I asked.

"Him," said Bull. "I told you: Fridays."

There was a line at the post office. The woman at the counter spent a long time looking for the package, then brought out two boxes, taped up so tightly you'd think there was a live animal inside that might get out.

"Heavy," she said. "What've you got in here, books?"

"Notebooks," I said.

"That's what I said. Books."

For twenty years the man hadn't trusted anybody, not even hand pies. And now he'd trusted the mail with thirty-six pounds. Maybe that was the letter.

---

We opened them at Mrs. Mazur's. That's just how it worked out. I called Val, Val called Mrs. Stern, and Mrs. Stern said she'd come by taxi, "but only if it's at Dorothy's," by which she meant Mrs. Mazur's, "because at Timothy's, Dorothy says, there's nowhere to sit." That's true: all I've got is one iron chair and a stool. It took her twenty minutes to climb to the fifth floor. On the second she rested by Old Pete's door, read his sign and said to the door, "I know too."

On Mrs. Mazur's table, between the jam and the sugar bowl, we cut the tape.

In the first box lay Professor Stern's notebooks. All thirty-one. Dry, flat, not one of them creased. Narrow paper strips stuck out from between some of the pages: the same heavy paper as the "Don't search." note. On the strips, small, in pencil: "Volume fourteen, page thirty-two: error in the calculation, see my sixth." "Volume twenty-two: Professor Stern is right, I checked." "Volume twenty-nine: yes."

Mrs. Stern picked up the twenty-ninth. Opened it at the bookmark. Read. Closed it. Took off her glasses.

"What did I tell you," she said. "With notes in the margins." She paused. "But he kept the bags. Bags aren't books."

Her voice didn't tremble. Her hands did, but she laid them on the notebook, and they stopped.

In the second box, on top, lay Mrs. Mazur's pan. Enamel, with the chipped rim. Scrubbed till it squeaked. Inside it, the dish towel with the roosters, washed and ironed. Folded in four, like a napkin in a restaurant.

Mrs. Mazur picked up the pan and studied it for a long time.

"He didn't wash it with magic," she said at last. "He washed it by hand. Right here, by the rim, where the enamel's chipped, it always burns on. The only way you get that off is by hand. For a long time."

Under the pan lay Glen's notebooks.

Twenty of them. Black, thick, hardbound, all identical. Each one with a year on it. From the year he left through last year. In every one, the same small, sharp handwriting: formulas, tables, diagrams, graphs. Not one "I'm afraid." Not one "Bella says." Twenty years of pure practice, written down the way Ms. Pratt writes down her joints: number, composition, cost, result.

Val took the first one. Opened it. Started reading. After a minute she turned the page. After two, another. Then she raised her head, and her face was the way mine must have looked back in October, when the wrench in my hand first went soft like modeling clay.

"Tim," she said. "He counted in kilowatt-hours."

"And?"

"All of it. Every experiment. Twenty years. How much he took, from where, how much went into the bonds, how much into heat." She turned a page. "Here. Sapphire. Cost by stage. Here's your welding: he calls it a 'homogeneous joint,' and his price is a third of yours, because first he warms it with heat from the boiler and then breaks the bonds while they're hot. You break them cold and pay for it out of yourself. And here…" She stopped and turned another page. "Here he writes about why he can't touch the living. With numbers. He tested it on dough. On yeast dough, Tim. Twenty years ago. The dough stopped rising. He writes, 'Do not repeat.' Underlined twice."

Mrs. Mazur looked at the notebook as if there were something in it about her personally.

"He's right to write that," she said. "Dough doesn't like people poking around in it."

Old Pete was sitting in the corner, not writing anything down. It was so unusual that eventually everybody looked at him.

"I don't know how to enter this," said Old Pete. "It isn't an expense and it isn't income. It's…" He searched for the word. "It's an inheritance."

---

There was no note. I went through all the notebooks, all the strips, shook out the boxes. Nothing. No "I'm sorry," no "use them," no "Don't search."

Only, on the last page of the last black notebook, last year's, there was one line in different ink, fresh, clearly added recently:

"It isn't the steel that holds a joint. It's whoever stands at it. Verified March twenty-second." And a signature: G. R.

I showed it to Val. Val read it and was quiet for a long time.

"I think this is the first entry in his notebooks with somebody in it besides him," she said. Then she thought and added: "This year's notebook isn't here. So he's still writing it." And then, very quietly: "Putting him down as a coauthor: we can't, not without his consent. So we'll ask."

---

Late that evening, when everybody had gone, I sat at home between the two boxes. Fifty-one notebooks. Thirty-one years of one man who was afraid, and twenty years of another who was afraid of nothing except people. Thirty-six pounds, two ounces.

Lugnut climbed into the empty box that Glen's notebooks had come in and settled down there, as if the box had been sent to him. Nothing in the box was shiny. But it smelled of somebody else's house, and apparently that was enough for him.

I sat and thought: what was to be done with all this?

Keep it at my place? It had already been stolen from me once. Out of the garage, overnight, in two plaid shopping bags. And the man who stole it had spent twenty years keeping everything to himself, and look what came of that: a house with no tracks in the snow and one word, "Yes," in the margin.

Give it to Val for the lab? The department doesn't even have money for reagents, never mind a safe.

Burn it? Mrs. Stern would have killed me. And she'd have been right.

It was the hand pie's twelfth day in the fridge. I remembered it, got up and took it out. It had dried out hard as a rock. I held it in my hand and wondered whether to give it back its softness: the water hadn't gone off into nowhere, after all. It had gone into the fridge, into the frost on the back wall, and you could ask for it back. I didn't. It wouldn't have been the same hand pie.

I threw it out. It had done its job.

Then I sat down and wrote in my own notebook, the graph-paper one I've had since October:

"Can't keep it in one apartment. We've been through that. Ms. Pratt's point seven: in different hands. And what if the hands are everybody's? Then where? Everywhere."

And I underlined it. Twice. Like Glen about the dough.
