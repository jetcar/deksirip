# Chapter 18. The First Apprentice

I took the folder back to Goldie on Wednesday, myself.

Her firm's office was on the third floor of a new building by the square, one of those buildings where, as Goldie had said, something breaks every week. The elevator didn't break. The girl at the reception desk looked at my work jacket the way, according to Goldie, the coat-check lady at the Summit would have suffered over it, and called upstairs. Goldie came down herself. In her coat, as if she were on her way somewhere, though I don't think she was on her way anywhere.

She opened the folder right there in the lobby. She read the "No" on the first page. She read the small print underneath. She looked up.

"'I've seen what it looks like,'" she repeated. "What did you see, Timothy?"

"A house," I said. "A very beautiful one. Empty."

Goldie was quiet for a moment. I could see that she wanted to ask whose house, and that she wouldn't ask. She closed the folder.

"Thank you for bringing it yourself," she said. "Usually people send it by courier. Or they don't send anything, they just stop picking up the phone." She smiled a little, briefly. "The steak was on the company. Not on you. So you don't owe anything."

"I know," I said. "Thanks anyway."

She walked toward the elevator. At the elevator she turned around.

"If you ever need a lawyer," she said, "call. Me. Not the company."

The elevator doors closed.

Outside, it was snowing. I stood there thinking that the "thirty times" had stayed behind on the third floor, in the folder, and that for some reason this made me feel lighter, not heavier. As if what I'd handed over wasn't money but a heavy bag I'd been lugging around for a week.

---

After that I went up to see Mikey.

Mikey opened the door in a hoodie, one earbud in. Behind him, two monitors glowed on his desk: a game on one, some kind of code on the other.

"Tim," he said. "What's up with you?"

"The chat," I said. "Ours. Stairwell Three Exchange. Who can read it?"

"Us," said Mikey. "Eight people."

"What about with the link?"

Mikey blinked. Then he turned to the monitor and clicked something. Then something else. Then he took out the earbud and set it on the desk.

"Uh-oh," he said.

"What's 'uh-oh'?"

"With the link, anybody can," Mikey said quietly. "I made it an open group so it'd be easier for old Shirley to get in. Her phone's ancient, she can never remember a password. And there's a view counter here…" He turned the screen toward me. "Tim, Mrs. Mazur's post about 'electricity out of sand' has two hundred forty views. And there are eight of us."

We both looked at the number. Two hundred forty. Eight people couldn't have viewed it two hundred forty times, not even if Old Pete reread every post to keep his books straight. That made two hundred thirty-some strangers. Glen doesn't read anything that many times. Which meant the link had been making the rounds.

"Lock it," I said. "Password. I'll help Shirley myself."

Mikey locked it. His fingers flew over the keyboard, but he had the face of a kid who'd just broken somebody else's window. Then, without turning around, he said:

"Is it because of me somebody found out about you?"

I thought about it. I could have said no. It wouldn't have been true.

"Partly," I said. "But partly because of me. I'm the one glowing. You just left the transom open. The light was mine."

Mikey nodded. Then he said:

"I won't do it again."

"I know," I said. "Listen. Saturday, at Mrs. Mazur's. Twelve o'clock. Come. There's something I want to try."

"What?"

"Teaching," I said.

Mikey spun around so fast that the earbud fell off the desk.

---

I spent a long time thinking about how to go about it.

I didn't know how to teach. I knew how to fix things. When somebody brings me an iron, I don't explain to them how an iron works. I just take it and do it. But this time I'd have to explain something I didn't understand myself. I feel steel the way you feel bathwater with your hands: warm, cold, cloudy, gritty. But how do you explain "warm water" to somebody who has no hands?

And do they even have hands?

Professor Stern had written in the ninth volume (I'd now reread that part, formulas and all): "The ability is like an ear for music. Some people have it, some don't. The thimble is not the ear. It is a hearing aid." So first you have to find out who can hear. Teaching them music comes after.

Val agreed right away. Too quickly, I thought later. On Saturday she came before anybody else, with a briefcase full of clinking glasses.

"Series Three," she said. "I've been thinking all week about how to test it. You can't ask, 'Do you feel anything?' Everybody feels something. That's not data. That's a mood."

"Then how?"

"Heat," said Val. "You said it yourself. Any change takes energy, and energy always comes from somewhere. If a person has even a drop of this ability, the first way it should show up is this: they want to change something, and somewhere nearby it gets colder. The simplest thing there is to measure."

On Mrs. Mazur's table she set out eight identical glasses, more than we needed. Into each one she poured the same amount of water from the same kettle, cooled to room temperature, two hundred grams apiece. She weighed it on Mrs. Mazur's kitchen scale and grumbled that the scale was off by two grams. She lowered a thermometer into each glass and stirred the water with it before every reading. The thermometers all said the same thing: twenty-one degrees Celsius, exactly.

"Everybody holds a glass in both hands for exactly three minutes," she said. "I measure before and after. I subtract 'before' from 'after': that's the result. First you just hold it. Your hands are warm, the water will warm up from them by a few tenths of a degree. That's the control: what the hand gives on its own. Then the second glass: you hold it and you want. You want the water to get colder. Don't think it. Want it. Then I compare: if the water in the second glass warmed up less than in the first, there's something there. And the difference between the glasses is what you did."

On a sheet of paper for Old Pete she drew three columns: "Before," "After," "Difference." And one more, off by itself: "Wanted minus control."

"And if it warms up more?"

"Then you just got nervous and your hands got sweaty," said Val. "That's data too."

"What about me?" I asked. "Without the thimble."

"You're a contaminated sample," said Val. "You've had the thimble for over two months. You're later. Separately."

---

Mrs. Mazur had baked hand pies for our arrival. There was never any question. "Studying on an empty stomach isn't studying, it's torture," she said, and set a platter covered with a dish towel on the table. It smelled of cabbage and apples.

Old Pete came too. Nobody had invited him. He came with his ledger, sat down in the corner and said:

"I'm an observer. I'll be taking notes. Without notes, science is just people sitting around chatting." He cast a sidelong glance at my thimble. "Timothy, I don't believe in these magic tricks of yours. But I write things down. The records will explain everything later on their own."

Val looked at him with respect. He noticed and sat up straighter.

Mikey arrived at exactly twelve. Without earbuds. For Mikey, that was like showing up in a suit.

Val held the glasses first. She'd decided that herself: "If it doesn't work, let it not work on me first, so nobody's feelings get hurt later."

Control: it started at twenty-one point zero and ended at twenty-one point eight. Plus eight tenths: that was what her warm hands gave.

The second glass. Val sat with it clasped between her palms, her eyes closed. Her lips were pressed together, and a crease had formed on her forehead. She was wanting. I could see her wanting. That's how you want to pass an exam you've already failed twice.

It started at twenty-one point zero and ended at twenty-one point nine. Plus nine tenths: one tenth more than just holding it. One tenth is within the error of the thermometer. Zero, she'd have said about anybody else's result. About her own, later on, she'd say "worse than the control."

Val opened her eyes. She looked at the thermometer. She wrote it down. She didn't say anything.

"Want to try again?" I said.

"Trying again is fitting the data to the result you want," Val said evenly. "Next."

---

Mikey was next.

Control: from twenty-one to twenty-one point seven, plus seven tenths. The second glass: Mikey sat staring at the water, focused, the way you stare at a screen where something's about to finish loading. From twenty-one to twenty-one point four. Plus four tenths.

Val looked at the thermometer twice.

"Your hands warmed the water seven tenths, and with wanting, only four," she said. "Three tenths went somewhere. That's on the edge of the error. But it's on the edge. Do it again."

Mikey did it again. Plus three tenths instead of seven. Then once more: plus two. Every time less than just holding it.

Val wrote down all three numbers. Then she looked up at Mikey. Her eyes were strange: shiny and dry at the same time.

"Do you feel anything?" she asked. The very question she'd said wasn't data.

"Well…" Mikey thought about it. "The water feels kind of… heavy. Like it doesn't want to. I don't know. Like ripping off a bandage."

I froze. A bandage. That was how the silicon had felt that first night. Like you're pulling, and it doesn't want to let go.

"Next," said Val. "Mrs. Mazur."

Mrs. Mazur wiped her hands on her apron and sat down at the table. She took the glass the way you take a cup of tea at somebody's house: with both hands, comfortably.

Control.

From twenty-one to twenty-one point one. Plus one tenth.

We all looked at the thermometer. Val's hands had warmed the water eight tenths, Mikey's seven, and Mrs. Mazur's hardly at all. Her hands were cold.

"Are your hands always cold?" asked Val.

"Always," said Mrs. Mazur. "As long as I can remember. That's a good thing. For dough, it's just the ticket. Dough doesn't like warm hands; the butter in it runs. That's why at the plant they put me in the pastry shop off the cafeteria, on puff pastry. The other girls would roll out puff pastry and their butter would run, and mine wouldn't. Thirty years on puff pastry."

Val slowly set down the glass.

"Mrs. Mazur," she said carefully. "Now the second glass, please. And want the water to get cold. As if it were dough, and you needed the butter not to run."

"Well, that I know how to do," said Mrs. Mazur, and picked up the second glass.

Three minutes. She sat there holding the glass and talking with Old Pete about how he salted his cabbage wrong. She didn't even look at the water.

It started at twenty-one point zero. It ended at nineteen point five.

Minus a degree and a half.

Minus.

The water in the glass had gotten colder than it was before Mrs. Mazur picked it up. It hadn't just warmed up less, like Mikey's. It had cooled down. Compared to the control, a full one point six. Val touched the glass and snatched her hand back: the glass was cool. Not warm from somebody's palms, like everybody else's. Cool.

Old Pete stopped arguing about cabbage.

"Those thermometers are no-name junk," he said. "They do it on purpose."

And he wrote the number in the ledger. Then he underlined it. Then he underlined it again.

"A degree and a half, in that much water, is more than a kilojoule," Val said quietly, looking into the glass. "She didn't do anything. Where did she put it?"

Nobody answered.

Val sat very still.

"You've been doing this your whole life," she said at last. "Every time you roll out dough. Thirty years on puff pastry. You take the heat out of the dough. And you don't even know it." She looked at the first glass. "And your control isn't a control. You were taking it then too."

"Taking, not taking," said Mrs. Mazur. "I work with my hands. The hands know."

"And your joints," I said. "By evening your fingers ache."

Mrs. Mazur looked at me.

"They ache," she said. "Twenty years now. The doctor says arthritis."

"What if it isn't only arthritis?" I said. "What if for thirty years you've been taking heat from the dough and from your own fingers too? Heat always belongs to somebody. And the closest thing to the dough is your hands."

Here, of course, I got carried away. I'm not a doctor. Val told me so afterward, and she was right. But Mrs. Mazur looked at her hands the way you look at old acquaintances you've suddenly learned something new about.

---

Then came the dough.

That was Mrs. Mazur's idea. "Why are you tormenting me with glasses?" she said. "You want to look, look at work." She took a block of dough and a block of butter out of the fridge, dusted the table with flour from a bag, like powder from a compact, and started making puff pastry. For a napoleon. New Year's was a week and a half away, and Mrs. Mazur's napoleon cake, as she put it, was "as mandatory as the fireworks."

Puff pastry is thin layers of dough and butter, stacked one on top of another. You wrap the butter in the dough, roll it out, fold it in thirds, roll it out again. Each time there are three times as many layers. If the butter is warm, it runs and the layers stick together. If the butter is cold, the layers lie flat, like the pages of a book. Then in the oven the water in the butter boils, the steam pushes the pages apart, and the dough rises in layers. Val explained all this to me later that evening. At the time, I just watched.

Mrs. Mazur rolled the dough out with a rolling pin. Slowly. Without strain. I put on the thimble and laid my palm on the edge of the table (not on the dough, on the table) so I could feel. Feeling isn't touching. Through wood I couldn't have changed anything even if I'd wanted to.

The table was wood, and I could barely feel through it. But I felt something else. How under the rolling pin, inside the block, the layers of butter were lying flat. Not just flat: flatter than I could have managed if I'd tried my hardest. Where the butter thinned out, the rolling pin seemed to linger on its own. Where it thickened, the pin seemed to pass more easily on its own. Mrs. Mazur wasn't thinking. Her hands knew where the butter was about to tear before it tore.

The block, though, wasn't getting any warmer. In a hot kitchen, with the oven on, under a rolling pin, for twenty minutes, and it stayed exactly the way it had been when she took it out of the fridge. That doesn't happen. I could feel it through the table and the thimble. The most heat went out of it when she folded the dough and pressed it down with her palms. Heat was leaving the dough and leaving her palms. Not into the air. Into the layers: into making the butter lie flat.

It wasn't magic. It was work. Thirty years of the same work, which, it turned out, had been something else all along as well.

"Timothy," said Mrs. Mazur, without turning her head. "Don't you go poking around in my hands with that hunk of metal. I can feel it."

I took my palm off the table. I hadn't been poking into anything. She'd felt me watching, though. From the inside.

She could hear me too.

---

The napoleon was baked by four. Eight layers, each one thin as paper and flaky as an old book. I broke off a piece of one, and it crumbled into translucent little leaves that melted in my mouth. Old Pete counted the leaves at the broken edge with a magnifying glass (he'd brought it with him, "for the records") and wrote: "Layers per sheet: approx. two hundred fifty. Exact count not possible. They melt."

"Two hundred fifty-six," said Mrs. Mazur. "Four book folds, four layers each time. I counted while I was folding. I always count."

Old Pete looked at his "approx. two hundred fifty" and corrected it without a word. The cook had turned out to be more precise than the accountant. He survived it, but just barely.

Meanwhile Mikey was sitting in the corner with a paper clip.

I didn't notice him right away. He was sitting hunched over the paper clip the way he hunches over his phone, holding it between two fingers. The paper clip had been unbent: he must have done it back at the table, while we were waiting for the napoleon. Now it was straightening out. Slowly. On its own. A hair at a time. Then another hair. As if it were remembering that it had once been a straight piece of wire.

"Mikey," I said.

Mikey flinched and looked at me. His face was pale, his forehead damp with sweat. The fingers holding the paper clip were white and ice-cold.

"Tim," he said. "It wants to. You know? It wants to be straight. I'm just… helping."

I took the paper clip from him. Almost straight. Not perfectly: there was a slight wave in the middle, "roughly," like everything I'd made my first week. But straight. Without a thimble. In the hands of a fifteen-year-old kid.

Steel doesn't remember anything. But where it's been bent, it's work-hardened and under strain, and it wants to rest: to get softer, more even. Like the paper clip in the Poly reading room, that very first time. Mikey had asked the bends to rest, and after that the paper clip had straightened out under his own fingers, a hair at a time. What wants to happen anyway: nearly free. He'd found that out on his own. On his very first day.

Nearly free for me, that is, with the thimble. Without a thimble, even "nearly free" had cost Mikey three hand pies and ice-cold fingers.

Then another thought came: what if he hadn't picked a paper clip? What if he'd picked something else that also wanted to happen on its own, only worse?

"I'm hungry," said Mikey. "Really hungry."

Without a word, Mrs. Mazur set a plate with three hand pies in front of him.

---

Val slipped out to the stairwell while everybody was eating the napoleon.

I found her between the fourth and fifth floors. She was sitting on a step with her arms around her knees, without her glasses. The glasses lay next to her on the step. That was bad. Val never sits around without her glasses, unless she's asleep, or… Anyway, when she takes them off, it always means something.

I sat down next to her. The step was cold.

"Don't," said Val. "Don't say anything. I'm going to talk now, and you don't interrupt."

I didn't interrupt.

"I've been in the department six years," said Val. "I know how a crystal lattice works. I know what enthalpy is. I know why puff pastry rises, and I know it better than Mrs. Mazur does. I know everything about hydrogen bonds. I explained them to you so well that on that Saturday you made a thread out of a bedsheet that holds over nine hundred grams. And she" — Val jerked her head upward, toward the fifth floor — "doesn't know what enthalpy is, and she does minus a degree and a half while talking about cabbage. And Mikey, who thinks chemistry is 'boring crap,' straightened a paper clip. Without a thimble. And me: plus nine tenths. Worse than the control."

She stopped. I waited.

"And the thimble stays silent on me," she said. "Remember, that time? You let me put it on. And nothing. Back then I thought: well, it's a fluke, one trial isn't data. Now I have data. Two glasses. Mine. And six other people's. I have everything except this. I understand more than anybody. And I can't do anything."

"That's not the same thing," I said.

"What isn't?"

"Understanding and being able to. You taught me that yourself. Mrs. Mazur can, but she doesn't understand. Mikey can a little, but doesn't understand at all. I can do more than anybody, but I understand like a first grader. You understand. And because of that, all of us can do it better. You saw my curve with the threads. Without you, it wouldn't exist."

Val was quiet for a long time.

"That's a consolation," she said at last.

"That's data," I said. "Series One."

She laughed. Briefly, through something that wasn't laughter. Then she put her glasses on. Then she looked at me through them, as if checking that I was still there.

"You know what's the most galling part?" she said. "Glen was right."

"About what?"

"About what you told me. 'Knowledge is something you have only as long as other people don't.' I was sitting here just now thinking: if none of them had been able to do it, I'd probably have been glad. I'd have been the only one who understands. And you'd be the only one who can. Just the two of us. Like him."

I went cold. Not from the step.

"And now?" I asked.

Val thought about it.

"Now I've got a better paper," she said. "Not about silicon. Not even about threads. About the fact that a lot of people have this. Quietly. Like an ear for music. That a factory cafeteria cook spent thirty years taking heat out of dough and thought she had arthritis. That a fifteen-year-old kid straightens a paper clip on his very first day, because the steel wants it to. That the lab tech in the chemistry department has nothing." She paused. "That's data too. A negative result is still a result. It's just that nobody wants to publish it."

"I do," I said.

"You're not a journal."

"I'll be the first reader."

She bumped me with her shoulder. Not hard.

"Come on," she said. "Or Old Pete will eat my piece of napoleon and log it under 'Science.'"

---

Old Pete really did log it. In the "Science" account, next to the minus twelve: "Napoleon, V. Finch's piece: minus one. Eaten by the observer."

That evening, when everybody had gone home, he sat at Mrs. Mazur's table with the ledger and the expression of a man who's found a hole in his balance. I was helping Mrs. Mazur with the dishes. Her hands were cold, as always. I noticed that now.

"Timothy," said Old Pete. "I have a question. An accounting question."

"Go ahead."

"If Mrs. Mazur," he said it carefully, "now knows that she… you know… takes heat out of the dough. And if she learns to take it better. Then she'll bake faster."

"Probably."

"Twice as fast," said Old Pete. "Let's say. Then there'll be twice as many hand pies. And our measure is the hand pie. Everything in the Exchange is counted in hand pies. If there are twice as many hand pies, a hand pie is worth half as much. Then my book, Timothy, my entire book, two months of it, is wrong. Your balance was plus forty, and now it'll be…" He tapped his pencil on the page. "That's inflation, Timothy. That's hyperinflation of the hand pie."

Mrs. Mazur turned off the water.

"Peter Palmer," she said. "I am not going to bake twice as much. I'm seventy-one years old. And don't you go writing me down as inflation. I'll bake the same amount. Just better."

Old Pete thought about it.

"Then a hand pie will be worth more," he said. "Because it's better. That's deflation."

"That's a napoleon," said Mrs. Mazur. "Eat."

He ate. And didn't write down anything else. Later I looked in the ledger: under the "minus one and a half" he'd written in large letters, in his accountant's hand: "Hand pie exchange rate: unchanged. Flavor: improved. Accounting: overwhelmed."

---

That night I sat in my kitchen writing in my notebook. Lugnut sat on the windowsill watching the snow. He often sat near me now when I wrote. I don't know what he was thinking.

I wrote:

"Val: nothing. Plus nine tenths, worse than the control. Understands more than anybody."

"Mikey: three tenths. Paper clip. Hungry. Cold fingers. Understands 'what wants to happen.'"

"Mrs. Mazur: minus one and a half, without looking. Thirty years on puff pastry. Cold hands, joints. Understands nothing, and doesn't need to."

Then I looked at that. And added at the bottom:

"The ability is like an ear for music. Professor Stern was right. Only the ones who hear aren't the ones who know the most. Or not only them. Maybe it's the ones who've worked with their hands the longest." Val would have said: three people isn't a sample. And Mikey hardly works with his hands at all; he works with his fingers, on a keyboard.

I thought some more. I remembered Ashot with his hammer, who can read people by their heel tips and an angry hand on a bolt. I remembered Greg, who knows by the sound what kind of steel he's holding. I remembered Bull, who'd spent eighteen years listening to engines. I remembered Glen, with his lens in ten seconds, and his fear that somebody would find out it could be done.

But it can be done. It isn't a secret anymore. Mrs. Mazur has it. Mikey has it. Maybe lots of people at the market have it. It's just that nobody knows they have it, because nobody's ever measured it with a glass of water.

I underlined that last line. Then I wrote one more, underneath:

"Need more hands. Where? Around. They've been around the whole time."

This time I underlined "Around." And I remembered for certain that I'd underlined it myself.
