## A. Chronology

Below are the book’s key dates. All dates for ancient periods are approximate: they are archaeologists’ estimates, and different studies vary by centuries—or, for the Paleolithic, by millennia. “c.” means “circa” or “approximately.” The chapter in which an event is discussed in greater detail appears in parentheses.

**Before the State**

- c. 300,000 years ago—the oldest known remains of anatomically modern humans (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco) (Ch. 1).
- c. 70,000–50,000 years ago—dispersal of modern humans from Africa across Eurasia (Ch. 1).
- c. 65,000–50,000 years ago (dates disputed)—humans in Australia; c. 45,000 years ago—in Europe; extinction of large animals on several continents over the following millennia (Ch. 1).
- c. 11,600 years ago—end of the last glacial period and beginning of the Holocene (Chs. 1–2).
- c. 9600 BCE—Göbekli Tepe: monumental structures before agriculture (Chs. 1–2).
- c. 9500–8500 BCE—domestication of wheat and barley, followed by goats and sheep, in the Fertile Crescent; independent centers of agriculture later emerge in China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, and New Guinea (Ch. 2).
- c. 7400–6000 BCE—Çatalhöyük: a large settlement with no clear traces of palace rule (Ch. 2).

**The First States**

- c. 4000–3100 BCE—Uruk: the first cities, temple economy, counting tokens, and seals (Ch. 3).
- c. 3200 BCE—writing appears in Mesopotamia and soon afterward in Egypt (Ch. 3).
- c. 3100 BCE—unification of Egypt (Ch. 3).
- c. 2600–2500 BCE—the pyramids of Giza (Ch. 3).
- c. 2600–1900 BCE—the Mature Harappan period in the Indus Valley (Ch. 6).
- c. 1754 BCE—the laws of Hammurabi (date conventional) (Chs. 3–4).

- third century CE—crisis of the Roman Empire and debasement of the currency (Ch. 5).

## B. Glossary of Terms

This section collects terms that appear more than once in the book. Definitions reflect the sense in which each word is used in the text. They are practical explanations for the reader, not strict textbook formulations. The chapters in which a concept first appears or is discussed in greater detail are listed in parentheses.

**Automation.** The transfer of work to machines and software—not only physical work, but mental work as well: accounting, calculation, and now some writing and analysis. Automation itself raises output, but does not determine who receives the gain (Chs. 10, 16, 17, 19).

**Barter.** The direct exchange of one good for another without money. Anthropologist Caroline Humphrey (1985) observed—and David Graeber made widely known—that societies living by barter alone are almost unknown: within a community, gifts and debt obligations usually prevail, while barter arises among strangers (Chs. 1, 4).

**Bullshit jobs.** David Graeber’s term for jobs that workers themselves acknowledge to be useless. Their true number remains disputed: a test based on worker surveys (Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood, and Brendan Burchell, 2022) found significantly fewer people in such jobs than Graeber believed. In the book’s framework, they are an example of deadweight expenditure of labor (Chs. 16, 17, 19).

**GDP (gross domestic product).** The market value of all final goods and services produced in a country in one year. GDP does not account for household labor, environmental depletion, or income distribution, while counting war and the work of repairing its damage as positive output. All GDP figures for ancient periods are reconstructions, principally those of Angus Maddison and the Maddison Project, which continues his work (Chs. 0, 10).

**GDP per capita.** GDP divided by the number of inhabitants. It is a rough measure of average living standards. For comparisons among countries and eras, it is usually converted at **purchasing power parity** so that the same notional sum buys the same basket of goods in different countries (Chs. 0, 18; App. C).

**The Great Divergence.** A phrase used by Samuel Huntington (1996) and popularized by Kenneth Pomeranz’s book (2000): the surge of Western Europe, which pulled ahead in income from the most developed regions of China, India, and the Middle East. Its causes are disputed: coal and colonies (Pomeranz), expensive labor and cheap energy (Robert Allen), a culture of knowledge (Joel Mokyr), institutions (Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson). Its timing is disputed as well: Pomeranz dates the beginning of the divergence to around 1800, while Stephen Broadberry and co-authors, using historical national accounts, place it one or two centuries earlier (Chs. 6, 9, 10, 18).

**External effect (externality).** The consequences of a transaction for those who did not take part in it. A negative example is a factory polluting a river; a positive one is knowledge spreading from a university throughout the surrounding area. Markets on their own account poorly for externalities (Chs. 11, 17).

**Hyperinflation.** Price increases so rapid that money ceases to function: under Phillip Cagan’s classic definition (1956), more than 50 percent per month. Classic cases include Germany in 1923, Hungary in 1946, and Zimbabwe in 2008. In the book’s framework, this is a destructive event: hyperinflation destroys savings and trust (Chs. 12, 15).

**Ambiguous case.** An expenditure that appears deadweight or even destructive but produces a developmental side effect, or vice versa. Examples include temple accounting that gave rise to writing, military research that produced radar and the computer, and the space race (Chs. 0, 3, 12, 14).

**Demographic transition.** The shift from high birth and death rates to low ones. Mortality falls first and fertility later, so population grows rapidly in between. Many countries have completed this transition; in others it continues (Chs. 11, 14, 17).

**Fiat money.** Money whose value rests not on metal, but on law and confidence in the state that issues it. Since 1971, after the United States ended the dollar’s convertibility into gold, all the world’s major currencies have been fiat money (Ch. 15).

**Trust among strangers.** A willingness to do business with people outside one’s circle of relatives and acquaintances. It is supported by courts, reputation, written contracts, and standards. In this book, it is one of the principal developmental “assets”: difficult to accumulate and easy to destroy (Chs. 0, 8, 18).

**Surplus.** Everything society produces beyond what is required for survival and simple reproduction: grain beyond subsistence, time beyond urgent work, energy beyond immediate needs. The book’s central question is where each era’s surplus went: into development, destruction, or deadweight expenditure (Ch. 0 and all subsequent chapters).

**Extraction, control, prestige.** Three forces identified in the introduction as the principal “absorbers” of the surplus apart from development. Extraction means taking by force or right. Control means spending resources to keep people and resources in submission. Prestige means spending them for status (Ch. 0).

**Institutions.** The “rules of the game” in society, in Douglass North’s definition: laws, property rights, customs, and ways of resolving disputes. Acemoglu and Robinson divide institutions into **inclusive** institutions, which give a broad range of people the incentive and opportunity to create, and **extractive** institutions, designed so that a narrow group can appropriate the fruits of others’ labor (Chs. 5, 9, 18).

**Inflation.** A sustained rise in the general price level—that is, a decline in money’s purchasing power. Moderate inflation of a few percent per year is considered normal in modern economics (Chs. 4, 15, 17).

**Capital.** Accumulated means of production: tools, machines, buildings, roads, canals. In the broader sense, it also includes **human capital** (people’s knowledge and skills) and **social capital** (trust and connections) (Chs. 2, 10).

**Keynesianism.** An approach associated with John Maynard Keynes: during a crisis, the state should support demand by spending and lowering taxes and interest rates. It predominated in the policies of advanced countries from roughly 1945 to the mid-1970s (Chs. 12, 14).

**Malthusian trap.** Named after Thomas Malthus. A model in which growth of production over the long run was accompanied by population growth and did not produce a sustained rise in income per person. Its applicability to different societies and centuries is disputed; the industrial era brought prolonged growth in income per person in many countries (Chs. 2, 7, 10).

**Neolithic Revolution.** The transition from hunting and gathering to agriculture and animal husbandry, which began around twelve thousand years ago in several centers of the world independently of one another. The word “revolution” is approximate: the transition unfolded over millennia (Ch. 2).

**Neoliberalism.** The name commonly used in the literature—more often by critics than supporters—for a policy course pursued since the late 1970s: privatization, deregulation, free movement of capital, and giving the fight against inflation priority over the pursuit of employment (Ch. 15).

**Planned economy.** An economy in which a central body rather than the market makes the principal decisions—what to produce, how much, and at what price. Difficulties of such an arrangement include gathering reliable information about needs and capabilities, providing feedback, and correcting mistaken decisions (Ch. 13).

**Platform.** A digital intermediary that brings together two or more sides: sellers and buyers, drivers and passengers, authors and readers. A platform earns money from access to users and from data about them (Chs. 16, 17).

**Coinage debasement.** Reducing the precious-metal content of a coin while retaining its face value. This is a hidden tax: the state gains immediately, but undermines confidence in money. The classic example is the Roman denarius of the third century (Chs. 4, 5).

**Labor productivity.** The amount of product created by one hour of labor (or one worker). The principal measure of development in the book’s framework: a society becomes sustainably richer when its output per hour rises (Chs. 0, 10, 18).

**Developmental, destructive, deadweight.** The three categories into which the book divides major social actions. Developmental action increases output, knowledge, access to goods, or resilience. Destructive action destroys capital, knowledge, trust, or people. Deadweight action consumes resources without increasing either productivity or well-being (Ch. 0).

**Rent and rent-seeking.** Rent is income received not for creating something new, but for control over something scarce: land, a license, a monopoly, a bottleneck. Rent-seeking is the expenditure of effort and money to obtain or retain such income—for example, lobbying for a privilege. In the book’s framework, it is a typical deadweight expenditure (Chs. 5, 8, 16).

**Network effect.** A situation in which the value of a service rises with the number of users: a telephone, social network, or marketplace is more useful where everyone else already is. Network effects push markets toward monopoly (Ch. 16).

**Accounting in Grain and Lives.** The author’s method for estimating the major expenditures of each era in comprehensible units (the section at the end of Chapters 1–17; summary in Chapter 18). Before the industrial era, expenditure is converted into sacks of grain (one sack ≈ 50 kilograms—about fifty days of sustenance for a worker), then into hours of labor and **working lives** (one working life is about 70,000 hours, with a range of 60,000–90,000 across different eras). From the industrial era onward, the price is divided by the median hourly wage. These are the author’s estimates expressed as ranges, not measurements (Chs. 0, 1–18).

**Status (conspicuous) consumption.** Thorstein Veblen’s term for expenditure intended to display one’s position rather than derive utility from an object. It ranges from pyramids and palaces to luxury goods (Chs. 3, 11, 17).

**Technological unemployment.** The loss of a job because a machine performs it. Historically, new occupations arose to replace those that disappeared, but with a delay; particular people and regions—weavers, miners, residents of industrial towns—paid for the transition (Chs. 10, 16, 19).

**Tragedy of the commons.** Garrett Hardin’s phrase (1968) for a situation in which a common resource—a pasture, fish stocks, the atmosphere—is depleted because each person benefits from taking more. Elinor Ostrom showed that communities often know how to avoid this through rules of their own (Chs. 1, 7, 17, 19).

**Energy per capita.** The amount of energy consumed by the average person. According to Vaclav Smil, it is one of the best measures of a society’s material capabilities. Its sharp rise after the transition to coal, oil, and gas is the principal physical distinction between the last two centuries and all previous history (Chs. 0, 10; App. C).

Each is based not on measurements, but on reconstructions, and the farther back one goes, the rougher the estimate: for periods before 1800, an error by a factor of one and a half or two is commonplace. How to read it. Millennia of agriculture did not lengthen life, and by many estimates the cities of antiquity even shortened it compared with life among foragers (Chapters 2–3). **The Four Curves Together.** All four charts bend at roughly the same point—between 1800 and 1950—but neither simultaneously nor identically. Energy and income in Britain began rising early in the nineteenth century, while global life expectancy did not shift markedly until the twentieth, when the surplus began to be invested in sanitation, medicine, and schools. This time lag suggests the book’s thesis: additional energy creates a surplus, but whether it becomes a longer life for the majority depends on what it is invested in.

## D. Sources and Recommended Reading

A complete list of sources—linked to chapters, dates, and disputed estimates—has been compiled in a separate “Sources” file. What follows is a short guide for readers who wish to go further: two or three dozen books on which the narrative rests, with a note on what makes each useful and what is disputed about it. The order runs from the general to the particular, in roughly the same sequence as the chapters.

**The Big Picture**

- Yuval Noah Harari, *Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind* (2011). The easiest entry point: how shared fictions—gods, money, states—allowed humans to cooperate on a large scale. Historians criticize the author for bold generalizations; the book is best read as an invitation, not a reference work.
- Jared Diamond, *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997). Why agriculture, and then power, emerged earlier in some places than others. Its strength is geography and biology; its weakness, according to critics, is that it understates human choice and institutions (Chs. 2, 6).
- David Graeber and David Wengrow, *The Dawn of Everything* (2021). A challenge to the “ladder of progress”: early societies experimented with very different ways of living together, and inequality was not inevitable. Many archaeologists dispute particular conclusions, but the book is effective at unsettling familiar schemes (Chs. 1–3).
- Robert Allen, *Global Economic History* (2011). A short, lucid book on why some countries became rich and others did not; it explains Britain’s surge through expensive labor and cheap coal (Ch. 10).

**Energy, Technology, Knowledge**

- Vaclav Smil, *Energy and Civilization* (2017). The principal foundation of this book: human history as a history of energy flows, from muscle to oil. Many figures, little ideology.
- Joel Mokyr, *The Lever of Riches* (1990) and *A Culture of Growth* (2016). How knowledge became a common undertaking, and why the “Republic of Letters” in early modern Europe accelerated invention (Chs. 9–10).
- Robert Gordon, *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016). Why electricity, running water, and the internal combustion engine changed life more than digital technology; a disputed but well-argued view (Chs. 11, 14, 16).

**Institutions and Power**

- Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson, *Why Nations Fail* (2012). The distinction between “inclusive” and “extractive” institutions is the closest relative of this book’s thesis. Critics regard the framework as too simple to explain China and other rapidly catching-up countries (Chs. 9, 17).
- Douglass North, *Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance* (1990). The classic work on the “rules of the game” and why bad rules endure.
- Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (1990). How communities preserve pastures, forests, and water for centuries without the state or private ownership. Winner of the 2009 Nobel Prize; important for Chapters 7 and 19.
- James C. Scott, *Against the Grain* (2017). Early states as machines for extracting grain and people; a useful companion to Graeber and Wengrow (Chs. 2–3).

**Money, Debt, the Market**

- David Graeber, *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011). Money grew not out of barter, but out of debt and power. Economists dispute many details, but most anthropologists now share the rejection of the “myth of barter”—following Caroline Humphrey (1985) (Chs. 1, 3–4).
- Karl Polanyi, *The Great Transformation* (1944). How the market tried to “disembed” itself from society and how society responded; key to Chapters 11–12.
- János Kornai, *Economics of Shortage* (1980). An inside account of why a planned economy produces queues: the concept of the “soft budget constraint” (Ch. 13).

**Divergence, Growth, and Inequality**

- Kenneth Pomeranz, *The Great Divergence* (2000). Why the Yangtze Delta and England were closer in 1750 than is commonly believed, and what coal and colonies gave Europe (Chs. 6, 10).
- Gregory Clark, *A Farewell to Alms* (2007). The Malthusian trap in figures; its conclusions about the “inheritance” of bourgeois habits have drawn sharp criticism (Ch. 9).
- Thomas Piketty, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013). The author’s thesis: over the long span of history, income from wealth usually grows faster than the economy, and the period from 1914 through the 1970s was an exception. Critics (Matthew Rognlie and others) show that the rising share of capital in recent decades is explained to a large extent by more expensive housing (Chs. 12, 14–15, 17).
- Thorstein Veblen, *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899). The origin of “conspicuous consumption”—one of the principal forms of deadweight expenditure in this book.
- David Graeber, *Bullshit Jobs* (2018). On jobs whose uselessness is acknowledged by the workers themselves; estimates of the phenomenon’s scale are disputed, with worker surveys (Soffia, Wood, and Burchell, 2022) yielding a markedly smaller share (Chs. 16–17, 19).

**Primary Sources**

Readers who want to hear the economists’ own voices rather than a retelling should open at least one text by each: Adam Smith, *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* (1776)—the opening chapters on the division of labor; Thomas Malthus, *An Essay on the Principle of Population* (1798); Karl Marx, *Capital*, Volume 1 (1867)—the chapters on the working day and machinery; John Maynard Keynes, “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren” (1930)—the short essay on the fifteen-hour working week with which this book begins; Friedrich Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945)—on why no center can know everything.

**Data**

The figures in the book and in Appendix C come primarily from open databases that readers can check for themselves: the Maddison Project Database (historical income per capita); Our World in Data (energy, health, education—with charts and links to primary sources); the UN’s *World Population Prospects* (population and life expectancy); the HYDE database (population in past millennia). All are estimates, and for the distant past they are very rough. This is the book’s final and perhaps most important warning: figures help us see where the surplus went, but they do not replace the question that every reader has the right to ask of their own era.
