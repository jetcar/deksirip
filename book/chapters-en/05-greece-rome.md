# Chapter 5. Greece and Rome

## Portrait of the Era

South of Rome, by the Tiber and beyond the Aventine Hill, stands a hill that nature did not make. It is not high—about thirty-five meters—and is overgrown with grass and trees, while the taverns and nightclubs of the Testaccio district are cut into its base. But dig into it, and beneath the turf you find the same thing everywhere: shards. The entire hill—Monte Testaccio, the “mountain of shards”—is made of fragments of large clay vessels, amphorae in which olive oil was brought to Rome. Spanish archaeologists led by José Remesal Rodríguez, who have spent decades excavating the hill, estimate that it contains the remains of roughly fifty-three million vessels.

The dump was laid out with Roman precision. Oil amphorae could not be reused—the oil soaked into the clay and turned rancid—so they were smashed and stacked in even terraces, with lime scattered between them to suppress the smell. Most of the amphorae came from Baetica, present-day Andalusia, from the banks of the Guadalquivir—more than two thousand kilometers away by sea. Their handles bear the stamps of workshops, while painted inscriptions survive on their sides: the weight of the empty vessel, the weight of the oil, the merchant’s name, inspectors’ marks, and sometimes the year identified by the serving consuls. These are thousands of pages of commercial records written directly on the packaging. The hill grew from roughly the age of Augustus until the middle of the third century CE. Then its growth stopped.

Whose oil was it? Some belonged to merchants who sold it in the markets of the enormous city. But a substantial share was purchased and distributed by the state service that supplied the capital—the *annona*. Rome, whose population historians estimate at between half a million and a million by the beginning of the Common Era (about a million is the figure most often cited), could not feed itself from the surrounding fields. Its grain came from Sicily, North Africa, and Egypt; its oil from Spain and Africa; its wine from Italy and Gaul. Some of that grain, and later some of the oil as well, was issued free of charge to several hundred thousand Roman citizens. The hill by the Tiber is both a monument to the largest supply system in the ancient world and a monument to the way the surplus of the provinces was turned into food for the crowds of the capital.

Now let us move six or seven centuries back from Rome and roughly a thousand kilometers east. Athens, in the middle of the fifth century BCE. Fish, oil, pottery, and slaves are sold in the agora—a square that was at once a market, a court, and a place of assembly. The council sits nearby, and any citizen may attend the popular assembly on the Pnyx and vote on a war, a tax, or the construction of a fleet. Five kilometers to the south, grain from the shores of the Black Sea is unloaded in the harbor of Piraeus. Attica itself fed only part of its population—how large a part remains disputed: Peter Garnsey believed it larger than is generally assumed, while Alfonso Moreno emphasizes the dependence on imports—but without Pontic grain the city would have starved in years of poor harvests. Farther south still, at Laurion, thousands of slaves mine silver ore in narrow galleries. From it are struck the “owls,” the very coins with which the previous chapter ended.

Between these two scenes—the Athenian agora and Rome’s mountain of shards—lies the path traveled by the ancient economy. It begins around 800 BCE, when Greece emerged from the “Dark Ages” that followed the collapse of the Bronze Age, and ends in the fifth century CE, when the Western Roman Empire broke apart into the kingdoms of Germanic chieftains. This span covers some thirteen centuries. During them, the Mediterranean became a single economic space for the first time: one set of laws, one currency, common measures, safe seas, and paved roads stretching from Scotland to the Euphrates. Historians’ estimates of the Roman Empire’s population at its peak in the second century CE vary, but most often range from sixty to seventy-five million people—roughly a quarter of all humanity at the time.

Antiquity left us more evidence than any earlier age: not only archives and coins, but books in which people reasoned about economic life for the first time. The word *economy* is Greek: *oikonomia* means “management of the household.” Xenophon devoted an entire treatise to it; Aristotle distinguished the acquisition of necessities from the pursuit of profit for its own sake; and the Roman writers Cato, Varro, and Columella left manuals for estate owners that calculated how many slaves a vineyard required and what they should be fed. Yet these texts were written by landowners for landowners. We still learn about the plowman, the oarsman, the miner, and the weaver from bones, shards, and gravestones.

That is precisely why antiquity is so difficult to assess. On the one hand, it was the age in which institutions without which the modern world is unthinkable first arose: citizenship and voting; contracts and courts; private property protected by law rather than by a ruler’s favor; Roman law, which to this day underlies the civil codes of many countries. It was an age of roads, aqueducts, and ports that remained in service for centuries. According to some historians, including Willem Jongman and Peter Temin, living standards in the Roman Empire at its peak were higher than anywhere in Europe until the late Middle Ages; others, such as Walter Scheidel, consider them close to medieval levels. On the other hand, this was a society founded on slavery on a scale never seen before; a society in which the brilliant engineer Hero of Alexandria built a rotating steam sphere—and it remained a toy; in which hundreds of thousands of tons of grain were brought to the capital each year, a substantial share distributed free, while fortunes were spent on spectacles; in which emperors repeatedly reduced the silver content of the coinage until, by the end of the third century, it had become silver-plated copper.

And at the end of this road came regression. After the collapse of the Western Empire, Europe’s economy fell below the ancient level for several centuries: cities emptied, coinage all but vanished from circulation, and houses were once again built from wood and clay rather than fired brick and stone. The Testaccio hill stopped growing because there was no longer anything to bring to Rome and no one there to consume it.

This brings us to the question of this chapter. Greece and Rome gathered a surplus of unprecedented size—from fields, mines, trade, and conquered provinces. Some of it went into things that expanded the capabilities of many: roads, ports, law, and coinage that people could trust. Some went into wars, the plunder of provinces, and the enslavement of millions. Some went into marble, games, and distributions that fed and entertained people but did not make a single hour of labor more productive. Where did the surplus of antiquity go? And why did a civilization capable of so much fail to take the step toward the machine, and in the end prove unable to preserve even what it already had? We shall begin, as always, with energy—with muscle, water, wind, and the human being whom the law treated as a thing.

## Energy and Technology

In 1900, sponge divers sheltering from a storm off the tiny island of Antikythera, between the Peloponnese and Crete, found the remains of an ancient ship on the seabed: bronze statues, amphorae, and glass. The recovery of the finds continued until 1901. Among them was a shapeless lump of green bronze and wood that initially attracted almost no attention. Only decades later, when it was examined first with X-rays and then with tomography, did it become clear that dozens of gearwheels lay inside. The Antikythera mechanism, made—depending on the dating—in the second or first century BCE, proved to be a calculating machine: the turn of a handle displayed the positions of the Sun and Moon and the phases of the Moon, predicted eclipses, and apparently marked the dates of the Olympic Games. Nothing comparable in the sophistication of its gearing is known for more than another thousand years, until the astronomical clocks of the late Middle Ages.

This discovery is a useful key to ancient technology. The Greeks and Romans knew more than they put into practice. They could cut gears and understood the lever, screw, pulley, and wedge. Archimedes described the laws of the lever and of buoyancy; Ctesibius of Alexandria built a piston pump and a water organ; Hero of Alexandria built a sphere rotated by steam escaping from two bent tubes, as well as a machine that dispensed a portion of holy water when a coin was inserted. Yet if we ask what powered the ancient economy, the answer is almost the same as in Sumer: muscle. Humans and oxen, mules and donkeys supplied the overwhelming share of mechanical work; firewood and charcoal supplied almost all heat. Hero’s steam sphere remained a curiosity for temples and banquets. Why it did so is one of the central questions of this chapter, and we shall return to it when we discuss slavery. For now, let us look at what antiquity did manage to accomplish with its energy.

We shall begin with the land, because the absolute majority of people worked on it. Mediterranean farming rested on a “triad”: grain, olives, and grapes. Wheat and barley were sown on the plains; olive trees and vines were planted on slopes where a plow could not go. Farmers used a light wooden scratch plow that scored the soil rather than turning it over—sufficient for the dry, thin soils of the Mediterranean. Yields were modest. Columella, the Roman agronomist of the first century CE, complained that in most of Italy grain rarely yielded even fourfold, meaning four grains for every one sown. This was the complaint of a moralist rebuking negligent landowners: Varro and Cicero cite yields of eightfold and more for the best land, while modern historians such as Michael Spurr give a range of fourfold to tenfold for Italy. Yet even in the best case, a substantial share of the harvest immediately went back into the ground as seed. Olives and grapes, meanwhile, turned stony slopes where grain would not grow into sources of oil and wine—goods that kept well and could be transported profitably. Oil was more than food: it lit homes and was rubbed on the body in baths instead of soap. Hence the fifty million amphorae on Monte Testaccio.

Here antiquity accomplished something earlier societies had achieved only in part: it made regional specialization systematic. Egypt and Africa became granaries; Baetica an oil mill; Campania and Gaul wine producers. Each region made what it was relatively best at and bought or received by sea whatever it lacked. Eighteen centuries later, David Ricardo would show that such specialization was beneficial even when one party was better than the other at everything, and would call it comparative advantage. By our measure, this was a developmental shift: the same peasant labor yielded more because it was applied to suitable land.

But specialization is possible only when transport is cheap. And antiquity’s great energy success was not steam, but wind. A sailing ship carried freight many times more cheaply than an ox cart. Economic historians who have recalculated prices from Diocletian’s Edict of 301—above all Richard Duncan-Jones, and later Walter Scheidel in the ORBIS model—arrive at ratios on the order of several dozen to one: shipping grain by sea across the entire Mediterranean cost no more than hauling it a hundred or two hundred kilometers by road. That is why the ancient world formed around the sea like frogs around a pond—a comparison Plato put into the mouth of Socrates. The scale of maritime trade can be seen indirectly. The British archaeologist Anthony Parker compiled a catalogue of known ancient shipwrecks, and the number of dated wrecks peaks between the second century BCE and the second century CE, then falls sharply. More ships sank because more ships were sailing. The curve, however, is an indirect indicator rather than a direct count of trade: Andrew Wilson and others note that the decline after the second century is partly explained by the transition from amphorae to wooden barrels, which leave no conspicuous traces on the seabed. Most vessels carried from several dozen to two or three hundred tons; the largest grain ships sailing from Alexandria to Italy carried considerably more.

The Greeks also knew how to economize on the overland leg. As early as the sixth century BCE, Corinth built the *diolkos*, a paved track across the isthmus along which ships and cargoes were hauled from one sea to the other, saving days of sailing around the Peloponnese. The Romans covered the empire with a network of paved roads whose total length, by widely cited estimates, exceeded eighty thousand kilometers. They were built primarily for legions and couriers, however, not merchants: heavy freight still moved along them slowly and at great cost. We shall examine how roads, ports, and aqueducts served development in the section “What Fostered Development.”

Roman construction technology was perhaps the most advanced the age knew. The Romans learned to make concrete by mixing lime with rubble and volcanic ash from the vicinity of Puteoli—*pozzolana*. This mortar hardened even underwater. It was used to build harbor breakwaters, bridge supports, and the vaults of baths. The dome of the Pantheon, erected around 125 CE, remains the largest unreinforced concrete dome in the world. Loads were lifted by cranes with elaborate pulley systems; the most powerful were driven by a large wheel inside which people walked—a crane of this kind is depicted on a relief from the tomb of the Haterii family in Rome. Note that even at the summit of engineering art, the motor was a human being.

There were also technologies that directly reduced the cost of everyday goods. Around the first century BCE, glassblowing was invented on the coast of Syria and Palestine, and glass, once a luxury, became ordinary tableware: drinking vessels, flasks, and jugs are found even in modest provincial homes. Pottery workshops producing red-gloss ware—*terra sigillata*—in thousands of identical pieces bearing the maker’s stamp became widespread. The British archaeologist Bryan Ward-Perkins emphasizes in *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* that in Roman times even peasants possessed sturdy mass-produced pottery and tile roofs, while after the empire’s collapse both disappeared for a long time. This is precisely the “broadening of access to goods” in our measure: glass and good tableware descended from the elite to the majority.

Yet antiquity did have a nonliving engine of its own: the waterwheel. In the first century BCE, the Roman architect Vitruvius described a watermill with gearing. For a long time it was thought that the Romans scarcely used it, since slaves were readily available. But archaeology in recent decades has shown otherwise. Near Arelate, present-day Arles in southern France, the Barbegal milling complex was built early in the second century CE: sixteen waterwheels, arranged in two rows of eight, descended the hillside in a cascade, supplied by an aqueduct. Fernand Benoit’s early estimates suggested that it could grind flour for more than ten thousand people; newer research by Philippe Leveau, Cécile Paschir, and coauthors in 2018 allows that the mill operated seasonally and may have produced ship’s biscuit, making the more modest figure of several thousand more likely. Waterwheels have been found in other parts of the empire as well—in mills, in mines where they pumped out water, and later in sawmills. The British archaeologist Andrew Wilson, who has written extensively on the subject, believes that the Roman economy used water power far more widely than was once thought. This is an honest counterargument to the convenient formula that “slaves made machines unnecessary.” But note where the wheels appeared: where the work was overwhelming or labor scarce—near large cities and in mines. The wheel did not become a universal engine around which an entire industry grew.

The greatest concentration of energy in antiquity went underground. At Laurion the Athenians mined silver; in Spain the Romans mined silver, copper, and gold. At Las Médulas in northwestern Spain, mountains were washed apart with water: galleries were cut into the slopes and then collapsed by torrents from aqueducts brought from far away. Pliny the Elder describes the method as *ruina montium*, “the wrecking of mountains.” The scarred orange landscape it left behind is now protected by UNESCO. Traces of this work have survived even in the ice of Greenland. Smelting silver from lead ores released lead into the air; winds carried it north, and in ice cores studied by Joseph McConnell’s group in 2018, the curve of lead pollution rises during Rome’s flourishing and plunges in years of war and epidemic. It is perhaps the most impartial graph of the ancient economy that we possess.

This brings us to the fate of surplus in technology. Where did antiquity’s finest engineering talent go? Into temples, palaces, baths, and amphitheaters; into the mines that supplied the mint—and into war. Around 399 BCE, Dionysius the Elder, tyrant of Syracuse, gathered craftsmen from across the Greek world and paid them to create new weapons; according to Diodorus, the first projectile-throwing machines appeared then. Later, Hellenistic kings maintained engineers who refined torsion catapults, which hurled stones with the force of twisted sinew, to carefully calculated proportions. The state willingly paid for knowledge—if that knowledge could shoot or raise a monument. Military technology here, as in the Bronze Age, presents a mixed case: mechanics and the calculation of proportions flowed from it into peaceful cranes and pumps. But no Hellenistic king offered a reward for a machine that could thresh grain faster.

The result is a picture that the historian Moses Finley once called the technological stagnation of antiquity, though later scholars have considerably softened that diagnosis. Antiquity was inventive; its engineers solved difficult problems, and material life in its best centuries was richer than in many that followed. Yet growth came chiefly from scale, specialization, and cheap maritime transport, not from a new source of energy per person. The output of a single plowman, a single oarsman, or a single miner changed little from the time of Pericles to that of Constantine. And since the principal engine remained the human being, everything depended on who that person was, whom they worked for, and what share of their labor they received. That is where we now turn.

## Organization of Labor

Between 409 and 406 BCE, the Erechtheion temple on the Acropolis was being completed in Athens, and stone tablets bearing the project’s accounts have survived: who worked, what they did, and how much they received. The masons, carpenters, and carvers listed include citizens, metics—free immigrants without civic rights—and slaves. Many slaves worked beside their owners. And almost everyone received the same amount: one drachma a day. An Athenian citizen, a foreigner, and a slave stood on the same scaffolding, dressed the same marble, and received the same wage. The money due to a slave might go in part or in full to the owner, but the fact itself is striking: on this construction site, the city paid for the work, not for a person’s status.

This scene illustrates the structure of labor in antiquity. Three worlds were mixed within it: the free peasant and artisan, the wage laborer, and the slave. The proportions among them changed from century to century and from region to region, and those proportions largely determined where the surplus went.

**The citizen-peasant.** The foundation of the Greek polis was the small landowner who worked his own plot with his hands, assisted by his family and perhaps one or two slaves. This was the man who bought a bronze helmet and shield with his own money and took his place in the hoplite phalanx—the heavily armed infantry. His right to vote rested on his plot and his shield. When Solon abolished debt slavery in 594 BCE and removed from the fields the mortgage stones that marked a debt, he was in effect protecting this peasant from becoming a farmhand or a slave. The early Roman Republic rested on the same figure: the legend of Cincinnatus, summoned from his plow to serve as dictator and returning to it after defeating the enemy, is a hymn to the smallholder-warrior.

From our perspective, this is an important detail. As long as the surplus remained with the person who plowed, it went back into his farm: into an ox, a vineyard, or an extra amphora of oil to sell. A free owner had an interest in working better because the gain belonged to him.

**The slave.** Slavery had existed before, but antiquity turned it into a system of production. In classical Athens, historians estimate that slaves probably made up between one-fifth and one-third of Attica’s population; the exact numbers are disputed, and estimates vary widely. They worked in homes, workshops, and the fields of wealthy citizens and—in the most dreadful conditions—in the silver mines of Laurion. According to Xenophon, the Athenian general Nicias leased a thousand slaves to the mines, receiving one obol per person per day. Here a human being was capital like a mule or a mill: bought, hired out, and written off.

In Rome, the scale became different. The victories of the second and first centuries BCE—over Carthage, Macedonia, the Greek cities, and later Gaul—brought hundreds of thousands of captives into Italy. By his own account, after taking one Gallic city Caesar sold its entire population into slavery—fifty-three thousand people. Demographers of antiquity dispute the figures, and their estimates diverge sharply. Peter Brunt counted about three million slaves in Italy at the end of the Republic—as much as two-fifths of the population; Walter Scheidel’s demographic model produces no more than a million and a half, or between one-sixth and one-quarter. Yet even by a cautious reckoning, two to four million captives were brought into Italy during the final two centuries BCE. Across the empire as a whole, the share was considerably lower: in Egypt, for example, papyri show slaves to have been only a small minority.

Slaves in Rome occupied very different positions. One might be a literate Greek secretary, teacher, or physician; another an estate manager, a *vilicus*, himself directing dozens of people; a third a chained plowman from an *ergastulum*, the prison-barracks attached to a villa. Cato the Elder advised selling old and sick slaves together with old oxen and worn-out iron. Varro, the Roman scholar of the first century BCE, divided agricultural equipment into “speaking” (slaves), “making inarticulate sounds” (oxen), and “mute” (carts). The classification is an exact portrait of the system.

**The villa and the latifundium.** The influx of captives and plunder transformed the Italian countryside. Wealthy families bought or occupied land, especially from the public domain—land taken from defeated Italian communities—and created large market-oriented estates producing wine, oil, and livestock. Such holdings would later be called *latifundia*, “broad estates.” Meanwhile, the small peasant spent years serving in legions far from home; his field declined and often passed to someone else for debt or a pittance. Many former landowners moved to Rome and joined the crowd that would later be fed with free grain.

The Gracchi brothers tried to reverse the process in 133 and 123–122 BCE by limiting the amount of public land one person could occupy and distributing the excess to the poor. Both were killed in street violence organized by their opponents in the Senate. The reform was partly implemented, but it did not stop the main current. Here we can clearly see how the division of labor was tied to the distribution of power: those who owned the land and the slaves decided how the countryside was organized.

The revolts reveal the price of this system. Sicilian slaves rose twice in the second half of the second century BCE, and in 73–71 BCE the gladiator Spartacus assembled an army that marched through Italy for two years, smashing legions. After the revolt was suppressed, Appian reports, six thousand prisoners were crucified along the Appian Way. A system founded on fear required constant expenditure on guards, supervision, and suppression—expenses that produced nothing.

**The free wage laborer and artisan.** Not everything depended on slaves. Cities contained multitudes of free artisans, shopkeepers, porters, and boatmen. In Rome and other cities, they formed *collegia*—associations organized by trade that held communal feasts, buried their members, and honored a patron deity. These were not yet medieval guilds with monopolies and master’s examinations, but they were already a form of self-organization among people in the same occupation. Seasonal work—the grain harvest and the gathering of grapes and olives—was often performed by wage laborers: a slave had to be fed all year, while a reaper only during the harvest. Roman agronomists explicitly advised comparing these costs.

Freedmen were an important part of urban labor. In Rome, slaves were freed far more often than in Greece, and the former slave of a Roman citizen became a citizen himself, though with certain restrictions. Many continued to conduct their former owner’s business, trading and keeping workshops and shops. The gravestones of Ostia and Pompeii are full of the names of freedmen who became prosperous. In the *Satyricon*, Petronius mocked one of them, the wealthy Trimalchio and his tasteless luxury. It is satire, but the social figure it depicts is recognizable. A skilled slave who could hope for freedom had an interest in working well; much of the urban economy rested on that hope.

**The army as employer.** In 107 BCE, the consul Gaius Marius began recruiting propertyless volunteers into the legions. The army gradually became a profession, and under Augustus service was fixed at twenty years, with pay and a cash reward or land allotment at the end. Under Augustus, an ordinary legionary received 225 denarii a year. By widely cited estimates, the imperial army numbered roughly three to four hundred thousand men including auxiliary troops, and maintaining it was the largest item of state expenditure. These were hundreds of thousands of young men removed from farming and crafts for decades. The army did build roads, bridges, and camps that grew into towns, but its principal task was guarding the frontiers and, all too often, deciding who would become emperor.

**Women’s labor.** Almost invisible in the texts, it was enormous. Spinning and weaving consumed endless hours in the home: clothing a family required spinning and weaving many meters of fabric each year. The Roman ideal of the virtuous matron was reduced to a formula often repeated on gravestones: she spun wool and kept the house. In the cities, meanwhile, women traded, kept taverns, and worked as midwives, wet nurses, and laundresses; inscriptions have preserved their names and occupations.

**The late empire: binding people to place.** By the end of the era, the picture changed again. The flow of captives dried up, and slaves became more expensive. Large landowners increasingly leased the land in small plots to *coloni*, tenant farmers. And a state that needed reliable tax collection benefited when everyone remained in place. In 332, Emperor Constantine forbade *coloni* to leave the land to which they were registered; a fugitive tenant was to be returned in chains. Many urban occupations became hereditary as well: bakers and shipowners who supplied the capital, and members of city councils. The free peasant of antiquity was slowly turning into a person bound to the land—the ancestor of the medieval serf.

Let us sum up. Ancient labor developed where people worked for themselves or for pay and could expect to benefit from the result: the Greek landowner, the freedman in his own shop, the hired reaper, the mason on an Athenian construction site. It stagnated where the worker was a thing with no reason to make an effort, and the owner could buy another thing instead of seeking a different way to work. A substantial share of the surplus went to those who owned land and people and was spent on more land, more slaves, supervision, and guards. How that surplus was distributed through markets, coinage, and taxes is our next question.

## Exchange and Money

In 375/374 BCE, the Athenian assembly passed a law on the inspection of silver coinage. Its text was carved on a marble stele, which was found during excavations of the agora in 1970. The law is thoroughly practical. A public coin tester—a slave owned by the city—sat in the agora and in Piraeus. Anyone could bring silver to him. If a coin was Athenian and sound, the tester returned it to its owner and the seller was required to accept it; anyone who refused risked forfeiting the goods. If the coin was counterfeit—copper or lead beneath a silver coating—it was cut and confiscated. If it was a good imitation coin minted elsewhere, bearing the same “owl,” it too was returned to its owner. In other words, the city assumed the task that every merchant in Kanesh and Nippur had performed for himself, with scales and a cut: deciding whether a piece of metal could be trusted.

This is a small but vivid example of what happened to exchange in antiquity. In the previous chapter we saw how the Lydians invented coinage—metal bearing a stamp that promised its weight and purity. The Greeks and Romans made coinage part of daily life. By the fifth century BCE, hundreds of Greek cities were minting their own money, while the Athenian “owl” circulated far beyond Attica: it is found in hoards from Egypt to Mesopotamia and was even imitated in the East. The silver of Laurion, pure and stable in weight, became what today would be called the region’s reserve currency. Trust in coinage was a public good—and the Athenians protected it by law.

**The market.** Where there is coinage, there is a market, and in ancient cities it was not an abstract concept but a place. Trade inspectors worked in the agora: *agoranomoi* monitored the quality of goods, while *metronomoi* checked measures and weights; in Rome, the aediles played a similar role. Stone standards for measures of volume have survived—slabs with hollows against which merchants checked their vessels. These were coordination costs placed on the common account: instead of every buyer measuring afresh, the city guaranteed the measure once.

How market-based the ancient economy was as a whole is an old and heated debate. Following Polanyi, Moses Finley insisted that there was no integrated market in antiquity, that prices did not connect the regions, and that the behavior of the wealthy was governed by status rather than calculation. The American economist Peter Temin objects in *The Roman Market Economy* (2013): insofar as they can be reconstructed, grain prices in different parts of the empire moved together, which means that markets were connected; wage labor, credit, and leases all existed. Where does the truth lie? Probably in the middle: markets mattered greatly in cities and along sea routes, while deep in the provinces most peasants lived by subsistence farming and sold only a small part of their harvest. Yet no earlier age had seen so many people buying bread, oil, and pottery with coin every day.

**Bankers and maritime loans.** In fourth-century BCE Athens, money changers called *trapezitai* sat in the agora—the name comes from *trapeza*, a table. They exchanged the coins of different cities, accepted deposits, and issued loans. The most famous of them, Pasion, began as a slave in a money-changing office, gained his freedom, inherited his owners’ business, and was awarded Athenian citizenship for generous gifts to the city—a thousand shields and five triremes equipped at his own expense. His own former slave, Phormion, in turn took over the bank after him. We know this from the legal speeches of Demosthenes: lawsuits over Pasion’s estate continued for years.

Still more important to the economy was the maritime loan. A merchant borrowed money for a voyage—say, to obtain grain from Pontus—pledging the ship or cargo as security. If the vessel returned, he paid a high rate of interest, often twenty to thirty percent for a single voyage. If it sank or was captured by pirates, the debt vanished. In essence, it was a loan and insurance at once: the risks of the voyage were shared by merchant and lender. The texts of such contracts, preserved in courtroom speeches, specify the route, cargo, and deadlines in detail. This was no longer a royal gift or a temple disbursement, but a private instrument that allowed someone else’s surplus to be invested in trade. By our measure, it was a developmental mechanism: it reduced the risk borne by the person taking action and found a use for the money of the person who was merely accumulating it.

Rome inherited and expanded this toolkit. Around 211 BCE, at the height of the war with Hannibal, the silver denarius appeared and soon became the principal coin of the Mediterranean. Roman bankers, the *argentarii*, kept accounts, processed payments, and lent to buyers at auctions. The law alternately restricted and tolerated moneylending; during the empire, the usual ceiling was considered to be one percent a month, or twelve percent a year. The depth to which credit permeated the economy is visible in the crisis of 33 CE. Tacitus relates that when the authorities began forcing lenders to comply with old loan laws, they all demanded repayment at once. Debtors rushed to sell land, its price collapsed, and cash ran short. Emperor Tiberius then provided one hundred million sesterces for interest-free three-year loans secured by land, and the panic subsided. This is one of the earliest recorded interventions by a government resembling a rescue of the financial system, and we shall recall it in the chapters on the twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

**Tax as an engine of trade.** The most powerful flow driving coin through the empire, however, was not private exchange but taxation. The British historian Keith Hopkins proposed a model that became a classic, though a disputed one: critics, including Richard Duncan-Jones, point out that a substantial share of taxes was collected in kind rather than coin. The provinces paid taxes to Rome in money. To obtain that money, they had to sell their goods—grain, oil, wine, and textiles—where money was being spent: in Italy, in the large cities, and among the frontier armies. Taxes pulled coin out of the provinces; trade returned it. According to Hopkins, this explains the integration of markets over which Finley and Temin disagree: a substantial part of the empire’s trade was generated by extraction itself.

This is an important turn for our book. Trade, which until now we have counted among the forces of development, appears here in part as a servant of extraction. The Spanish oil whose amphorae were smashed on Monte Testaccio traveled to Rome largely because Rome taxed the provinces and distributed oil to its own citizens. Sea routes, harbors, and coinage were genuine achievements—but the surplus flowed along them predominantly in one direction.

**Who collected.** During the Republic, Rome often did not collect taxes itself but farmed out the right to do so. Companies of tax farmers—the *publicani*—paid the treasury an agreed sum and then squeezed as much as they could beyond it from the provinces. In his speeches against Verres, governor of Sicily in 73–71 BCE, Cicero describes how the governor and his men plundered the island, extorting grain from farmers far beyond the prescribed tithe. The “publicans” of the Gospels, collectors of farmed-out tolls who inspired universal hostility, are a related figure born of the same tax-farming logic. After 167 BCE, when Macedonian plunder poured into the treasury, Roman citizens in Italy ceased paying direct tax altogether: the burden fell entirely on the provinces. Augustus and his successors regularized collection—they conducted censuses, made levies more predictable, and gradually pushed tax farmers away from direct taxation. This made life appreciably easier for provincials: a predictable tax is better than arbitrary robbery. But the direction of the flow did not change.

**Distribution.** At the other end of this flow stood the Roman people. In 123 BCE, Gaius Gracchus passed a law under which the citizens of Rome could buy a monthly ration of grain at a reduced price. In 58 BCE, the tribune Clodius made the distribution free, and according to Suetonius the number of recipients reached three hundred and twenty thousand by mid-century. Caesar reduced the list to one hundred and fifty thousand; under Augustus it settled at around two hundred thousand adult male citizens. Each received five *modii* of grain a month—more than thirty kilograms, more than one person required. Later, under Septimius Severus and Aurelian, oil was added to the grain, then wine and pork, while baked bread replaced grain. What this system cost and whether it was wasted expenditure is a subject for another section. Here we need only note the mechanism: part of the empire’s surplus was distributed not through the market and not through earnings, but through the right of citizenship—that is, through political membership in the capital.

**The drain to the East.** There was one more flow. Pliny the Elder complains that India, China, and Arabia took one hundred million sesterces from the empire every year in exchange for silk, pearls, pepper, and incense. The figure is probably rhetorical, but hoards of Roman coins in southern India and the so-called Muziris papyrus—a contract for shipping a valuable cargo from an Indian port through the Red Sea to Alexandria—confirm that gold and silver really did flow east. In return, the empire received pepper, without which Roman cuisine could not do, and luxuries for those who could afford them.

Antiquity, then, made exchange cheaper than ever before: trusted coinage, common measures, credit, banks, and safe seas. These were developmental inventions, and we shall encounter their descendants in every chapter that follows. Yet when we trace where the money ultimately went, the picture divides in two. A substantial share of coin moved not because someone created and sold something, but because someone extracted and distributed something: taxes from the provinces, pay for the army, bread for the capital. Who decided where this flow would go, and what constrained that choice, is a question of power and institutions.

## Power and Institutions

In fourth-century BCE Athens, a strange machine stood in the agora—the *kleroterion*. It was a stone slab with rows of narrow slots into which citizens arriving at court in the morning inserted bronze tokens bearing their names. Along one side ran a tube filled with white and black balls. A white ball emerged, and the corresponding row of tokens was assigned to judge; a black one, and those people went home. Fragments of these slabs and the tokens themselves have been found in excavations. The device existed for one purpose: so that no one could know in advance which citizens would decide a case and bribe them. The Athenians trusted neither the wealthy nor the wise, nor even themselves—and therefore entrusted the choice to lot.

This is a good place to begin a discussion of power in antiquity, because for the first time in our book the question “who decides?” became an object of conscious design. In Sumer and Egypt, there was one answer: the god, his temple, and his king. The Greeks and Romans began inventing rules that constrained the rulers themselves. Not always successfully, and far from for everyone—but it was these experiments that left antiquity’s most enduring legacy.

**The polis.** The Greek polis was a city-state, usually small: several thousand citizens, a city, and the surrounding fields. Its chief novelty was that power belonged not to a ruler but to the citizen body. After the reforms of Cleisthenes in 508/507 BCE, the highest authority in Athens was the popular assembly: any adult male citizen could attend, speak, and vote. The council of five hundred that prepared business for the assembly was selected by lot, and most offices were likewise filled by lot for one year. Only posts demanding clear competence remained elective—above all the ten *strategoi*, the military commanders. Pericles served as *strategos* for many consecutive years, but he was elected anew each year.

The democracy was narrow. Only free men of Athenian descent were citizens—by varying estimates, merely a minority of all Attica’s inhabitants. Women, metics, and slaves had no vote. But something else matters to the economy: decisions about taxes, wars, and expenditure were for the first time made in public by people who would themselves pay and fight. At the end of their term, officials rendered accounts to auditors, and many reports were carved in stone. That is precisely why the Erechtheion construction records with which the labor section began exist. Transparency in spending was not a virtue but a rule.

**How the wealthy paid the city.** Athens imposed no regular direct tax on its citizens. Instead, there were *liturgies*—“public services.” The wealthiest citizens took turns bearing major expenses: a *trierarch* equipped and maintained a trireme warship for a year; a *choregos* paid for the staging of tragedies and choruses at the festival of Dionysus. Refusal was difficult, and if someone believed another man was richer and evading his obligation, he could offer him a choice: assume the liturgy or exchange property with him. It was a curious mechanism: the surplus of the wealthy went to the common cause, while honor was the return. Where exactly it went—to the fleet and the theater, to power and celebration—is a question for later sections. But the very idea that great wealth imposes obligations rather than merely conferring power was an important invention.

The Athenians took another step: they began paying people to participate in government. Under Pericles, jurors received a small daily payment for sitting in court; in the fourth century, people were paid for attending the assembly as well. Critics from Aristophanes to Aristotle mocked this as feeding the mob. Supporters replied that otherwise only the wealthy, who possessed free time, could judge and vote. This is the first debate known to us over whether civic participation should be paid so that it does not become the privilege of those who need not work.

**The dark side: the allied treasury.** Democracy could extract too. After the Persian Wars, in 478 BCE, Greek cities formed an alliance to defend the Aegean Sea, with a shared treasury on the island of Delos. Contributions were paid in money or ships. Gradually Athens turned the alliance into an empire of its own: around 454 BCE the treasury was moved to Athens, attempts to leave the alliance were suppressed by force, and contributions became tribute. According to Plutarch, part of this money paid for the Parthenon and the other temples of the Acropolis, and even Pericles’ contemporaries accused him of adorning the city at others’ expense. Democracy at home and empire for one’s neighbors—a combination we shall encounter more than once.

**The Roman Republic: rules against the individual.** Rome organized power differently. After the expulsion of the kings, traditionally dated to 509 BCE, power was divided among elected magistrates, the Senate, and popular assemblies. There were two consuls, the highest magistrates, and each could stop the other; their term was one year. The tribunes of the plebs could prohibit—their “veto,” meaning “I forbid”—the actions of other magistrates. The Greek historian Polybius, who observed Rome in the second century BCE, admired this mixed constitution in which no one part could prevail. Two thousand years later, the authors of the American Constitution read Polybius closely.

The Republic was nevertheless an oligarchy. Votes in the principal assembly were apportioned by property class, so the wealthy voted first and often decided the matter before the poor had their turn. The Senate consisted of former magistrates—that is, members of a few hundred aristocratic families. But the rules bound even this nobility: no one could hold the highest office indefinitely, and after his term a former magistrate could be prosecuted.

**Law.** Rome’s most enduring creation was its law. Around 450 BCE, under pressure from the common people, the plebeians, customary law was written down and displayed in the Forum on twelve tablets: the nobility could no longer interpret it however it pleased. Thereafter Roman law grew less through legislation than through practice. The praetor—the magistrate responsible for the courts—announced in an edict which actions he would accept; successful formulas passed from edict to edict. Around the courts arose a class of jurists who gave advice and wrote commentaries. The second-century CE textbook of Gaius, the works of Papinian and Ulpian in the early third century, and three centuries later the Justinian compilation, the *Digest* of 533, carried this legacy into the Middle Ages and down to the civil codes of our own day.

What did law give the economy? A clear concept of ownership: a thing belonged to its owner, who could sell, pledge, or bequeath it, while a court would protect him even against a more powerful neighbor. Standard contracts—sale, hire, partnership, agency—arose through the simple consent of the parties, without rites or formulas. There was also the *peculium*, special property that an owner assigned to a slave for conducting business: as a rule, the owner’s liability for claims arising from the slave’s transactions was limited to that property. Some researchers—including Barbara Abatino, Giuseppe Dari-Mattiacci, and Enrico Perotti (2011)—see in this a distant prototype of limited liability. All of this reduced the costs of trust between strangers—the fifth criterion in our measure. A merchant from Alexandria could do business with one from Gaul knowing that their dispute would be decided under intelligible rules.

But law protected owners, not everyone. A slave was a thing in the full legal sense. And law was powerless against a man who stood above it with an army.

**When the rules break.** The Roman Republic died from the same thing that made it rich: conquest. Generals who returned from campaigns with plunder and slaves needed soldiers, and after the reforms of Marius those soldiers expected their reward from their commander, not the Senate. In the first century BCE, Sulla, then Caesar, then Antony and Octavian led armies against their own capital. In 82 and 43 BCE, the victors drew up proscription lists—registers of “enemies of the state” whom anyone could kill with impunity, while their property went to the treasury and informers. Cicero was among the victims in 43. Proscription is a pure case of arbitrary extraction: property is protected by law exactly until its owner’s name enters the list.

After 27 BCE, Octavian, now Augustus, preserved republican forms—the Senate, consuls, elections—while keeping the army, key provinces, and money in his own hands. Historians call this system the Principate, from *princeps*, “first citizen.” For the provinces, it proved more a relief than otherwise: the great civil wars ceased for nearly two centuries, governors were controlled more strictly, and taxes were collected on the basis of a census. This was the “Roman peace,” the *Pax Romana*.

**An empire without a bureaucracy.** It is astonishing how few officials governed this enormous empire. In *The Roman Empire: Economy, Society and Culture*, historians Peter Garnsey and Richard Saller write that in the second century the senior administrators drawn from senators and equestrians numbered only in the hundreds—for more than sixty million subjects. The cities did the rest. Every city had a council of local magnates, the *decurions*, who collected taxes themselves, answered for shortfalls with their own property, built baths and paved streets, often at their own expense, in exchange for an honorific inscription. This was the same logic as the liturgies, extended across the empire: the local elite paid and governed and in return received honor, Roman citizenship, and a path upward.

Citizenship gradually expanded. After the Social War of 91–88 BCE, the free inhabitants of Italy received it; it then spread through the provinces via the army, public offices, and city councils. In 212, Emperor Caracalla granted citizenship to almost every free inhabitant of the empire. The historian Cassius Dio suspected that the chief purpose was to expand the pool of people liable for the inheritance tax, which was imposed only on citizens. Both were probably true: inclusion and extraction advanced side by side.

**The weak point.** The Principate had an ineradicable defect: there was no firm rule for the transfer of power. Formally, the emperor received his authority from the Senate and the people; in practice, he received it from the army. In 69 CE, after Nero’s suicide, four emperors succeeded one another in a single year, each brought to power by the legions. In the third century this would become the norm, and what followed will be discussed in the section on destruction. Here let us note the essential point. Power that can be seized by force is expensive: it must be purchased. New emperors gave soldiers cash gifts and raised their pay—and to find the silver, they increasingly debased the coinage.

Let us sum up. Antiquity invented three things that we shall encounter throughout the rest of this book: public decision-making about the use of common funds; law that protects property and contracts from arbitrary power; and the self-governing city, where the elite pays for common purposes in exchange for honor. All three constrained extraction and directed at least part of the surplus toward public benefit. But all three worked only for citizens—and all three retreated when the army entered the picture. The answer to “where did the surplus go?” in antiquity almost always came down to who held the sword: the citizen-soldier, the paid legion, or the soldier who chose his own emperor.

## What Fostered Development

In 97 CE, Senator Sextus Julius Frontinus received from Emperor Nerva a post that today might be called director of the municipal water utility: he became curator of Rome’s aqueducts. Frontinus was a man of action—he had previously commanded an army in Britain—and he approached the job as one approaches a neglected estate. He inspected the conduits, compared the records with what he saw with his own eyes, and wrote a book about it, *On the Aqueducts of the City of Rome*. It has come down to us complete. It lists every aqueduct serving the capital, its length, sources, and volume of water, along with a multitude of abuses: secret taps through which owners of gardens and baths stole public water, bribed inspectors, and pipes wider than authorized. Frontinus proudly contrasts these structures with the “idle” pyramids and the celebrated but useless works of the Greeks. The comparison is unfair, but profoundly Roman: a structure’s value lies in how many people it serves every day.

This book offers a convenient starting point for discussing what broadened the capabilities of many in antiquity. There were several mechanisms, and not one of them was a steam engine.

**Water and the city.** Rome’s first aqueduct, the Aqua Appia, was built in 312 BCE; by the third century CE there were eleven. Water flowed by gravity along a gradient, sometimes for dozens of kilometers: across valleys on arches that still stand outside Rome, and through hills in tunnels. The Pont du Gard in southern France, an aqueduct bridge nearly fifty meters high, formed part of the conduit serving Nemausus, present-day Nîmes. Across its entire fifty-kilometer course, the water level fell by only about twelve meters. Simple leveling instruments and the surveyors’ patience achieved such precision.

The water went to public fountains where it could be collected free of charge, to baths, to the workshops of fullers and dyers, while the surplus flushed the sewers. Rome’s great drain, the Cloaca Maxima, served for centuries. An aqueduct is a classic example of what economists call a public good: it is unprofitable for one person to build, but it benefits everyone at once, and the more people use it, the less it costs each of them. Ancient cities and the empire excelled at creating precisely such goods, better than anyone before them.

In fairness, the health benefits were smaller than they might seem. Baths whose water was rarely changed could spread infection, lead pipes could slowly poison, and the crowding of large cities made them places of high mortality. Studies of skeletons synthesized by economic historians Nikola Koepke and Joerg Baten do not show a marked rise in average human height—a useful indicator of childhood nutrition and health—during the Roman era; in their data, it may even have declined slightly. Water improved urban life, but it did not defeat the diseases of crowding. This is an honest qualification to our third criterion—the quality of life of the majority.

**Ports and lighthouses.** If wind was the principal energy source of ancient trade, the harbor was its bottleneck. The Romans invested enormous resources in it. Emperor Claudius began construction of an artificial harbor, Portus, at the mouth of the Tiber in 42 CE because large grain ships could not dock at old Ostia. Trajan later added a hexagonal inner basin surrounded by warehouses. Earlier still, at the beginning of the third century BCE, the Pharos of Alexandria, one of the Seven Wonders of the World, rose—by estimates—to more than a hundred meters; its light guided vessels into the principal grain port of the Mediterranean. Roman concrete that hardened underwater made it possible to build breakwaters where nature had provided no harbor, as at Caesarea Maritima, the port King Herod constructed on the open sandy shore of Judaea.

Every such harbor reduced cargo losses and waiting time, and therefore lowered the cost of every amphora of oil and every sack of grain. This directly advances the fifth criterion in our measure—the costs of exchange. A port reduces them for everyone who uses it and for centuries to come.

**Safe seas.** Neither a port nor a ship is worth anything if pirates rule the sea. In the second and first centuries BCE, Cilician pirates from the southern coast of Asia Minor robbed merchants, captured people to sell into slavery, and eventually threatened the grain supply of Rome itself. In 67 BCE, the popular assembly, under the Gabinian Law, granted Pompey extraordinary authority throughout the Mediterranean. He divided the sea into districts, assigned a squadron to each, and according to ancient authors cleared it in roughly three months. He did not execute many of the pirates who surrendered, but settled them on land in various cities—turning raiders into farmers.

This is an unobvious but perhaps the most important developmental mechanism of antiquity: peace. The *Pax Romana*—two centuries without major wars inside the empire—made trade predictable. A merchant in Baetica could expect his oil to reach Rome and the proceeds to return. Parker’s shipwreck curve and the lead curve from Greenland ice discussed above rise during precisely these centuries. Peace is not an invention but an absence of destruction; yet in an economy where surplus is easily seized, it may be the most important condition of all.

**Standards.** Less conspicuous but no less important was unification. One coin, the denarius, from Britain to Syria. Common measures. Latin in the West and Greek in the East as shared languages of business. One law for citizens and, from 212 onward, for almost every free person. Millions of people who spoke dozens of languages received a common set of rules by which they could make agreements with strangers. This is the same logic as that of the Athenian coin tester: an expensive verification is performed once by a common institution, not again and again by every party to a transaction.

**The spread of skills.** A unified space carried knowledge. Following the Greeks of Massalia, who had brought the vine to southern Gaul around 600 BCE, the Romans spread viticulture and olive cultivation farther into the provinces: vines took root along the Rhône and, by the second to fourth centuries, along the Moselle, where vineyards still stand today. Britain received concrete, roof tile, the potter’s wheel for mass-produced ware, paved roads, and waterwheels. Roman agronomists described rotations of cereals with legumes, manuring, and the grafting of fruit trees, and these practices spread across the estates of the empire. Here we see the mechanism that the framework of this book calls the accumulation and transmission of knowledge: a skill once discovered in one province became available in another.

**Knowledge as a value.** Finally, there was the culture of knowledge itself. In Alexandria, the kings of the Ptolemaic dynasty founded the Mouseion—the “shrine of the Muses,” where scholars lived on royal stipends—and with it the Library, which collected books from the entire known world. Around 240 BCE, its librarian Eratosthenes compared the angle of the noonday sun in Alexandria with that in Syene far to the south and calculated the circumference of the Earth—with an error ranging, in different modern conversions, from a few percent to roughly fifteen percent, depending on the length of the stadion he used. Euclid organized geometry into a system of proofs, and his *Elements* was used for teaching for two thousand years. In the second century CE, Ptolemy compiled works of astronomy and geography that remained authoritative until Copernicus. Galen brought together the medical knowledge used for treatment until the modern era.

What matters is not only the sum of the discoveries but the habit that the Greeks passed on: a claim must be proved, not merely asserted. From our perspective, this was an investment of surplus with the longest return. It gave the ancient economy almost nothing directly—Eratosthenes did not make a single harvest richer—but through Arabic translators and medieval universities it would become one of the foundations of the scientific revolution discussed in Chapter 9.

**How much did all this yield?** Can the overall result be measured? The American historian Ian Morris attempted to do so for Greece. By his estimates, the median area of excavated houses grew from roughly fifty square meters around 800 BCE to more than three hundred by 300 BCE, while consumption per person over those five centuries rose, in his cautious estimate, by one and a half to two times. In *The Rise and Fall of Classical Greece* (2015), political scientist Josiah Ober explains this growth through a combination of three forces: competition among hundreds of independent poleis, which adopted one another’s successful solutions; civic institutions that constrained arbitrary power; and cheap exchange. Comparable estimates for Rome, to which we shall return in the section “The Numbers,” likewise indicate an increase in living standards during the first and second centuries CE. All these figures are reconstructions with wide margins of error, and they are disputed: Walter Scheidel and other critics note that simultaneous growth in population and per capita consumption would require an increase in output difficult to imagine without new technologies. But they point in the same direction: for the first time in our history, it was not only the number of people that rose, but prosperity per person as well—noticeably and for centuries.

Let us assemble the answer to the chapter’s question, for this part of it. Ancient surplus fostered development when it was transformed into things many could use: water, ports, peace at sea, common rules, and knowledge open to scrutiny. Almost all of these were public goods created by cities and states rather than individual households. And almost all shared one characteristic: they reduced costs—of transport, exchange, and trust—but did not change the central fact, the output of a single worker. The plowman, the oarsman, and the miner remained the same. Antiquity knew how to make their labor yield more through order, but not through the machine. And when order collapsed, most of the gain disappeared with it. How exactly that happened is the subject of the next section.

## What Destroyed

In the summer of 146 BCE, Roman legions almost simultaneously wiped two great trading cities of the Mediterranean from the earth. In Africa, Carthage fell after a three-year siege: the city was burned and the surviving inhabitants—according to ancient authors, some fifty thousand people—were sold into slavery. In Greece, the consul Lucius Mummius took Corinth, the very city with the portage across the isthmus: the men were killed, the women and children sold, and statues and paintings loaded onto ships and carried to Italy. Polybius, who accompanied the Romans in Africa, witnessed the burning of Carthage. According to his account, the commander Scipio Aemilianus looked upon the city in flames, wept, and said that one day the same fate might befall Rome.

Two destroyed harbors in a single year make a useful point of entry into this part of the chapter. Antiquity created public goods better than any of its predecessors. But it was equally skilled at destroying them—and, more importantly, its economy depended in large part on destruction.

**War as an industry.** Let us begin with the most obvious. The Peloponnesian War between Athens and Sparta lasted from 431 to 404 BCE—nearly thirty years. The Spartans invaded Attica again and again and ravaged its fields, groves, and vineyards; an olive tree takes years to grow, so the damage from a single campaign was felt for a long time. The Athenians sheltered behind their walls, and in 430 an epidemic broke out in the overcrowded city, described by Thucydides, who himself survived it. Historians estimate that it probably killed between one-quarter and one-third of the city’s inhabitants. In 413, the Spartans fortified Decelea within Attica itself, and according to Thucydides more than twenty thousand slaves, many of them artisans, fled to them; production at Laurion nearly ceased. The Sicilian Expedition of the same years destroyed tens of thousands of lives and roughly two hundred ships. By the end of the war, Athens had lost its fleet, treasury, and allies. The investments of many decades—in ships, workshops, groves, and people—burned within a single generation.

Rome fought for longer and on a greater scale. The Second Punic War against Hannibal (218–201 BCE) brought Italy a decade and a half of devastation. At the Battle of Cannae in 216 alone, roughly fifty thousand Romans and allies died in one day according to Livy, and around seventy thousand according to Polybius. Wars in Spain, Greece, Asia, and Gaul followed. We have already seen in the section on labor that these campaigns hollowed out the Italian countryside from within: the small landowner served for years in a legion while his field fell into neglect.

**Captives: the destruction of people as a source of income.** What chiefly distinguishes ancient warfare from the wars of the Bronze Age is the scale on which people were turned into commodities. Caesar sold the inhabitants of a Gallic city; after his victory over Macedonia in 167 BCE, according to Livy, Aemilius Paullus devastated dozens of cities in Epirus and sold one hundred and fifty thousand people into slavery. The island of Delos became the largest slave market. Strabo writes that ten thousand slaves could be received and sold there in a single day—a figure probably exaggerated, but revealing. By our framework, this is destruction in its purest form: people, their skills, families, and communities were destroyed so that their labor could pass to another owner. And we have already discussed how that labor ultimately yielded less than it would have under a free worker.

Here it is important not to confuse two accounts. For an individual Roman senator, war was a profitable enterprise: land, slaves, booty. For the Mediterranean as a whole, it was a pure loss: every slave on an Italian villa meant an abandoned field in Epirus or Gaul. Surplus was not created but moved—and a substantial part of it burned along the way.

**The plunder of provinces.** Even where there was no war, conquered lands were initially treated as booty. We have already discussed Verres and the tax farmers. The provinces’ response could be terrible. In 88 BCE, Mithridates, king of Pontus, began a war against Rome and called on the cities of Asia Minor to kill every Italian living among them on the same day. Ancient authors give a death toll of eighty thousand, and one even a hundred and fifty thousand; these figures are probably exaggerated, but the cities’ readiness to participate reveals how much hatred the tax-farming system had accumulated. Extraction breeds retaliatory destruction, which breeds new punitive campaigns. We saw this cycle as early as the Sumerian cities.

**Epidemics.** The integrated space that made exchange so much cheaper also made the path of infection cheaper. Around 165 CE, an army returning from war against Parthia brought a disease into the empire known as the Antonine Plague; from Galen’s descriptions, it may have been smallpox. It swept through the empire and returned for two more decades. Estimates of mortality vary sharply—from a few percent of the population to a quarter or more—and historians Richard Duncan-Jones, Walter Scheidel, and Kyle Harper disagree on the matter. Egyptian papyri show depopulated villages and abandoned fields; the lead curve in Greenland ice falls during these decades. In the middle of the third century came a new epidemic, the so-called Plague of Cyprian, coinciding with wars. In *The Fate of Rome* (2017), Harper argues that disease and climatic cooling were not background conditions but major causes of the empire’s decline; other historians, including John Haldon and coauthors (2018), consider these factors overstated. In any case, an interconnected world proved a fragile one—a lesson familiar from the end of the Bronze Age.

**Debasement of the coinage.** Now we come to the central story of destruction, one that in the Roman case can be traced almost year by year. The denarius of Augustus was a coin of nearly pure silver. In 64, Nero substantially reduced its weight and silver content—probably needing funds after the great fire of Rome. His successors continued little by little. Under Marcus Aurelius, during years of war and epidemic, the denarius contained still less silver. Septimius Severus, who raised army pay, reduced the silver content to roughly half. In 215, Caracalla introduced a new coin, the antoninianus, which circulated as a double denarius but, according to numismatists, contained only about one and a half denarii’s worth of silver.

The logic of every step was the same, and we promised to return to it in the section on power. An emperor needed more money than taxes supplied—for the army, gifts to soldiers, and war. Raising taxes was difficult and dangerous. Debasing the coinage was easier: the same pound of silver produced more coins, and for a time the treasury grew richer. But everyone who held money paid the price: their savings quietly lost value. Debasement was a tax for which no one voted and which at first no one noticed.

In the middle of the third century, the process broke loose. In the half-century from 235 to 284, more than twenty recognized emperors succeeded one another, not counting usurpers, and nearly all died violently. Emperor Valerian was captured by the Persians in 260. For a time the empire split into three parts: a separate Gallic Empire arose in the west and the Palmyrene kingdom in the east. Every ruler paid his troops—and every ruler minted coins from ever poorer metal. By the 260s, the antoninianus had become, in effect, a copper coin with a thin silver wash: according to numismatists, it contained only a few percent silver, or even less.

The consequences were precisely what one would expect. Prices in Egypt, where papyri allow them to be traced, rose many times over by the end of the third century. The state itself ceased to trust its coinage and increasingly collected taxes in kind—in grain, cloth, and horses—and paid soldiers in rations. Exchange, which antiquity had spent so long making cheaper, became expensive again. The Athenian stele of 375 protected trust in coinage; the Roman emperors of the third century destroyed it themselves.

In 301, Diocletian attempted to halt rising prices with the Edict on Maximum Prices. It set price ceilings for hundreds of goods and services—from wheat to sea freight and teachers’ pay—and threatened violators with death. Fragments of the text have been found across the empire; it is from them that historians compare the costs of maritime and overland transport. The Christian writer Lactantius, hostile to Diocletian, says that goods vanished from the markets, much blood was shed, and the edict eventually ceased to be observed. Lactantius is partisan, but the conclusion is corroborated: the edict failed. Forbidding prices to rise is not the same as restoring trust in money.

**Walls instead of roads.** Another sign of destruction was material. In the 270s, Emperor Aurelian surrounded Rome with a new wall about nineteen kilometers long: the capital, which had needed no fortifications for centuries, was now building them. Cities in Gaul contracted into small fortified cores whose walls often incorporated stones from dismantled temples and gravestones. Surplus that a century earlier had gone into aqueducts and forums now went into defense. In itself this was rational, but it was a sign: society was spending its strength not on growth, but on avoiding loss.

**Disintegration.** Diocletian and Constantine restored order—at the cost of a vast expansion of the army, taxes, and officialdom, and the binding of people to land and trade discussed in the section on labor. In the East, this worked: the Eastern Empire endured for another thousand years. In the West, it did not. In 410, Alaric’s Visigoths sacked Rome—the first time in eight centuries. In 439, the Vandals took Carthage, and the Western Empire lost its principal granary and tax base—the very Africa whose amphorae lie in Monte Testaccio. In 455, the Vandals came by sea and plundered Rome itself. In 476, the Germanic commander Odoacer deposed the last western emperor.

The depth of the regression is clearly visible in Rome itself: a city that had held roughly a million people fell, by the sixth century, to a population historians estimate in the tens of thousands. Ward-Perkins shows that after the Romans left Britain in the early fifth century, the potter’s wheel, coinage, brick, and roof tile nearly disappeared, and in his assessment material life fell below the level that had existed before the Roman conquest. The regression was not so deep everywhere, and many historians following Peter Brown prefer to speak not of the “fall” but of the “transformation” of the Roman world. Yet the essential economic fact is clear: the integrated space disintegrated, trade contracted, and most of the gains from specialization disappeared with it.

Let us sum up. Antiquity destroyed in three ways. Through war—burning groves, fleets, and cities and turning people into commodities. Through extraction—plundering provinces until they answered with massacre. And through the debasement of money—undermining the trust on which its own exchange depended. The third method is especially instructive: no one intended to destroy the Roman economy; each emperor merely paid his soldiers. But the sum of those decisions proved heavier than any invasion. And while one part of the surplus burned in wars and lost value in the coinage, another went into marble, games, and free bread—into what destroyed nothing, but created nothing either.

## What Was Wasted

In 80 CE, Emperor Titus opened a new amphitheater in Rome—the one we call the Colosseum. The celebration lasted one hundred days. The historian Cassius Dio writes that nine thousand wild and domestic animals were killed in the arena during that time; gladiators fought, and according to the same account, a naval battle was staged in the flooded amphitheater. Twenty-seven years later, Trajan celebrated the conquest of Dacia with games that Dio says lasted one hundred and twenty-three days: eleven thousand animals killed and ten thousand gladiators. The figures may be rounded, but the order of magnitude is clear—this was an industry.

The Colosseum has a history that almost demands inclusion in this book. It stands on the site of the lake of the Golden House, the immense palace complex Nero built for himself in the middle of Rome after the fire of 64, appropriating the burned-out districts. Suetonius relates that when Nero moved into the palace, he remarked that at last he could begin to live like a human being. The Flavians who succeeded him demolished part of the palace and gave the people an amphitheater in its place. And with what money? From the fixing holes left by a vanished bronze inscription on one of the blocks, epigraphist Géza Alföldy reconstructed a text stating that the amphitheater had been built from war booty—probably from the treasures seized in Jerusalem in 70. The prestige of one ruler replaced that of another; surplus taken in war went into spectacle.

**Bread and circuses.** Around the turn of the first to the second century, the poet Juvenal coined a phrase about the Roman crowd that became proverbial: it wanted only “bread and circuses.” Over two thousand years, these words became a verdict: rulers bought obedience, and the people sold freedom for handouts. Our framework requires a more careful examination, because bread and circuses are very different categories.

Let us begin with the spectacles. Games, chariot races in the Circus Maximus, wild-beast hunts, and gladiatorial combat created nothing that survived the day of the performance. They did not teach or feed anyone, or reduce the costs of exchange. Animals were captured in Africa and the East, carried across the sea, and killed in the arena; environmental historian Donald Hughes and others believe that such captures undermined populations of large predators over centuries and contributed to the disappearance of the North African elephant. Gladiators—trained, powerful people whose preparation took years—died for the audience’s pleasure. This is wasted expenditure in something close to laboratory form: resources consumed, productivity and welfare not increased, and some things destroyed.

Bread is more complicated. We discussed grain distribution in the section on exchange: two hundred thousand adult citizens received a monthly ration. It was not wasted expenditure—the people ate the bread, and in a city where many survived on casual work, the ration served as a form of insurance against hunger. In the history of social policy, the Roman *annona* was one of the first experiments in a guaranteed minimum. But it had two defects that make it a model of unproductive redistribution. First, the recipients were not those in need but citizens of the capital: political membership in Rome, not poverty, conferred the right to bread. A poor person in Capua or Carthage received nothing. Second, the bread was paid for by provinces that received nothing in return. Redistribution ran not from those with means to those in need, but from those far from power to those near it. Such redistribution creates nothing; it merely moves surplus to where it helps preserve order.

Honesty nevertheless requires us to note the ambiguity. To bring hundreds of thousands of tons of grain into Rome each year, the state had to build the ports, warehouses, and lighthouses discussed above, negotiate with shipowners, and grant them privileges. According to Suetonius, Emperor Claudius assumed merchants’ storm losses so that they would transport grain even in winter. The objective was political, but the by-product—the logistics on which private trade also relied—was thoroughly developmental.

**Games as the price of power.** Why did the Roman elite spend such sums on the arena? In *Bread and Circuses* (1976), the French historian Paul Veyne examined the phenomenon he called euergetism, from the Greek word for “benefactor”: the wealthy gave a city buildings, feasts, and performances and received honor and influence in return. We have already seen this mechanism in Athenian liturgies and the city councils of the empire. It could produce an aqueduct—or gladiators.

In the late Republic, it became a rent-seeking machine. To win elected office, a candidate had to please the voters; the best method was to stage games. In 65 BCE, while serving as aedile—a magistrate responsible, among other things, for city festivals—Julius Caesar, according to Plutarch, brought three hundred and twenty pairs of gladiators into the arena and incurred enormous debts. To leave for Spain as governor, he needed a guarantee from the richest man in Rome, Crassus. Caesar returned from Spain with money. The cycle is clear: borrow to stage games; use the games to buy office; use the office to obtain a province; use the province to repay the debts. Economists call such behavior rent-seeking—spending not to create wealth, but to gain the right to extract it. Money burned twice in the games: once in the arena, and again in the province from which it was later squeezed.

**Prestige against enterprise.** Rent-seeking was reinforced by outright prohibitions. The Claudian Law of 218 BCE (not to be confused with the emperor; this was the tribune Quintus Claudius) forbade senators and their sons to own seagoing ships with a capacity greater than three hundred amphorae. Trade was considered an occupation unworthy of the nobility. Land, office, and war were worthy. The wealthiest and best-educated men in Rome were therefore cut off from activities in which surplus might have become new methods of production and directed toward those in which it became estates, slaves, and honor. Some senators, of course, traded through freedmen and fronts, but the general norm remained: wealth was for status, not enterprise.

Hence the consumption that ancient moralists denounced for centuries. Villas on the Bay of Naples with ponds for moray eels and oysters; the feasts of Lucullus, whose name became a byword; silk, pearls, and incense from the East, for which, as Pliny lamented, coin drained away to India. The Romans tried to combat such consumption with sumptuary laws. The Oppian Law of 215 BCE, passed at the height of the war with Hannibal, limited women’s gold jewelry and colored garments, but was repealed twenty years later. Such laws were passed again and again—and almost never enforced. Tacitus relates that when the Senate discussed restraining luxury, Tiberius essentially replied that it was impossible. Status consumption was too important to the way the elite displayed its position.

**Athens: marble, fleet, and festival.** The Greek case is subtler, and easy to misjudge. Were the Parthenon and the temples of the Acropolis, built with the allies’ money, wasted expenditure or investment? Plutarch gives the argument of Pericles’ supporters: the construction fed stonemasons, carters, coppersmiths, and goldsmiths—everyone who did not serve in the fleet—so that the treasury shared its wealth with the whole city. This is the classic argument for public works, and we heard it in the case of the pyramids. It is partly true: the money did become wages for free people, not merely luxury for one family. But employment could also have come from construction that left behind harbors and roads—and what remained here was primarily temples. Twenty-five centuries later they bring tourists to Athens, but for fifth-century Greeks they were prestige paid for with tribute.

Another example is still clearer. In the middle of the fourth century BCE, urging the Athenians to arm themselves against Philip of Macedon, Demosthenes reproached them in his *First Philippic*: the city spent more on the Panathenaea and Dionysia, its great festivals, than on any military campaign, and the festivals were always ready on time while the fleet was always late. Thirteen years later, at Chaeronea, Macedon prevailed. The festival was not, of course, mere expenditure: the theater in which Sophocles and Aristophanes were performed was one of the great investments in culture. But the allocation of money when the city’s freedom was at stake shows how difficult it is for a community to direct surplus where it is needed most.

**The army: defense or the price of power.** The army was the largest item in Roman expenditure: historians including Keith Hopkins and Richard Duncan-Jones estimate that it consumed most of the imperial state budget. It would be unfair to classify all of it as wasted expenditure: the legions on the Rhine and Danube formed the very wall behind which the trade of the *Pax Romana* lived. But once the army began deciding who would be emperor, an ever greater share of its support became payment for loyalty: accession gifts, bonuses, promises. This was no longer defense, but the price of power taken by force. In the third century the price grew so high that it was paid through debasement of the coinage—and waste became destruction.

**Intelligence spent on toys.** Finally, there was another, less visible form of waste: wasted talent. Hero of Alexandria described automata that opened temple doors when a fire was lit on the altar, a mechanical theater with moving figures, and vessels that poured wine and water in alternation. This was inventive mechanics, and it passed down to Arab and medieval engineers. But its purpose was to astonish an audience in a temple or at a banquet. Talents capable of changing production went where people paid for them: into marvels for priests, amusements for the wealthy, and engines of war. The point is not that ancient engineers were less intelligent than ours, but that the surplus which might have paid for their work on a useful machine went elsewhere.

Let us assemble the answer. Antiquity’s wasted expenditure was not a collection of isolated eccentricities but a system: games that bought public office; bread distributed by right of residence in the capital; marble paid for with tribute; luxury as the language of status; an army that changed from defense into the purchase of power. Together, these account for a substantial part of why Rome, despite possessing roads, harbors, law, and coinage, never made the transition to productivity growth. The surplus existed—more of it than in any earlier civilization. But its best part went into displaying and preserving power, not into making the labor of the plowman or weaver more productive.

## The Lesson of the Era

About six hundred years passed between the Athenian stele on coin inspection and the final layer of shards on Monte Testaccio. During that time the Mediterranean became a single market and then broke apart again into regions, each living on its own. Antiquity is the first age in our book in which prosperity per person appears to have risen for centuries, and the first in which we can trace how that growth was lost. Let us reduce the argument to five propositions.

**First: growth produced by order is fragile unless it rests on growth in output.** Ancient wealth was created by peace at sea, ports, common coinage, unified law, and provincial specialization. Economists sometimes call such growth “Smithian,” after Adam Smith, who first showed how the division of labor and expansion of the market make every hour of work more productive. But this kind of growth has a ceiling and a vulnerability. The ceiling was that the plowman, the oarsman, and the miner worked with the same hands and the same ox as in the age of Pericles. The vulnerability was that every gain depended on the integration of the space. When epidemics, civil wars, and debased money came, the connections broke and the gain vanished with them. The knowledge of how to fire roof tiles did not disappear from books in fifth-century Britain—but the market that bought them and the coin that paid for them did. A skill not embedded in each worker’s output proved to have been borrowed from order.

**Second: cheap coerced labor suppresses demand for invention.** Antiquity knew gears, pumps, waterwheels, and steam power. It was no less intelligent than the modern era. But where work could be performed by the hands of a purchased person, an owner had little reason to pay an engineer for a machine. Slaves received none of the gain from better work and had no interest in seeking a new method; owners who did receive the gain could simply buy more slaves as long as wars supplied them. We have presented the honest counterargument: the watermills of Barbegal and the wheels in the mines show that Romans could mechanize where labor was scarce. Perhaps slavery was not the only or even the chief reason—expensive fuel, weak markets deep in the provinces, and the status-based contempt for craft all played a role. But the conclusion stands: a society in which labor is cheap and without rights pays little to conserve it. We shall return to this in Chapter 10, when we examine the reverse situation—expensive labor and cheap coal in eighteenth-century England.

**Third: a rule that binds the ruler is the most enduring investment.** The *kleroterion*, accounts carved in stone, a tribune’s veto, a praetor’s action, a contract formed by simple agreement of the parties—all cost very little and lasted longer than the aqueducts. Roman law outlived the empire by fifteen hundred years. These inventions directed surplus toward public benefit by making extraction visible and punishable: Verres could plunder Sicily, but Cicero could prosecute him. Yet these rules had two limits. They protected citizens, not everyone, and the slave remained a thing. And they retreated before anyone with an army: the proscriptions showed that property was protected by law exactly while the law remained stronger than the sword.

**Fourth: power without a rule of succession pays for itself by debasing money.** The debasement of the denarius was no one’s evil plan. Each emperor was solving his own problem—paying the soldiers on whom his life depended—and each found the quietest means: taking a little silver from everyone who held a coin. The sum of those quiet decisions destroyed the trust the Athenians had once protected by law and by the tester in the agora. From this comes a conclusion we shall encounter repeatedly, all the way to the twentieth century: inflation is a tax for which no one voted, and it is used most often by a government unable to ask consent for an explicit tax.

**Fifth: surplus distributed by proximity to power feeds, but does not build.** Bread for two hundred thousand citizens of Rome, debt-financed games staged to obtain office, the prohibition on senators engaging in maritime trade, marble paid for with allied tribute—in all these cases the deciding factor was not where the gain would do the most good, but who stood closest to the center. A Roman received the ration, not a poor person from Capua; the province paid, but did not decide. Liturgies and euergetism show that an obligation on the wealthy to share could produce either an aqueduct or gladiators—and the choice depended on what earned honor. A society that rewards spectacle with honor gets spectacle.

Now for the answer to our recurring question. Where did the surplus of antiquity go? As in earlier ages, most of it went to support a growing population—at the empire’s peak, several times larger than the population of the entire Bronze Age Mediterranean. Of the remainder, a notable share went into development, more than we have yet seen: roads, harbors, aqueducts, safe seas, common coinage, law, and knowledge open to scrutiny. A very large share went into destruction: wars, the conversion of millions of people into commodities, the plunder of provinces, the debasement of money. And a large share went into waste: games, distributions based on citizenship, marble, luxury, and toward the end, the army as payment for power. What was new compared with the age of metals was that surplus was allocated publicly for the first time: an assembly voted on a fleet, a magistrate rendered accounts, a court heard a province’s complaint. Publicity did not guarantee a wise choice—Demosthenes rebuked his fellow citizens for their festivals in vain—but it offered a chance to correct one. Where the decision passed into the camp of the legions, that chance disappeared.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. Almost everything we know about the ancient economy was written by wealthy men about others like themselves; the share of slaves, the population, and living standards are reconstructions disputed by Finley and Temin, Scheidel and Harper. What destroyed the Western Empire is disputed as well: some historians place disease and climate first, others Germanic invasions, still others internal wars and the tax burden, while a fourth group following Peter Brown questions whether “fall” is the right word at all. The eastern half of the same empire, with the same law, the same monetary history, and the same slavery, survived for another thousand years—so our five propositions do not explain everything. Yet the central point holds: antiquity created public goods better than anyone before it, but never transformed surplus into the machine, and when order collapsed, it had nothing with which to replace it.

Was this ceiling peculiar to the Mediterranean? During the same centuries, on the other side of Eurasia, lay a civilization that built canals longer than Roman roads, recruited officials through examinations, invented paper, and later paper money and printing. Its fleet once reached Africa—and was disbanded by order of its own court. In the next chapter we shall leave Europe and ask why wealthy civilizations capable of so much repeatedly came to a halt.

## The Numbers

Antiquity left behind more numbers than all preceding ages combined: inscriptions, accounts, censuses, papyri, coin hoards, shipwrecks, and even traces of lead in Greenland ice. Yet almost all these figures concern cities, citizens, and treasuries. They speak only indirectly about peasants, slaves, and women. Everything below is an estimate, and where specialists disagree, that is stated.

**Population.** Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones estimate that around the beginning of the Common Era, some 170 million people lived on Earth; Angus Maddison gave a higher figure, about 230 million. Bruce Frier and Walter Scheidel estimate the population of the Roman Empire under Augustus at roughly 45 million, and at its peak in the second century most estimates range from 60 to 75 million. Rome itself held between half a million and a million inhabitants by varying estimates (Elio Lo Cascio and Neville Morley favor around a million, Glenn Storey fewer); by the sixth century, historians estimate that only tens of thousands remained. According to calculations by Mogens Hansen, fourth-century BCE classical Athens had about thirty thousand full adult male citizens within a total population of Attica numbering several hundred thousand, including women, children, immigrants, and slaves.

**Income per person.** Maddison estimated average income in the Roman Empire around 1 CE at roughly 570 “1990 international dollars” (an artificial unit that permits comparison across eras), and in Italy at around 800. For comparison, subsistence in the same scale was about 400. Scheidel and Steven Friesen arrived at a similar result in a 2009 study: average income in the empire was roughly one and a half to two times the level of bare survival. By their calculation, the top one and a half percent of the population—senators, equestrians, and urban elites—received about one-fifth of all income. In figures, this is the answer to “where did the surplus go?”: a thin layer above subsistence, a substantial part of which was captured by a narrow upper stratum.

**Signs of growth.** Output cannot be measured directly, but indirect traces remain. In A. J. Parker’s data, the number of dated Mediterranean shipwrecks in the first century BCE and first century CE is several times higher than before or after—sailors drowned more often because they sailed more often. Lead produced during silver smelting left, according to research by Joseph McConnell and coauthors (2018), a curve in Greenland ice that rises with the Republic and the Empire and falls sharply after the Antonine Plague of 165. Ian Morris estimated that the median area of a Greek house grew from roughly fifty square meters around 800 BCE to more than three hundred by 300 BCE. Monte Testaccio—approximately fifty-three million broken amphorae, according to the team led by José Remesal Rodríguez—shows the scale of the flow of oil alone into the capital.

**Money.** Under Augustus, the denarius contained about 3.9 grams of nearly pure silver. Nero reduced its weight and fineness; by the end of the second century the coin was roughly half silver, while in the 260s the antoninianus, which circulated as a double denarius, contained only a few percent silver according to numismatists. A legionary’s annual pay rose from 225 denarii under Augustus to 300 under Domitian and, according to ancient authors, to roughly 675 under Caracalla—each increase financed in part by debasing the coinage. Diocletian’s Edict of 301 set a maximum price of one hundred denarii for a military *modius* of wheat (about seventeen and a half liters—twice the ordinary *modius*, according to Richard Duncan-Jones’s calculation), which historians calculate was dozens of times higher than first-century prices.

**Distributions and spectacles.** Before Caesar’s reform, according to Suetonius, about three hundred and twenty thousand people in Rome received free grain. Caesar reduced the list to one hundred and fifty thousand; under Augustus it settled at around two hundred thousand. At Trajan’s games of 107, according to Cassius Dio, roughly eleven thousand animals were killed and ten thousand gladiators appeared in the arena over one hundred and twenty-three days.

**Slavery.** Estimates of the slave population of Roman Italy at the end of the Republic range from Peter Brunt’s roughly three million (as much as two-fifths of the population) to Walter Scheidel’s no more than one and a half million (between one-sixth and one-quarter); for the empire as a whole, the figure usually cited is ten to fifteen percent. A single campaign could deliver an immense consignment to the market: according to Livy, one hundred and fifty thousand people were sold in Epirus in 167 BCE; according to Caesar, fifty-three thousand Aduatuci in 57 BCE.

**The body and life expectancy.** Reconstructed life expectancy at birth in the Roman Empire, according to Frier, was roughly twenty to thirty years; as before, infant mortality determined the figure, while a person who reached twenty had a good chance of living to fifty. Based on skeletal evidence analyzed by Nikola Koepke and Joerg Baten, average male height remained between 165 and 170 centimeters and did not increase during the imperial period. This may be the most sobering figure in the chapter: roads, harbors, and common coinage made the world wealthier, but the ordinary person’s body barely noticed.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; errors by a factor of several are possible. By Columella’s norms, one *iugerum* of wheat (a quarter of a hectare) requires about ten working days and yields one hundred to two hundred kilograms after seed is deducted. That comes to twenty-five to fifty hours of peasant labor per fifty-kilogram sack of grain. A working life is about seventy thousand hours.

### The Colosseum

**Labor.** No construction accounts survive, so I calculate from volume. According to engineer Giuseppe Cozzo’s estimates, more than one hundred thousand cubic meters of travertine were used; including tuff, brick, and concrete, I assume two hundred to three hundred thousand cubic meters of masonry (this needs verification). Quarried stone had to be extracted near Tibur, hauled thirty kilometers, dressed, and raised—ten to twenty person-days per cubic meter; concrete and brick required three to six. With finishing, the total is three to ten million person-days: one to four thousand people working year-round for eight to ten years. The popular figure of “twenty to thirty thousand builders” has no reliable source; if true, it raises the total five- to tenfold. Fodder for the oxen is not included.

**Grain, hours, lives.** Sixty to two hundred thousand sacks represent one and a half to ten million hours of peasant labor; adding the builders’ own hours brings the total to thirty to one hundred and ten million. Altogether, that is five hundred to fifteen hundred working lives—about a thousand, roughly twenty times fewer than the Pyramid of Khufu. But games continued in the Colosseum for four centuries, and their cost—animals, gladiators, years of training—is not included here: the data do not exist.

**Alternative.** According to Suetonius, under Claudius thirty thousand people spent eleven years cutting a tunnel to drain Lake Fucine and obtain farmland. Even if the figure is overstated threefold, the Colosseum represents between one-tenth and one-third of such a project. Its grain, meanwhile, equals between two weeks and a month and a half of Rome’s grain distributions.

### The Sicilian Expedition

**Assumptions.** In 415–413 BCE, Athens and its allies sent roughly two hundred triremes to Sicily; during the retreat, according to Thucydides, the army numbered about forty thousand. I assume thirty-five to forty-five thousand men over six hundred days. According to Thucydides, the rowers were paid one drachma a day—roughly four thousand talents, six to seven years of allied tribute.

**Labor withdrawn.** Twenty to twenty-seven million person-days; feeding them required four hundred to five hundred and fifty thousand sacks, or ten to twenty-seven million hours of peasant labor. Adding the hours of the men themselves, who were not working at home, brings the total to two hundred and ten to three hundred million hours: three to four thousand working lives.

**Lives in the literal sense.** According to Thucydides, about seven thousand prisoners were held in the Syracusan quarries and sold; few returned. If thirty to thirty-five thousand men around the age of thirty were killed or sold, each lost roughly half a working life—another fifteen to seventeen thousand lives. The total is about twenty thousand, on a par with the Pyramid of Khufu, but over two years rather than twenty. The lost triremes are not included.

**Alternative.** The expedition’s grain amounted to twenty to twenty-seven thousand tons—a year’s bread for eighty to one hundred thousand people, roughly a third of Attica. Athens depended on imported grain; such a reserve would have closed the vulnerability Lysander exploited in 405 BCE when he cut the grain route at the Hellespont.

**Conclusion.** The arena’s stone cost antiquity hundreds of lives; one failed war cost tens of thousands. Destruction, not marble, was the most expensive item in its accounts.
