# Chapter 2. The Neolithic Revolution

## Portrait of the Era

On the middle Euphrates, in what is now northern Syria, there is a place that no longer exists. In 1972–1973, British archaeologist Andrew Moore was racing to excavate the mound of Abu Hureyra: the Tabqa Dam was about to begin operating, and Lake Assad was flooding the valley. Moore managed to uncover only a small part of the settlement before the water closed over it forever. But that was enough to make Abu Hureyra one of the world’s best-known archaeological sites. Its layers record, like the pages of a book, the transition that changed everything that followed: over several thousand years, people who lived by hunting gazelles and gathering wild cereals became settled farmers with fields, herds of goats and sheep, and mud-brick houses pressed tightly together.

The most eloquent evidence in this record is not the grain or the walls, but the bones of the inhabitants themselves. British anthropologist Theya Molleson, who studied skeletons from Abu Hureyra, noticed unusual marks. Many people, especially women, had deformed toes, worn knees, distorted lower vertebrae, and powerful signs of strain on their arms and shoulders. Such changes occur when a person spends hours kneeling, toes braced against the ground, rocking back and forth. That is how grain is ground on a quern—a flat stone across which another stone is pushed. Bread, which became the foundation of the new way of life, demanded this labor every day: several hours on one’s knees to produce enough flour for a single family.

This is the defining scene of the era. Not the solemn moment when someone cast the first seed into the soil, but thousands of ordinary mornings on which women wore their knees against stone for the sake of porridge and flatbread. The Neolithic Revolution—the word *Neolithic* means “New Stone Age,” after the polished stone tools archaeologists once considered its defining feature—was neither sudden nor deliberate. No one decided to “adopt agriculture.” Step by step, people came to rely more heavily on a small number of plants and animals, while the plants and animals, in turn, changed under human influence. After hundreds of generations, neither side could manage without the other.

The scale of the event is clearest on a map. Agriculture did not arise in a single place and was not “invented” only once. Around eleven to ten thousand years ago in the Fertile Crescent—the arc of land running from the Levant through southeastern Turkey to the foothills of the Zagros in Iran—people domesticated einkorn and emmer wheat, barley, lentils, peas, and flax, followed by goats, sheep, pigs, and cattle. Around nine to eight thousand years ago in China, people began cultivating rice in the Yangtze Valley and millet in the Yellow River Valley. In New Guinea’s marshy Kuk Valley, people were digging drainage ditches for taro and bananas around seven thousand years ago, and perhaps earlier. In Mesoamerica, they domesticated squash and, over millennia, bred maize from the unremarkable wild grass teosinte; in the Andes, they domesticated potatoes, quinoa, llamas, and alpacas. Other centers arose in the Sahel, Ethiopia, and eastern North America. In *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, Jared Diamond counts between five and nine such independent centers: five beyond dispute and as many as four more that are probable. People on different continents, who had never heard of one another, arrived at the same solution. This suggests that agriculture was not the product of one brilliant insight, but of circumstances that emerged at roughly the same time across the planet.

The principal circumstance was climate. The Ice Age ended, and around 11,700 years ago the Holocene began—the warm and, above all, unusually stable epoch in which we still live. Before it, the climate fluctuated sharply: droughts and cold spells could alternate within one or two generations. Shortly before the Holocene came the last such blow, a cooling period geologists call the Younger Dryas; it lasted about twelve hundred years. Some researchers, including Gordon Hillman, who studied plant remains from Abu Hureyra, proposed that during these difficult centuries local people began sowing wild rye so as not to lose a familiar source of food. This interpretation is disputed, and the finds on which it rests have been reexamined many times. But the broad picture is accepted by almost everyone: in a stable, warm climate, betting on a field ceased to be a gamble. People could reasonably expect to harvest what they had sown.

Within a few thousand years, the world looked different from the one in the previous chapter. At Jericho in the Jordan Valley, around ten to eleven thousand years ago, inhabitants had already erected a stone tower about eight meters high and a wall around the settlement—structures that required the labor of hundreds of people. At Çatalhöyük on the Anatolian plain, between 7400 and 6000 BCE, the population numbered between three and eight thousand by various estimates. Houses stood wall to wall, with no streets; people entered through the roof and buried their dead beneath the floor. For people whose ancestors had traveled for centuries in groups of twenty or thirty, this was unprecedented crowding.

What, then, happened to surplus, the central character of this book? For the first time in history, it became tangible and abundant: grain in pits and baskets, herds, houses that could be passed on to children. In the first chapter, we saw that hunter-gatherer surplus rarely took the form of things, instead flowing into knowledge, connections, and population growth. Now it acquired a body. And with that body came questions that had never existed before. Who stores the grain? Who decides how much of it to eat, how much to save for seed, and how much to give to neighbors, ancestors, or gods? Who will protect the store—and who will want to seize it?

Here, too, lies the era’s central paradox, which the rest of this chapter will explore. Agriculture made it possible to feed dozens of times more people from the same land. But bones from Abu Hureyra and hundreds of other settlements show that, at first, it brought the individual not relief but harder work, a poorer and more monotonous diet, and new diseases. In *Sapiens*, Yuval Noah Harari even called the transition to agriculture “history’s biggest fraud.” That is a considerable exaggeration, and we will see why. But the question behind it—who received the benefit of this new power if most people worked harder?—runs through this entire chapter. And therefore through the entire book.

## Energy and Technology

Seen through an engineer’s eyes, the Neolithic’s central invention was not a tool but an organism. Farmers did not invent a new engine: the Sun remained the source of energy, as before, while plants and muscles remained its converters. But they remade the plants themselves, so that sunlight falling on each patch of land became edible matter far more densely than on the wild steppe.

The process can be seen in an ear of grain. When wild wheat and barley ripen, the ear shatters on its own: every grain falls to the ground and sows itself. This benefits the plant but costs the gatherer, because a ripe field literally sheds its grain beneath human hands. Occasionally, mutant plants appear with a tough rachis, the central stem of the ear, that does not break. In nature they are doomed: their grains do not fall and germinate. But a person who cuts ears with a sickle and sows part of the harvest unwittingly selects precisely these plants. Botanists studying cereal remains from the Middle East have established that this trait became fixed slowly—over one to three thousand years, according to the varying estimates of archaeobotanists such as Catherine Tanno and George Willcox, and Dorian Fuller—not within a few harvests, as scholars once believed. Domestication was not a discovery but a very long, unconscious breeding experiment. In Mesoamerica it produced an even more dramatic result: over millennia, an ear of wild teosinte a few centimeters long, with a dozen hard-cased kernels, became maize.

The same occurred with animals. Goats, sheep, pigs, and cattle became smaller than their wild ancestors, calmer, and more tolerant of confinement. At first they were kept for meat—a living reserve, “canned food on legs” that could be slaughtered in a hungry year. Only later, British archaeologist Andrew Sherratt proposed in 1981, did people learn to take from an animal what did not require its death: milk, wool, and power for transport and plowing. Sherratt called this the “secondary products revolution.” Chemical analysis of fatty residues on pottery fragments has shown that milk was used in the Middle East and Anatolia as early as nine thousand years ago, earlier than he supposed; the draft power of oxen, the plow, and the wheel appeared closer to the end of the era, in the fourth millennium BCE. But Sherratt’s logic holds: an animal that gives milk throughout its life produces many times more food from the same grass than one eaten only once.

Supporting technologies grew up around plants and animals. The polished stone axe that gave the era its name was not an ornament but a tool of clearance: it felled forests to make fields. Sickles were made from a wooden or bone base fitted with flint blades; archaeologists find a characteristic sheen on their edges—“sickle gloss,” the trace left by thousands of cut stalks. The querns discussed in the previous section became the era’s most labor-intensive household device. People learned to spin and weave flax. Houses were built of mud brick—sun-dried clay mixed with straw.

Pottery has a separate history. It is often treated as a marker of agriculture, but the first clay vessels appeared earlier: in East Asia, in China and among Japan’s Jōmon culture, they were made by hunters and gatherers. Sherds from Xianrendong Cave in China date to around twenty thousand years ago; Japanese examples to around sixteen thousand. Conversely, the early Neolithic Middle East went for a long time without pots; archaeologists call it the “Pre-Pottery Neolithic.” This is a good example of technologies refusing to arrange themselves into a tidy staircase. Yet it was among farmers that pottery became ubiquitous: grain and legumes could be boiled for long periods in a pot, making them softer and more nutritious, while stores could be protected from mice and moisture. Firing clay was the first major use of fire not for food or warmth but to alter a substance. Thousands of years later, metallurgy would grow from the same skill: the first beads of smelted copper are known from the fifth millennium BCE.

Finally, storage. At Dhra‘ near the Dead Sea, archaeologists Ian Kuijt and Bill Finlayson described in 2009 structures around 11,300 years old that they interpret as purpose-built granaries: raised wooden floors protecting against damp and rodents, standing between dwellings. If this interpretation is correct, people built grain stores before grain was fully domesticated. The granary was a modest but revolutionary technology: it stretched the harvest of one brief season across the entire year and turned good fortune into a reserve.

Now to the section’s central question: did all this make human beings more productive? The answer is surprising. Per unit of land, unquestionably, and by a great deal. In *Energy and Civilization*, Czech-Canadian energy scholar Vaclav Smil shows that even primitive agriculture can feed dozens of times more people per hectare than hunting and gathering in the same region. But the picture differs per hour of labor. In 2011, American economist Samuel Bowles attempted to compare how many calories an hour of work yielded for an early farmer using hand tools and for a forager. His estimate, based on ethnographic and experimental evidence and necessarily very approximate, suggests that early cereals brought no gain and probably a loss: obtaining the same food required as much labor or more. A field had to be cleared, dug, sown, weeded, guarded from birds and neighbors, harvested, threshed, and ground.

This distinction is crucial to the entire book. The Neolithic increased output not per hour but per hectare and, above all, made it predictable and storable. Society had more energy because it had more people and more land converted into fields, not because each person worked more intelligently. By the first of our criteria—productivity per person—it was a questionable step. By others—density, the ability to store, the accumulation of many people’s experience in one place—it was enormous. For the first time, the era’s surplus could be measured in granaries. But it grew together with the mouths that had to be fed, creating a trap to which we will return: every new increment of grain was quickly consumed by a new increment of population. Escaping it would require draft animals, the plow, metal, and ultimately coal. Nearly ten thousand years still lay ahead.

## Organization of Labor

Let us return to the women of Abu Hureyra kneeling over their querns. Their worn joints tell us not only how arduous the work was, but also how differently it was organized. Among hunter-gatherers, labor was scattered across the day and the landscape: go out for nuts, track an animal, return to the fire. Agriculture gathered it into different forms. The household appeared—a home, field, granary, and herd fixed in one place, with people who tended them throughout their lives. For millennia to come, the chief unit of production would be the family living under one roof.

The first feature of the new labor was its delayed return. British anthropologist James Woodburn, who studied the Hadza of Tanzania, divided societies into two groups as early as the 1980s. In one, returns are immediate: what you obtain, you eat or distribute the same day. In the other, returns are delayed: you invest effort today and receive the result months or years later. A field is the classic example of the latter. It is cleared in winter, sown in autumn, harvested in early summer, and its grain eaten throughout the following year. A long interval now separated labor from reward, and throughout it people had to remember who was owed what. This seems a small change, but from it would grow debt, obligation, and the right to a harvest—all the subjects of the sections on money and power.

The second feature was unevenness. The agricultural year consists of long, relatively quiet weeks and brief, desperate peaks. A harvest must be gathered within days, before the grain shatters and the rains arrive; a field must be sown while the soil is moist. In such weeks, no family has enough hands. The Neolithic therefore created a new kind of cooperation: neighbors came together for a shared harvest or forest clearance, and afterward the host fed and gave drink to the helpers. Ethnographers describe such “work feasts” throughout the world—from African villages to the Russian *toloka*, in which help given to neighbors was rewarded with a feast. Many archaeologists believe that major Neolithic structures, such as the tower at Jericho, were organized in a similar way.

The most striking example is Göbekli Tepe in southeastern Turkey. German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt, who excavated there beginning in 1995, found circular enclosures formed by T-shaped stone pillars up to five and a half meters tall, carved with foxes, boars, snakes, and vultures. The oldest are around 11,500 years old, meaning they were erected by people who had not yet adopted agriculture or were only at its very beginning. Quarrying, hauling, and raising multi-ton pillars required hundreds of workers, whom someone had to feed. Schmidt believed that the settlement did not create the sanctuary; rather, the sanctuary drew people together and may have prompted them to cultivate cereals to feed the collective workforce. The hypothesis is disputed: later excavations showed that people lived permanently on the hill. But the fact itself matters: large-scale collective labor—and therefore the ability to assemble and feed many workers—appeared together with agriculture, not after it.

The third feature was that children became workers. For nomadic foragers, frequent childbirth was difficult: a mother cannot carry two small children at once, so births were usually spaced three or four years apart. Sedentary life and cereal gruels, which allowed infants to be given supplementary food earlier, shortened the interval. French demographer Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel compared dozens of ancient cemeteries in Europe, the Middle East, and the Americas and found that the proportion of children’s skeletons in graves rises sharply with the arrival of agriculture. He interpreted this as evidence of fertility rising so much that the change has been called the “Neolithic demographic transition.” In a farming family, a child of six or seven could already tend animals, gather brushwood, or help with weeding. More children meant more hands. But also more mouths. Here lies the key to the trap described in the previous section: a large share of Neolithic surplus went not into things but into people.

The fourth feature was a more rigid sexual division of labor. Analysis of bones from many Neolithic sites shows that the physical loads borne by men and women differed more sharply than among foragers. Women were more often assigned daily domestic tasks: grinding, food preparation, weaving, and childcare. Men cleared land, plowed where the plow had appeared, traveled long distances with herds, and conducted exchange. Generalization is dangerous here, since the pattern varied greatly between regions. But the direction is visible: work was increasingly divided not by skill and circumstance but according to roles fixed in advance.

What scarcely existed were professions. At Çatalhöyük, British archaeologist Ian Hodder, who directed excavations beginning in 1993, found neither workshops nor craft districts: pottery, beads, and tools made from obsidian—volcanic glass—were produced independently in every household. The obsidian itself came from Cappadocia, almost two hundred kilometers away, and someone must have transported it regularly, but there were not yet whole classes of craftspeople. Individual specialists—potters, flintknappers—appeared toward the end of the era as settlements expanded. A full division into farmers, artisans, priests, and warriors would arrive in the next chapter, together with the first cities.

Where, then, did the surplus of labor go? To three places. Into the future—in cleared fields, granaries, houses, and herds that served for years and passed to children; this was the first capital in history in the narrow sense: made by human hands and accumulated. Into new people—in family growth that quickly consumed the larger harvest. And into collective enterprises—sanctuaries, walls, and feasts that held communities together but already required someone to assemble the workers and allocate the stores. The first was clearly developmental. The second was ambiguous: population brought security and density, but not wealth per person. The third opened the door both to organization and to extraction. Who would stand at that door is the question for the section on power.

## Exchange and Money

In the first chapter, we saw how hunter-gatherers strictly prevented anyone from becoming conceited about a kill: meat was shared, the successful hunter was mocked, and every surplus was given away. This order rested on more than morality alone. It made sense where meat spoiled within days and tomorrow’s luck might desert you and favor your neighbor. Sharing meant insurance. Grain changed the arithmetic. It could be stored for a year or longer, and the owner of a full granary no longer depended on the neighbors’ generosity tomorrow. The insurance once supplied by the community now lay in a pit beneath the owner’s floor.

Archaeologists can see this change literally in settlement plans. American archaeologist Kent Flannery compared the earliest villages of the Middle East and noticed a change in the shape of dwellings. The earliest settlements contained small round huts, while stores appear to have been kept in places accessible to many. Later, by roughly the ninth to eighth millennia BCE, rectangular houses with internal storerooms spread everywhere. Flannery interpreted this as a transition from communal to family storage: reserves moved behind a wall, where neighbors could no longer see them. The granaries at Dhra‘ discussed above stood between dwellings and probably belonged to the whole community. Three thousand years later at Çatalhöyük, each family kept its own supplies in the rear rooms of its house. Flannery’s reading is precisely that—an interpretation—and the finds permit alternatives. But the direction of travel is clear: surplus was moving from common to private ownership.

This does not mean that people stopped sharing. Rather, distribution changed form. Canadian archaeologist Brian Hayden, who spent many years studying so-called “competitive feasts,” argues that in the Neolithic, hospitality changed from an obligation into an instrument. A successful host held a feast, invited neighbors, fed workers clearing a field—and thereby acquired debtors. Guests were obliged to help, support the host in a dispute, or answer one feast with another. Hayden even suggests that the first domesticated plants, such as cereals used for beer, were valued not as daily bread but as raw material for such celebrations. This is one of the most controversial hypotheses about the origins of agriculture, and many specialists regard it as exaggerated. But the logic of the feast as a means of converting grain into influence is confirmed by ethnography across the world, from New Guinea to the Northwest Coast of North America.

Here emerges what anthropologist David Graeber placed at the center of all economic history in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*. The picture popular since Adam Smith runs as follows: people first exchanged things directly through barter, then found it inconvenient and invented money, followed later by credit. Citing ethnographers, Graeber argues that the order was the reverse. Anthropologists have found no society in which barter was the principal mode of exchange between neighbors. Neighbors live within a network of mutual obligations: I helped with your harvest—you owe me; you fed me—I owe you. Debt appears before money, while money is a later invention for measuring such debts and transferring them to strangers. Graeber has also been criticized: economists point out that barter between unfamiliar groups did occur historically and that his book sometimes oversimplifies. But for the Neolithic, his picture appears closer to the truth: money did not yet exist, but debts did, and their number was growing.

Did the Neolithic possess even the rudiments of money? To some extent. Livestock almost everywhere became a measure of wealth and the chief means of settling large obligations—bridewealth, compensation for an insult or killing. It is no accident that the Latin word *pecunia*, “money,” derives from *pecus*, “livestock.” But a cow is an awkward coin: it cannot be divided into change, and it grows sick and dies. Something else is more intriguing. In the 1970s through the 1990s, American archaeologist Denise Schmandt-Besserat drew attention to small clay tokens—spheres, cones, disks, and cylinders—found in Middle Eastern layers dating from around the eighth millennium BCE onward. She proposed that these were accounting symbols: one cone for one measure of grain, one cylinder for one animal. If she is right, people began counting property and obligations in the Neolithic, long before the first cities. Thousands of years later, these tokens would be sealed in clay “envelopes,” their impressions pressed into the outside; from these impressions, she argues, writing would grow. Schmandt-Besserat’s hypothesis is disputed in its details: apparently not every token was used for accounting. But the connection between keeping track of stores and the birth of writing is accepted by almost everyone. This is the subject of the third chapter.

Meanwhile, long-distance exchange expanded. Obsidian from Cappadocia is found in settlements in the Levant hundreds of kilometers from its sources, becoming scarcer with distance—a sign of hand-to-hand transfer from village to village rather than regular caravans. British archaeologist Colin Renfrew demonstrated this as early as the 1960s through the chemical composition of obsidian, which reveals the volcano from which it came. In Europe, shells of the Mediterranean mollusk *Spondylus* from the Aegean Sea reached graves in the Danube Valley and even what is now Germany around 5500–5000 BCE. Polished axes of Alpine jadeite, found from Scotland to Bulgaria and studied by French archaeologists Pierre and Anne-Marie Pétrequin, often bear no traces of use: they were too precious for chopping. They were prestige objects, not tools.

And here is the section’s central conclusion for our recurring question. Neolithic exchange did two different things. One part—obsidian for blades, salt, flint, seed from new varieties, and the knowledge of how to sow and herd—carried useful things across the world and was clearly developmental; it was along such chains that agriculture spread from a handful of centers across almost all of Eurasia. The other part—jadeite axes, shells, competitive feasts—converted surplus into status. This was not yet wasteful expenditure in the full sense: feasts cemented alliances, while prestige goods connected distant communities. But for the first time, a mechanism appeared through which one person’s store became other people’s obligations. Debt, accounting, and prestige were the three bricks from which the next era would build temples, palaces, and the first states.

## Power and Institutions

In 1953, in layers at Jericho dating to the eighth and seventh millennia BCE, British archaeologist Kathleen Kenyon found strange objects: human skulls covered with plaster masks reproducing the features of the face, with shells inserted into the eye sockets. Similar skulls were later found in other settlements of the Levant. Bodies were buried beneath the floors of houses; after a time, the heads were removed, decorated, and apparently kept on display. Ancestors literally lived in the house alongside their descendants.

Why does this matter to economics? Because an ancestor beneath the floor is an argument. Among nomadic foragers, land belonged to everyone who moved across it, and no one had to prove a right to it. A cleared field, a house, and a granary were different: they embodied the labor of several generations, and those who inherited them wanted their neighbors to recognize their rights. The graves of ancestors, inside the house or beside the field, became something like the first proof of property: “Our people lie here, therefore this is ours.” Thus accumulated surplus brought with it the first institution in the narrow sense—a durable rule that outlived individual people. That institution was inheritance.

And inheritance is the principal engine of inequality. A hunter could not pass his luck in hunting to his son, but a farmer could pass on a field, a herd, and a full granary. If one family enjoyed better land, better weather, or more working hands, its advantage no longer dissipated within a generation; it accumulated. In 2017, American archaeologist Timothy Kohler and a large group of colleagues published in *Nature* an attempt to measure this process. They examined dwelling sizes in sixty-three ancient settlements around the world and calculated how unevenly they were distributed: if all houses were roughly alike, inequality was low; if spacious compounds stood beside hovels, it was high. They found little difference between the houses of foragers and early farmers, after which the gap widened. Over time, it became markedly larger in the Old World than in the Americas. The authors connected this to large domestic animals: oxen that pulled plows, and horses. A household with a team could cultivate more land than a family with hoes, widening the difference between farms. House size is a crude measure of wealth, as the authors acknowledge. But the trend it reveals is consistent with other evidence.

The most vivid example is the cemetery near the Bulgarian city of Varna, discovered by accident in 1972 during excavation of a cable trench. Around 4600–4300 BCE, people belonging to one of Europe’s earliest copper-working cultures were buried there. Most graves are modest. A few, however, contain thousands of gold objects: bracelets, plaques, fittings, and scepters. In one, numbered 43, a man was buried with almost a thousand gold objects weighing approximately one and a half kilograms in total. He is usually called a chief, though some researchers allow that he may have been a master metalworker; we do not know for certain. More than three thousand gold artifacts with a combined weight of about six kilograms were found in the cemetery—the oldest worked gold on this scale anywhere in the world. Gold neither plows nor feeds. This was surplus converted into a sign of status and then buried in the ground—permanently withdrawn from circulation.

Yet it would be a mistake to think that the Neolithic led inevitably and directly to chiefs and kings. Here again, Çatalhöyük is worth recalling. Over decades of excavation, Ian Hodder and his colleagues found no palace, temple, or elite quarter there. Houses were nearly identical in size and arrangement; isotope analysis of bones—the chemical traces of diet—shows that men and women ate broadly the same food. Several thousand people lived side by side for a thousand years with no visible supreme authority. Another two and a half millennia later, around 4000 BCE, so-called Trypillia megasites arose in what is now Ukraine. The largest, Talianki, covered more than three hundred hectares. Population estimates range from several thousand to fifteen thousand or more, but in any case these were concentrations of people comparable to early cities. They, too, had neither palaces, rich tombs, nor fortified citadels. Houses stood in rings around an empty center, while the settlements contained large “assembly” buildings that many researchers regard as places for communal deliberation.

These findings form one of the main arguments of David Graeber and David Wengrow’s *The Dawn of Everything* (2021). They challenge the familiar ladder of “community—chiefdom—state,” which humanity supposedly climbed by necessity. In their view, Neolithic peoples consciously experimented with different arrangements: in some places they allowed chiefs to rise; in others they stubbornly resisted them; sometimes they changed the organization of their community with the seasons. Critics reply that Graeber and Wengrow select convenient examples and underestimate how often such experiments nevertheless ended in hierarchy. Both observations are fair. What matters for our question is something else: surplus did not automatically dictate who would control it. There was a choice, and different societies made it differently.

The transitional forms of power are known best through ethnography. In 1963, American anthropologist Marshall Sahlins—the same scholar who coined the phrase “the original affluent society”—described the Melanesian “big-man.” He was neither a ruler nor an heir. He was an energetic household head who drove his wives and relatives to produce beyond their needs, raised pigs, held feasts, and distributed gifts, receiving supporters in return. His influence endured while he remained generous and collapsed when he aged or grew stingy. In *The Creation of Inequality* (2012), Kent Flannery and Joyce Marcus show that the decisive step occurred when such an earned position began to pass by inheritance. As long as status had to be earned, the “big-man” was compelled to share. Once it could be inherited, sharing was no longer necessary.

Finally, violence. The Neolithic world was no peaceful paradise. Around 5000 BCE at Talheim in southwestern Germany, thirty-four people—men, women, and children—were thrown into a single pit. Most had been killed by blows to the back of the head, largely with the same polished axes used to fell forests. At Schöneck-Kilianstädten near Frankfurt, a similar pit from the same period contained twenty-six people, many with broken lower legs—perhaps evidence of torture or mutilation. These were communities belonging to the same early farming culture. It appears that once wealth remained fixed in place—in fields, granaries, and herds—neighbors acquired a reason to come for it, while owners acquired a reason to build walls. In Israeli archaeologist Ofer Bar-Yosef’s interpretation, the wall of Jericho defended chiefly against floods and mudslides rather than enemies, but by the end of the Neolithic fortified settlements had become common.

Power in the Neolithic thus stood at a crossroads. A person who assembled workers for a communal harvest, guarded the common store, and reconciled neighbors performed genuinely useful work: reducing coordination costs and insuring the community against crop failure. But the same skills—directing the labor and grain of others—also served extraction. In *Against the Grain* (2017), American political scientist James C. Scott observed that cereals are especially convenient for anyone seeking to collect tribute: their harvest ripens at the same time, is visible in the field, and is easy to measure, transport, and store. A root crop can be left underground and dug up in secret; wheat cannot be hidden. Scott is concerned mainly with the first states, and we will return to his argument in the next chapter. But their soil was prepared here. The Neolithic created a surplus that could be counted, inherited, and seized, and for the first time posed the question that will run through this entire book: will the person who manages the common reserve be its organizer—or its owner?

## What Drove Development

Walk into any supermarket today, from Lisbon to Shanghai, and almost everything on its shelves can be traced back to the Neolithic. Bread and pasta lead back to the wheat of the Fertile Crescent; porridge and noodles to the rice of the Yangtze; cornflakes and popcorn to the teosinte of Mexico; potatoes to the Andes; and milk, cheese, beef, mutton, woolen sweaters, and linen tablecloths to the same handful of centers discussed at the beginning of this chapter. According to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), three cereals—rice, wheat, and maize—still provide humanity with anywhere from two-fifths to nearly two-thirds of all calories, depending on how they are counted. In ten thousand years, people have added remarkably few fundamentally new species to the Neolithic set. In essence, we live on an inheritance left by the nameless breeders of the Stone Age.

This was the era's first and principal engine of development: **the domesticated species as accumulated knowledge**. In the first chapter, we said that the foragers' main capital was knowledge stored in people's heads and transmitted through stories and demonstration. The Neolithic found a new medium for knowledge: the living organism. An ear of grain with a nonshattering rachis, a cow that stands calmly in an enclosure, flax with long fibers—all embody the experience of thousands of generations, recorded not in clay or memory but in the heredity of plants and animals. Such a “text” is not forgotten when an elder dies and does not have to be memorized: preserving the seed and the herd is enough. It can also be passed on—given away, exchanged, or carried to a new place. This is knowledge of a special kind: it works even for those who do not understand how it was acquired.

The second mechanism was **diffusion**. Agriculture did not have to be reinvented in every valley. Ancient-DNA research over the past decade and a half has shown that Europe's early farmers were largely descended from people who came from Anatolia and, over two or three thousand years—roughly from the seventh to the fourth millennium BCE—carried their seeds and herds as far as the Atlantic and Britain. In some places they mixed with local hunters, in others they displaced them, while elsewhere local people adopted the new skills themselves. In *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, Jared Diamond drew attention to an important fact: Eurasia extends from west to east, and places at the same latitude tend to have similar climates and day lengths. Wheat domesticated in Syria could grow in Greece, Germany, and northern India without extensive modification. In the Americas, which extend from north to south, crops had to be adapted repeatedly to new climates, and maize took millennia to travel from Mexico to what is now the United States. Diamond's argument simplifies matters—mountains, deserts, and soils impeded movement no less than latitude did—but its essence is sound: development accelerates wherever useful knowledge can easily pass from one community to another. This book will return to that idea more than once.

The third mechanism was **reserves as insurance and as a basis for deferred investment**. For the first time, a granary and a herd made it possible to survive a failed harvest without relying entirely on the mercy of one's neighbors. More important, however, reserves made possible work that did not pay off at once. Clearing a field, digging a ditch, building a brick house, or planting an orchard were investments that yielded returns over years. A forager living from one acquisition of food to the next could hardly make such investments: there was nothing to live on while they matured. A farmer could. The habit of denying oneself in the present for the sake of the future—the same “delayed return” discussed in the section on the organization of labor—became widespread. Without it there would have been no irrigation, no cities, and, thousands of years later, no factories.

The fourth mechanism was **density as an accelerator of invention**. In 1993, American economist and future Nobel laureate Michael Kremer published a paper that became a classic. His argument was simple: any person can make an invention, and everyone can use it. The more people there are, therefore, the more potential inventors there are and the faster progress becomes. Kremer supported this claim by comparing regions isolated after the end of the Ice Age: by 1500, technology was more complex across the vast expanse of Eurasia than in the Americas, more complex in the Americas than in Australia, and simplest of all on little Tasmania. Kremer's model is a simplification, criticized for underestimating the importance of connections and institutions: a populous but fragmented country may innovate slowly. For the Neolithic, however, the argument works. Settlements of thousands linked by chains of exchange accumulated skills faster than scattered groups of thirty. It was in this dense environment that, over a few thousand years, people developed pottery for bulk storage, weaving, fired brick, copper smelting, the wheel, and the plow.

The fifth mechanism was **accounting**. The Schmandt-Besserat clay tokens discussed in the section on exchange may be the era's most underrated achievement. People began to record outside their heads how much each person possessed and who owed what to whom. This was the first information technology after language, and from it writing would grow—along with law, science, and the bookkeeping that made large states and large-scale exchange possible.

Finally, the Neolithic changed people themselves. The best-known example is the ability of adults to digest milk. In most mammals, and still in most people around the world, the enzyme that breaks down milk sugar—lactase—stops being produced after infancy. Among pastoral peoples, however, a mutation spread that preserves its production throughout life. Ancient DNA shows that this mutation was rare among Neolithic Europeans and became common only by the Bronze Age, thousands of years after dairy livestock appeared. The connection with dairying is beyond doubt; the mechanism remains disputed. For a long time, scholars assumed that anyone able to drink milk simply gained an additional source of food. But in 2022, a group led by British chemist Richard Evershed examined traces of fat on nearly seven thousand pottery sherds and found that the extent of dairying did a poor job of explaining where and when the mutation spread. The authors proposed that its advantage emerged during famines and epidemics, when milk caused severe intestinal distress in people without the mutation—distress that a weakened body might not survive. This is a hypothesis, but in any event it offers a rare example of an economic innovation literally rewriting human genes.

We should now be honest about the limits of this list. Almost all these mechanisms promoted development over the long term. They increased the capabilities of humanity as a whole: its knowledge, its numbers, and its ability to store and invest. But by the third criterion in our framework—the quality of life of the broad majority—the Neolithic, as we saw in the bones from Abu Hureyra, initially lost ground. The benefits appeared on the scale of centuries and generations, while the costs fell on particular people in the here and now. The woman at the grinding stone did not become richer because her great-great-grandchildren would live in a world with the wheel and copper. This is an important distinction: the expansion of a society's capabilities and the growth of an individual's welfare do not always coincide in time. Sometimes millennia separate them.

Where, then, did the developmental share of the surplus go? Into seeds selected to be better than the previous year's. Into fields cleared once for many generations. Into herds that reproduced. Into skills that passed from village to village. This was the quiet, distributed work of millions of people, with neither an author nor a patron. No chief ordered maize to be domesticated. The people who ate it domesticated it.

## What Destroyed

Unlike in later eras, destruction in the Neolithic had almost no author. No one decided to exhaust the soil or infect a village. The damage arose as a by-product of the very changes that produced growth: population density, sedentism, proximity to animals, and dependence on a handful of crops. That is precisely what makes it instructive. It is history's first example of a developmental solution, pushed to its limit without regard for its costs, beginning to destroy its own foundation.

Let us begin with disease. Hunter-gatherers fell ill too—from parasites, infected wounds, and diseases acquired from wild animals. But small nomadic groups make poor hosts for mass epidemics. The measles virus, for example, must constantly find new susceptible people if it is not to disappear; epidemiologists estimate that sustaining its circulation requires a population of several hundred thousand. In a group of thirty, the disease would quickly infect everyone it could and then die out. The Neolithic created an entirely new environment for contagion. People lived close together, in the same place for years, beside their own refuse and animal dung; drinking water became contaminated; mice and rats infested granaries. Above all, herds now lived side by side with humans.

Jared Diamond made this one of the central arguments of *Guns, Germs, and Steel*. Many of humanity's deadliest diseases, he wrote, were gifts from domesticated animals. By comparing viruses, geneticists concluded that measles evolved from rinderpest; relatives of the pathogens that cause smallpox, whooping cough, and influenza are likewise found among animal diseases. Diamond, however, wrote before ancient DNA began to be read on a mass scale, and newer evidence has refined his picture. Current estimates suggest that measles diverged from the cattle virus much later than the Neolithic, around the first millennium BCE, when large cities had emerged. This does not refute the argument but extends it: herds created the reservoir, and population density gave the infection a chance to establish itself among humans. The Neolithic laid both foundations.

There is direct evidence as well. In 2015, a team led by Danish geneticist Eske Willerslev found traces of *Yersinia pestis* DNA in the teeth of Bronze Age people who lived about five thousand years ago. At the end of 2018, geneticist Nicolás Rascovan of Aix-Marseille University and his colleagues found the plague bacterium in a burial at Frälsegården in Sweden dating back roughly 4,900 years. The authors cautiously proposed that an early form of plague may have spread through the large settlements and trade routes of the late Neolithic, perhaps contributing to the decline experienced by many European communities in those centuries. This is a hypothesis, not an established fact. But the discovery itself shows that the disease that would devastate medieval Europe four and a half thousand years later was already traveling beside the first farmers.

The second calamity was the deterioration in health caused by the food itself. In 1984, American anthropologists Mark Cohen and George Armelagos assembled the collection *Paleopathology at the Origins of Agriculture*, in which dozens of specialists compared skeletons from before and after the transition to farming in various parts of the world. The picture was similar in most regions. Average height declined—in some places by several centimeters. Signs of anemia in the bones of the skull, apparently linked to iron deficiency and parasites, became more common. Horizontal grooves appeared in tooth enamel, evidence of hunger or severe illness in childhood. The number of decayed teeth rose sharply: porridge and flatbread made from starchy grain feed bacteria in the mouth far better than meat and roots do. Later research showed that the pattern was not identical everywhere: in some places, especially where farming was combined with fishing or herding, health suffered little. But most specialists accept the general direction of change during the first millennia. Food became more reliable but less nutritious.

The third calamity was the exhaustion of the land itself. The clearest example is Ain Ghazal, on the outskirts of modern Amman in Jordan, one of the largest settlements of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic. Around 7000 BCE, an estimated two to three thousand people lived there. Its residents were fond of lime plaster: they covered floors and walls with it and fashioned from it their famous statues with enormous eyes. But producing lime requires firing limestone at high heat, which in turn requires mountains of wood. American archaeologist Gary Rollefson and his colleague Ilse Köhler-Rollefson calculated that plastering, heating, and construction consumed vast amounts of timber, while a growing number of goats browsed on young shoots and prevented the forest from recovering. Bare slopes spread around the settlement, rain washed away the soil, and harvests declined. Around 6500 BCE, Ain Ghazal's population fell, and later much of it dispersed into small pastoral groups. Rollefson considers the devastation of the surrounding environment the main cause; other researchers add periods of arid climate. Most likely both acted together: a degraded environment withstands a climatic shock poorly.

Ain Ghazal's story was not unique. In many parts of the Middle East, large settlements emptied toward the end of the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, and archaeologists still debate how much blame lies with human activity and how much with climate. In Europe, British archaeologist Stephen Shennan and his colleagues compared thousands of radiocarbon dates in 2013 and showed that the arrival of agriculture in different regions was followed by rapid population growth and, several centuries later, a sharp decline. The “boom-and-bust” pattern recurred from Germany to Ireland. The causes of decline probably differed: soil exhaustion in the first cleared fields, disease, war, climate. But the general pattern is familiar: a new technology produces a surge, population rises to its limit, and the system collapses at the first adverse shock.

Here too lies the fourth calamity, a less conspicuous one: fragility. A forager ate dozens and sometimes hundreds of plant and animal species; when one failed, others compensated. A farmer staked everything on two or three cereals and a few kinds of livestock. This produced more food in a good year and catastrophe in a bad one. Drought, locusts, wheat disease, or livestock mortality now threatened not part of the diet, but all of it. The Neolithic thus exchanged diversity for volume—a bargain that scored well by the first criterion in our framework but badly by the fourth, resilience to shocks. Famine as a mass disaster recurring from generation to generation was largely an invention of agricultural societies. This is a strong claim, and it rests on comparison: in 2014, anthropologists Caryl Berbesque, Frank Marlowe, and their colleagues compared ethnographic data from dozens of societies and found that foragers suffered famine no more often—and probably less often—than farmers. The sample, however, is modern, and it can be applied to the Neolithic only with caution.

Finally, there was the violence discussed in the section on power. The pits at Talheim and Schöneck-Kilianstädten were not isolated episodes. German researchers have identified several similar mass graves associated with the Linear Pottery culture, Central Europe's first farmers, many of them dating to the culture's late period, around 5000 BCE—precisely when, according to Shennan's data, its population began to decline. It appears that when land grew scarce, neighbors began to settle the matter by force. Here war was not the cause of the crisis but its companion and accelerator: it destroyed people, harvests, and trust at the very moment all three were already in short supply.

Where, then, did the destroyed share of the surplus go? No one literally burned it. But much of the gain produced by agriculture was spent covering agriculture's own costs: burying children who died of new diseases, clearing new fields to replace depleted ones, rebuilding burned villages, and surviving crop failures that people had once had less reason to fear. This is the first, but not the last, appearance in this book of a regular pattern: without mechanisms to monitor its side effects, a developmental technology gives back part of its gains. Neolithic communities learned this through trial and error—and paid for every error in generations.

## What Was Wasted

Around 2500 BCE, on Salisbury Plain in southern England, people arranged enormous sandstone blocks known as sarsens in a circle. The stones weighed about twenty tons on average, and their builders capped them with stone lintels. Within the circle stood, or soon came to stand, smaller “bluestones” brought from the Preseli Hills in Wales, more than two hundred kilometers away. Scholars still debate exactly how they were transported, but one thing is clear: without wheels, draft animals capable of pulling such loads, or metal, the task required hundreds of people working for many years. Stonehenge was built and rebuilt over more than a thousand years. Nearby, at Durrington Walls, British archaeologist Mike Parker Pearson excavated a settlement where the builders apparently lived and held enormous winter feasts: tens of thousands of pig bones were found there. Isotope analysis of those bones by Richard Madgwick's team in 2019 showed that some of the animals had been driven from far away, even from Scotland.

Stonehenge is merely the most famous of thousands of monuments. Near Carnac in Brittany stretch rows of more than three thousand standing stones. In Ireland, the passage beneath the Newgrange mound, built around 3200 BCE, admits a ray of sunlight precisely at dawn on the winter solstice. Silbury Hill in England, raised around 2400 BCE, is Europe's largest human-made mound, standing about thirty meters high, yet no burial or treasure has been found inside. As early as the 1970s, British archaeologist Colin Renfrew attempted rough estimates of the labor required for such structures. By his calculation, a long barrow demanded about ten thousand person-hours, while large monuments such as Stonehenge and Silbury Hill required millions, perhaps tens of millions. These calculations are highly approximate, but their order of magnitude speaks for itself.

Was this wasteful expenditure? Recall our test: resources were consumed, yet productivity and welfare did not increase. A megalith does not plow, feed, or provide warmth. The pigs eaten at the winter feasts at Durrington Walls could have become reserves for a hungry year. The millions of hours devoted to moving stones could have gone into clearing fields, improving drainage, or building better homes. In that sense, the answer appears obvious.

Yet honesty requires us to acknowledge that almost every great Neolithic monument is an ambiguous case. First, it served as a gathering place. People from scattered farmsteads across the surrounding country converged there once a year, exchanging goods, livestock, brides, and news, and settling disputes. If we recall the fifth criterion in our framework—coordination and trust costs—shared labor and a shared feast built trust among people who otherwise scarcely saw one another. Second, judging by their alignment with solstice sunrises and sunsets, many structures served as calendars, and for a farmer an accurate calendar meant knowing when to sow. Third, as we saw at Göbekli Tepe, the organization of large projects was itself a school: people learned to assemble, feed, and direct hundreds of workers. That skill would later be needed for irrigation canals and city walls.

There is another important consideration that is often overlooked. As discussed in the section on the organization of labor, agricultural work is uneven. Between harvest and sowing come long weeks when there is almost nothing to do in the fields. If people hauled stones during precisely those periods, the cost of moving them was lower than it appears: those hours would not have turned into grain anyway. Economists call this opportunity cost—the value of the best alternative forgone in favor of the chosen activity. When idle labor has no useful outlet, even a relatively unproductive use of it costs society little. We will need this idea at the end of the book: wasteful expenditure becomes more expensive as the useful alternatives it displaces increase.

Still, the megaliths cannot be called “free.” Feasts consumed reserves, and in the Neolithic reserves were a thin barrier between an ordinary year and famine. In 2012, British archaeologists Chris Stevens and Dorian Fuller proposed that from roughly the middle of the fourth millennium BCE, cereal cultivation in the British Isles declined markedly, while people largely returned to pastoralism and foraging. Their hypothesis is disputed, but if it is correct, Britain's most ambitious monuments were built by societies undergoing not an agricultural flowering but an agricultural retreat. The pattern is familiar from later history: the less confidence a society has in tomorrow, the more effort it expends on symbols of unity and bargaining with higher powers.

If the case of megaliths is ambiguous, other forms of expenditure leave almost no ambiguity. These were objects made to be destroyed or hidden forever. We have already mentioned the gold of Varna that disappeared into graves. In Denmark and northern Germany, farmers of the Funnelbeaker culture, who lived around 4000–3000 BCE, submerged hundreds of beautifully polished flint axes in bogs, many of them never used. The Alpine jadeite axes discussed in the section on exchange took months to make—the hard stone was polished away literally millimeter by millimeter—and they too were often buried or thrown into water. Here surplus was converted not into a communal celebration, a calendar, or trust, but into a display: look, we are so rich that we can throw this away. In 1899, American sociologist Thorstein Veblen would call this behavior “conspicuous consumption.” The Neolithic appears to have invented it long before the term itself.

Who paid also matters. A communal feast and a communal monument were costs shared by many, and many received at least some benefit from them: food, an encounter with others, a sense of belonging. A grave filled with gold was an expenditure whose benefits went to a narrow circle, while its costs were ultimately borne by everyone whose labor extracted and exchanged the metal. The boundary between ambiguous and wasteful expenditure lies precisely here: while surplus is spent collectively and for a common purpose, it binds society together even without direct utility; when it is spent to elevate a few, it begins to tear society apart.

Where, then, did the wasted share of the Neolithic surplus go? Into stones raised beyond the needs of a calendar and gathering place. Into axes sunk in bogs. Into gold buried with its owners. The scale was modest for now: even Stonehenge, spread across centuries and divided among thousands of people, did not cost each generation very much. But the mechanism already existed. Several thousand years later, the same skills—assembling workers, feeding them from communal reserves, and directing them toward a structure that proclaimed greatness—would be placed at the service of pharaohs. Then the question “What did this monument cost the people who built it?” would resound at full force.

## The Lesson of the Era

The Neolithic Revolution lasted several thousand years and unfolded so slowly that none of its participants could have noticed it. The woman of Abu Hureyra who ground grain on her knees did not know that she was living “in a revolution”; she knew only that her mother and grandmother had lived the same way. Yet by the end of the era, the world had changed: in place of tens of thousands of nomadic bands stood villages, fields, herds, granaries, the first chiefs, and the first wealthy graves. What does this transformation tell us about the question running through the book? We can condense the answer into five propositions.

**First: surplus does not by itself improve life.** Agriculture produced more food from each hectare than hunting ever could—yet in most regions, the bones of early farmers reveal shorter stature, worse teeth, frequent signs of hungry years, and severe wear on the joints. There was an increase, but it went not into feeding each person better, but into the number of people: more children, more villages, more mouths on the same land. This is the Malthusian trap discussed in the introduction, in action. It gives us the first measure for every chapter that follows: ask not only “How much more did they produce?” but also “What did the majority receive?”

**Second: the most durable capital is created without an author.** Wheat, rice, maize, cattle, goats, and flax resulted from thousands of years of imperceptible selection by millions of people who received neither fame nor rent in return. This was knowledge encoded in living organisms and carried freely from village to village with migrants and seeds. No monument of the era brought posterity even a thousandth of the benefit provided by a single nonshattering ear of grain. What drove development in the Neolithic was above all distributed, open work that belonged to no one.

**Third: reserves that cannot be carried away create power—but do not predetermine its form.** A cleared field, a house, and a full granary cannot be taken along, and so the foragers' principal defense against arbitrary power—the freedom to leave—disappeared. Inheritance and inequality emerged, as did ancestors beneath the floor as an argument for land rights, followed by graves like those at Varna. Yet Çatalhöyük, where neither a palace nor an elite quarter arose over centuries, shows that the path from reserves to chiefs was not automatic. Societies made choices—and, as Graeber and Wengrow insist, sometimes chose differently. We will continue this debate in every chapter: the mere possibility of extraction does not mean extraction is inevitable.

**Fourth: growth has costs that no one chose.** Measles and other diseases inherited from livestock, the exhausted soils around Ain Ghazal, and crop failure striking a society that had staked everything on two or three cereals were not anyone's evil decision. They were side effects of the same changes that produced growth. Destruction without a culprit is dangerous because it is difficult to notice and there is no one to hold to account. We will encounter this pattern again—in the salinized fields of Sumer, the coal smog of London, and climate change.

**Fifth: the boundary between communal and wasteful expenditure is determined by the question “Who pays, and who benefits?”** Stonehenge and Neolithic feasts consumed labor and reserves, but they also united scattered farmsteads into a society, and the stones may have been hauled during weeks when there was no work in the fields. Gold buried with its owner and axes sunk in bogs are different: many bore the cost, while only a few benefited—or no one did. While surplus is spent collectively and for a common purpose, it can bind society together even without direct utility; when it is spent to elevate a few, it begins to tear society apart.

Now we can answer the book's recurring question. Where did the Neolithic surplus go? Most of it went into population growth: agriculture fed not better, but more. A significant share went into development: fields, herds, seeds, houses, pottery, accounting tokens, and knowledge that still serves humanity today. A substantial portion covered the costs of the new way of life itself: disease, depleted land, failed harvests, and raids. And a share still modest but growing went into prestige: megaliths, competitive feasts, and grave goods. Compared with the first chapter, the main difference lies not in the size of the wasted share, but in the first appearance of a mechanism capable of enlarging it: reserves that could be concentrated in a few hands, and people able to control them.

We should also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. The Neolithic was an era without writing; everything we know about people's intentions has been reconstructed from bones, grains, house plans, and DNA. Many estimates—the populations of settlements, the role of climate in their decline, the causes of violence—remain disputed, and new excavations and genetic research will revise them more than once. But the central framework is robust: for the first time, humanity acquired a durable surplus—and immediately confronted the question of whom and what to give it to. In the next chapter, scribes, priests, and kings will begin answering that question, and for the first time the answer will be written in clay.

## The Numbers

The figures for the Neolithic are somewhat more reliable than those for the age of hunters: villages left behind houses, granaries, and cemeteries, and therefore bones that can be measured. But writing still did not exist, and everything below is an estimate—an order of magnitude, not exact data.

**Population.** Around 10,000 BCE, according to a widely used estimate by historical demographers Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones, about four million people lived on Earth. By 5000 BCE, various compilations put the figure between five and twenty million, and by 3000 BCE, around fifteen to thirty million. Converted into annual terms, growth remained minuscule—roughly one or two hundredths of a percent—but for the first time it was sustained. French demographer Jean-Pierre Bocquet-Appel studied dozens of Neolithic cemeteries and showed that soon after the transition to agriculture, the proportion of child and adolescent skeletons rose sharply: women were giving birth more often. By his calculations, the interval between births fell from three or four years among mobile foragers to roughly two or three among sedentary farmers.

**Density.** Hunter-gatherers in temperate climates generally needed several square kilometers per person; an agricultural village fed tens or hundreds of people from the same area. According to estimates by Jared Diamond and Vaclav Smil, the difference ranged from tenfold to a hundredfold depending on the region. Hence the size of settlements: Jericho in the ninth and eighth millennia BCE probably held anywhere from several hundred to two or three thousand residents, while Ian Hodder estimates that Çatalhöyük at its height held between 3,500 and 8,000.

**Health and stature.** The survey assembled by paleopathologists Mark Cohen and George Armelagos (1984), together with later reviews, reveals a similar pattern in many regions: early farmers had more cavities, signs of anemia, disruptions in tooth-enamel growth caused by hunger, and joint disease than their predecessors. Average adult height fell by several centimeters in most of the regions studied. For Greece and Anatolia, anthropologist Lawrence Angel estimated a decline of seven to twelve centimeters among men, though from very small samples; Jared Diamond popularized an even higher figure of about fifteen centimeters. These numbers remain disputed and subject to revision, but most researchers accept the overall direction.

**Life expectancy.** By most estimates, life expectancy at birth remained around its previous level—roughly twenty to thirty years—or even declined slightly. Infant mortality continued to pull the average down, while new diseases and crowded living beside livestock and refuse added their effects.

**Labor.** In 2011, economist Samuel Bowles calculated that early cereal cultivation yielded no more—and probably fewer—calories per hour of labor than foraging. Agriculture's advantage lay not in hourly productivity, but in the amount of food obtained per hectare: people had to work longer, but the land fed more of them.

**Income.** As among hunters, per capita income is estimated at subsistence level. Surplus existed, but scarcely increased per person: it turned into new people rather than better lives.

**Inequality.** In 2017, a team led by archaeologist Timothy Kohler compared dwelling sizes in dozens of ancient settlements. Their calculations suggest that inequality increased everywhere after the transition to agriculture, but substantially more in the Old World than in the Americas: several thousand years after domestication, the Gini coefficient—a measure of inequality ranging from zero, where everyone is equal, to one, where a single person owns everything—based on house sizes reached roughly 0.6 in Eurasia. It is tempting to compare this with income inequality in modern countries, but the measures are different, and the authors themselves warn against doing so. They link the pattern to large livestock and the plow, which made it possible to accumulate wealth and pass it down through inheritance.

**Inheritance.** One figure has survived the entire era: according to the FAO, rice, wheat, and maize—three cereals domesticated during the Neolithic in different parts of the world—still provide humanity with between two-fifths and nearly two-thirds of all calories consumed today (the range depends on whether one counts direct consumption alone or includes animal feed). No palace and no tomb produces such a return ten thousand years later.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

As in the previous chapter, everything below is the author's estimate with wide margins of error: there are no written reports from Neolithic construction projects, and every link in the chain rests on assumptions made by archaeologists and by me.

First, the price of a sack. A Neolithic farmer worked with a hoe and digging stick, without a plow or draft animals, and Samuel Bowles, as we saw in “The Numbers,” argues that early cereals yielded no more calories per hour of labor than foraging. The range used in the introduction for plow agriculture—fifteen to forty hours per sack—is therefore too optimistic here. I assume forty to one hundred hours per sack, including harvesting, threshing, grinding, and storage: somewhere between the foragers of the previous chapter and the plow farmers of those to come. A working life remains roughly seventy thousand hours.

### Silbury Hill

**The object.** A human-made mound about thirty meters high, raised around 2400 BCE near Avebury. It contains neither a burial nor treasure, and no calendrical function has been demonstrated. Of all the monuments in this chapter, it comes closest to pure wasteful expenditure, so I use it here.

**Labor.** Richard Atkinson, who excavated the hill in 1968–1970, estimated the work at roughly eighteen million person-hours—as if five hundred people had labored for fifteen years. The estimate rests on the mound's volume, around a quarter of a million cubic meters of chalk and earth, and an assumption about how much soil one person could move in an hour with an antler pick and basket. The estimate is old and may be too high or too low, so I use a range of ten to twenty million hours. At ten hours a day, that is one to two million person-days.

**Grain.** The builders probably worked in the agricultural off-season and fed themselves during the rest of the year. I therefore count only working days: at one kilogram of grain per day, one to two million kilograms, or twenty to forty thousand sacks.

**Hours.** At forty to one hundred hours per sack, that is between eight hundred thousand and four million hours of peasant labor.

**Lives.** Adding the builders' own hours gives a total of eleven to twenty-four million hours, or 150 to 350 working lives; the midpoint of the range is about 250. This is already the same order of magnitude as the hypothetical tomb in the introduction, at roughly four hundred lives. In the previous chapter, the largest expenditure was measured in single lives; here it is measured in hundreds.

For comparison, according to old estimates by Renfrew and his colleagues, Stonehenge with all its reconstructions required tens of millions of hours, but these were spread across more than a thousand years. Silbury was raised much more quickly—according to modern dating, probably over several generations—and so its cost bore more heavily on the people then alive.

**The alternative.** Twenty to forty thousand sacks would have sustained a village of three hundred people—children, adults, and the elderly—for ten to twenty years without a single harvest; or, more importantly for the Neolithic, carried it through several consecutive years of famine. And ten to twenty million hours of labor could have gone into forest clearance. In a well-known experiment by Danish paleobotanist Johannes Iversen in the 1950s, three people with flint axes cleared around five hundred square meters of birch woodland in four hours. If we assume that a hectare of true mixed forest with oaks required several hundred to a thousand person-hours, the hours spent on Silbury could have cleared ten to sixty thousand hectares of new arable land. This is a very rough conversion, but the scale matters: enough fields for dozens of villages, at precisely the time when, according to Stevens and Fuller's hypothesis, agriculture in the islands was in retreat.

In fairness, if the mound was indeed raised during the dead season, the opportunity cost of the builders' own hours was lower—you cannot plow a field in winter. But forest clearance is precisely a winter task, and the grain consumed at the site vanished from reserves regardless of the season.

### What Cannot Be Counted

The gold of Varna, jadeite axes sunk in bogs, and the feasts at Durrington Walls are expenditures one would like to count as well. But for them, not even rough estimates of labor exist: we do not know how many hours went into mining and working the metal, how many people attended the feasts, or how many pigs a season cost. I will not invent figures. It is clear only that they were probably smaller in scale than the Silbury mound, while their benefits went to a narrower circle and were even further removed from utility.

**The result.** The granary transformed wasteful expenditure from fractions of a life into hundreds of lives. The mechanism was the same as among hunters—only the reserve was larger, and there was more of it to spend.
