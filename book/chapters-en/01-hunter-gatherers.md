# Chapter 1. Hunter-Gatherers

## Portrait of the Era

An evening in the Kalahari Desert in the early 1960s. Several dozen people—men, women, children, and elders—sit around a fire. They are Ju/’hoansi (called Bushmen or !Kung in older literature), and beside them sits a young Canadian anthropologist, Richard Lee, notebook in hand. Lee is doing something unusual for an anthropologist: he is counting. How many hours a week people spend looking for food, how many calories each expedition brings back, who eats what was gathered or killed, and who does not. He expected to find a desperate struggle for survival. What he saw was different: adults spent an average of two or three days a week procuring food, while the rest of their time went to domestic tasks, visiting neighbors, conversation, games, dancing, and sleep. Food—mongongo nuts, roots, berries, and occasionally antelope meat—was shared among everyone, including those who had gone nowhere that day.

We will return to this scene, its figures, and the disputes surrounding them: Lee’s findings have serious critics. But it illustrates where our discussion must begin. No money, no bosses, no warehouses. And yet there is an economy: people produce and distribute, decide who gets how much, and determine how to compensate for the risk of an unsuccessful hunt. They simply make these decisions in ways very different from ours.

Why begin with them? Because this is not a side branch of history but almost its entire length. The oldest known remains of our species, found at Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, date back roughly 300,000 years. Agriculture appeared about 12,000 years ago. This means that for more than 95 percent of our species’ existence, every human being lived by hunting, fishing, and gathering. Compress human history into a single day, and farmers appear less than an hour before midnight; the steam engine, about a minute and a half before it. Our bodies, our instincts for fairness and envy, our love of gossip and gifts—all were shaped there, around the fire, long before the first field was planted.

What was the scale of this world? It was unimaginably empty. Estimates of the human population before the advent of agriculture, around 10,000 BCE, vary by a factor of several—from one or two million to ten million people across the entire planet; four to five million is the most common estimate. That is fewer than the population of modern-day St. Petersburg, scattered across every continent except Antarctica. The ordinary unit of life was a small mobile group—what anthropologists call a band—of twenty to fifty people, made up of several related families. Bands formed broader networks of several hundred people who spoke the same language, exchanged spouses, gifts, and news, and occasionally gathered at shared camps. In a desert or tundra, population density could be as low as one person per dozens of square kilometers. For most of their lives, people saw the same thirty or forty faces.

Over those millennia, this sparsely numbered species accomplished what no other large animal ever had. Around 60,000–70,000 years ago, modern humans began a large-scale migration out of Africa. Roughly 50,000–65,000 years ago—the dates remain disputed—they crossed sea channels and reached Australia, which means they could build watercraft and plan a journey toward an unseen shore. Later they settled Ice Age Eurasia all the way to the Arctic, where no one could survive even a single winter without sewn clothing, shelter, and fire. Humans reached the Americas no later than 15,000–16,000 years ago, and perhaps earlier, then made their way to Tierra del Fuego within a few thousand years. It was the greatest expansion in history, accomplished by people without the wheel, metal, writing, or the state.

They had other things. They had fire, which we will discuss in the next section. They had tools made from stone, bone, wood, and antler, tools that gradually grew more refined: from hand axes that changed little over hundreds of thousands of years to composite spearpoints, eyed needles, spear-throwers, and, later, the bow. They had art: the paintings in Chauvet Cave in France were made around 36,000 years ago, those at Lascaux around 17,000 years ago. They had shell ornaments that passed from hand to hand across hundreds of kilometers. And they had language—the most powerful tool for cooperation ever invented, allowing people to agree on who would go where tomorrow and to pass on to their children knowledge accumulated by their great-grandparents.

Now for the central question of this book: where did the surplus go? The short answer is that there was almost none, and what little existed could not be held onto. A mobile band carries all its possessions on its back. A woman carrying a child cannot also carry a winter’s supply of grain, while antelope meat spoils within days in the heat. Accumulation in the sense familiar to us was physically impossible—and, as we will see, socially undesirable. A successful hunter who tried to keep his kill for himself would quickly discover that no one shared with him during the next lean season.

But “no surplus in the granary” does not mean “no surplus at all.” It existed in other forms: as free time, as knowledge, and as a network of obligations. Time not spent finding food went into raising children, telling stories, performing rituals, making tools and ornaments, and visiting distant relatives. Knowledge—where to find water in a drought, which roots were poisonous, how a wounded animal behaved—was the group’s principal capital, stored not in warehouses but in the minds of its elders. And debts of reciprocity—“I shared with you last time”—provided better insurance than any storehouse. These three forms—time, knowledge, and trust—are what we will trace through the rest of this chapter.

It is important not to fall into either of two extremes. The first is to imagine hunter-gatherers as perpetually starving savages whose lives, in philosopher Thomas Hobbes’s famous phrase, were “nasty, brutish, and short.” The second is to see in them a lost paradise of equality and leisure. The truth is more complicated and more interesting. Child mortality was appalling, violence between groups was not uncommon, and a drought could wipe out an entire band. Yet this world had its own solutions to problems humanity would later try to solve all over again—the problems of distribution, insurance, power, and free time. We have lost some of those solutions. Others were abandoned for good reason.

## Energy and Technology

Every economy is ultimately a way of obtaining energy and turning it into things people need. A hunter-gatherer had one principal source of energy: human muscles fueled by food. An adult needs roughly 2,000–3,000 kilocalories a day. Almost everything a person does—walking, digging, carrying, throwing a spear—is paid for from that budget. To survive, every expedition for food must bring back more calories than it costs. If hunting yields less energy on average than the hunter burns, the group slowly fades away, however bravely it may labor. This is the harshest accounting system in history, and every technology of the era was a way of shifting its balance in humanity’s favor.

The first and most important shift was fire. Scholars still debate when human ancestors learned to control it. Traces of hearths in Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa are around a million years old, dating back to the age of Homo erectus. Regular, everyday use of fire can be firmly traced at sites roughly 300,000–400,000 years old. One way or another, our species was born with fire already in its hands.

Fire was history’s first external source of energy: energy humans could use without burning it inside their own bodies. It provided warmth and therefore made it possible to live where survival would otherwise have been impossible. It drove off predators and extended the day: around a fire, people could work, teach, and tell stories after sunset. Fire was used to burn grass and flush out game. Most importantly, it was used for cooking. In his book *Catching Fire*, British primatologist Richard Wrangham proposed that cooking was what made us human. Cooked food is far easier to digest than raw food: heat does part of the stomach’s work by breaking down fibers and starches and neutralizing many toxins and parasites. Chimpanzees spend around six hours a day chewing raw food; humans spend less than one. According to Wrangham, the energy and time this released went into the growth of our large and voracious brain. The hypothesis is disputed—some archaeologists believe the brain began growing before fire came into everyday use. But its central point matters for this book: the first energy revolution produced a surplus not in the form of things, but in the form of time and brainpower.

The second line of technological development was tools. The oldest known stone tools predate our species by millions of years: Oldowan flakes and choppers appeared around 2.6 million years ago, while finds from the Lomekwi site in Kenya may be older still. Then came Acheulean hand axes—teardrop-shaped stones worked on both sides. What is remarkable is how slowly they changed: the same basic design was made for more than a million years, from Africa to India. By comparison, in the past thousand years humanity has gone from the watermill to the smartphone. The hand axe is mute testimony to how difficult it was to accumulate knowledge when it was stored in only a few dozen minds within a small group and every master’s death could erase a skill forever.

Over roughly the past hundred thousand years, the pace changed. Composite tools appeared, with a stone point fastened to a wooden shaft using resin and sinew—a complex object made of several materials and requiring a sequence of operations planned in advance. Around 40,000 years ago came eyed bone needles, and with them sewn clothing, without which Ice Age Siberia could never have been settled. The spear-thrower, a simple stick with a hook, extended the arm and allowed a dart to be cast considerably farther and harder. Then came the bow. The earliest proposed arrowheads from Sibudu Cave in South Africa are around 60,000 years old, though that interpretation is disputed; the undisputed wooden arrows found in the Stellmoor bog near Hamburg are about 12,000 years old. Nets, fishhooks, boats, traps, and microliths appeared—tiny standardized stone inserts that could easily be replaced when one broke. These were no longer merely tools, but interchangeable parts.

All these inventions did the same thing: they increased the return on every calorie invested in work. A bow let hunters take game from a safe distance and return empty-handed less often. Nets and traps worked while their owners slept—the first “machines” in history to labor without a person’s constant presence. A stone fish trap was already genuine capital: something made once that continued to yield benefits for years. The best-known example is the Budj Bim system of channels and weirs for trapping eels in southeastern Australia, built by the Gunditjmara people and estimated to have existed for 6,000–7,000 years; today it is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Where nature provided such a dependable source of food, hunter-gatherers became sedentary and began to build—a development we will discuss in the next chapter.

Fire was also a tool for managing the landscape. For millennia, Aboriginal Australians deliberately burned patches of grass at chosen times. This produced young shoots that attracted kangaroos, cleared routes through the land, and, in the view of many researchers, reduced the risk of large uncontrolled fires. Australian archaeologist Rhys Jones called the practice “fire-stick farming.” It is a reminder that the boundary between “gatherer” and “producer” is blurred: long before the first field was planted, people were already reshaping nature to suit their needs.

Where, then, was the surplus? The technologies of the era produced gains in two forms. The first was time: the greater the return on each hour spent searching for food, the more hours remained for everything else. The second was access to new places: every successful invention expanded the boundaries of where humans could live. Over tens of thousands of years, almost the entire gain in productivity went not into increased consumption per person, but into a larger population and its spread across the planet. This is our first encounter with the pattern economists would call Malthusian, after the English cleric and economist Thomas Malthus: while food remains the primary constraint, every improvement produces more mouths, not more bread for each mouth. Within the framework of this book, almost everything described above is pure development: fire, the needle, the bow, and the trap make an hour of labor more productive without taking anything from anyone else. But development had a dark side. The better weapons became, and the more hunters entered new lands, the faster the large animals on which this entire economy depended disappeared. We will return to this in the section on what caused destruction.

## Organization of Labor

Morning in a Hadza camp by Lake Eyasi in Tanzania, among one of the last peoples who still live partly by hunting and gathering. The men leave alone or in pairs, carrying bows into the bush to ambush antelope at a watering hole or find a bees’ nest and smoke it out. The women go out together, taking children and digging sticks to gather tubers, berries, and baobab fruit. An older woman digs alongside everyone else, though she is past sixty—and, according to anthropologists’ observations, no less effectively than the young women. Adolescents gather berries closer to camp and eat them on the spot. In the evening, everything brought back is distributed among the hearths according to rules no one has written down but everyone knows.

This is the division of labor in its oldest form: by sex and age. There are no professions in our sense of the word—no potters, blacksmiths, or scribes. Every adult knows nearly everything required for survival, and everyone is at once a worker, a consumer, and a keeper of knowledge. Yet their roles are not entirely equal. Within this simple arrangement, we can already see all the fundamental questions of economics: who does what, who takes the risks, who feeds whom, and who teaches whom.

Let us begin with sex. In most hunter-gatherer societies known to ethnographers, men primarily hunted large game, while women primarily gathered plant foods, small animals, shellfish, and fuel. Many explanations have been proposed, and they are not mutually exclusive: pregnancy and breastfeeding are poorly suited to tracking an animal for days, while gathering can be done with a child in one’s arms. But this should not be turned into a rigid law. Among the Agta of the Philippines, women regularly hunted with dogs and knives, as American anthropologists Agnes Estioko-Griffin and P. Bion Griffin documented in the 1980s. In 2018, archaeologists excavated—and in 2020 described—a roughly 9,000-year-old burial at Wilamaya Patjxa in the Peruvian Andes: a young woman buried with a set of hunting tools. The research team led by Randall Haas concluded that between one-third and one-half of hunters in the ancient Americas may have been women; many of their colleagues consider that inference from a handful of burials too bold. How often women hunted in the past is now fiercely debated. One thing is clear: roles were customary rather than forbidden, and they changed flexibly with circumstances.

More interesting is the different risk profile of men’s and women’s work. Women’s gathering produces a little, but produces it almost every time. Hunting large animals is a lottery: a week of empty-handed outings, then hundreds of kilograms of meat all at once. American anthropologist Kristen Hawkes and her colleagues, who studied the Hadza, showed that a Hadza hunter kills large game on only a small share of hunting days. If a family depended solely on the husband’s hunting, it would starve. Women often provided the reliable, everyday foundation of the diet—in arid regions, sometimes more than half of all calories. Meat was a feast, a source of protein and fat, something distributed to the whole community—and, as we will see in the next section, a source of prestige.

Why, then, did men not abandon risky hunting for dependable gathering? Hawkes proposed that hunting is partly a “display”: a successful hunter gains not so much meat for his own family—it will be shared with everyone anyway—as respect, allies, and attractiveness in the eyes of women. Other researchers, including Hillard Kaplan and Kim Hill, who worked with the Aché in Paraguay, regard hunting as an entirely rational contribution to the common pool: meat divided among everyone provides insurance for everyone. The debate is unresolved. But it raises a question that will run throughout this book: how much labor serves the common good, and how much serves the worker’s status? Even around the hunters’ fire, the boundary between the two is not obvious.

Now consider age. In a well-known 2000 study, Kaplan and his coauthors combined data from several hunter-gatherer societies and produced a striking curve. Children and adolescents consume more calories than they obtain until almost the age of twenty. Adult production rises very slowly, peaking only around age thirty or forty—and later still among hunters. Why so late? Because hunting and gathering are not muscular labor but knowledge work. One must know hundreds of plants, read tracks, understand animal behavior, and remember where water could be found three droughts ago. A young, strong hunter is outperformed by one who is experienced and no longer quite as fast. Humans are the only primates with such a long childhood, and in Kaplan’s view, childhood is an investment: years in which society feeds a child on credit in order to produce a worker who knows more than could be learned in a chimpanzee’s shorter life.

This is why the transmission of knowledge was the principal capital of the era. In a world without writing, the only storehouse of information was human memory, and the only way to copy it was through teaching: demonstrate, explain, take a child along on the hunt, tell a story by the fire. Myths and songs were not merely entertainment: they encoded maps, information about the seasons, and rules of conduct. Researchers studying Aboriginal Australians describe “songlines”—long cycles of songs that enumerate landmarks and water sources along routes through the desert. Here, a story was a navigation system.

And here appears a figure unknown among other primates: the grandmother. Kristen Hawkes proposed the “grandmother hypothesis.” Hadza women do not stop working after their childbearing years. On the contrary, they gather extensively and feed their grandchildren, allowing their daughters to have another child sooner. This may be why human women, unlike chimpanzees, live for many years after the end of fertility. Like most ideas in this field, the hypothesis is disputed. But the economic meaning is clear: elders are both additional hands and living libraries. When a group lost its oldest members prematurely, it lost not only workers but part of its knowledge. In this world, the death of a master was literally the destruction of capital.

How much did these people work? Here we return to Richard Lee and the calculations from the beginning of the chapter. In 1966, the “Man the Hunter” conference was held in Chicago, where Lee and other anthropologists presented their figures. American anthropologist Marshall Sahlins developed them into the essay “The Original Affluent Society,” later included in his book *Stone Age Economics* (1972). Sahlins’s argument was audacious: affluence can be achieved in two ways—by producing a great deal or by desiring little. Hunter-gatherers chose the second path. They did not need many possessions because they would have had to carry them. Instead, they had leisure that a modern office worker might envy.

Serious criticism came quickly. First, the early estimates counted only the time spent obtaining food. But nuts still had to be cracked, roots cleaned, meat butchered, water carried, firewood gathered, and tools repaired. When Lee later included this work, his estimate rose to roughly 40–45 hours a week—not so far from our own working week, though the rhythm was less intense. Studies of other peoples, such as the Aché or the Hiwi in Venezuela, produced even higher figures. Second, the Ju/’hoansi of the 1960s did not live in isolation or in the “Stone Age”: they lived alongside pastoralists, and the season in which they were observed may have been unusually favorable. Third, free time is not always a choice. In extreme heat or during a hungry season, people also rest because extra effort burns calories they do not have.

What remains of Sahlins’s thesis after these criticisms? Not paradise, and not a “society of leisure.” But an important observation remains: with simple tools and modest needs, the ceiling on useful work was reached quickly, and surplus time was not converted into surplus goods—it became socializing, ritual, child-rearing, and sleep. No one worked beyond what was necessary to feed someone else who did no work at all. There were no tribute collectors, no priests supported by the community, no armies. The share of surplus consumed by extraction and prestige was close to zero—not because people were uniquely virtuous, but because there was almost nothing to extract and no one to extract it. When people begin to work more in the chapters ahead, it will be important to ask: for whom, exactly, and to what end?

## Exchange and Money

Back to the Kalahari, and back to Richard Lee. One Christmas during his fieldwork in the early 1960s, he decided to thank the Ju/’hoansi among whom he was living and bought the largest, fattest ox he could find from neighboring pastoralists. Their reaction stunned him. One by one, people came to him and said sympathetically that the ox was thin, nothing but skin and bone; there would not be enough meat for everyone; and he had clearly been sold a worthless animal. The ox proved excellent, and the feast was a success. Only later was the custom explained to Lee: this was how any large kill was received. If a hunter returned with a large animal, he was expected to say modestly that he had caught nothing, or hardly anything, while everyone else mocked and belittled his meat. Otherwise, one man told Lee, the hunter would begin to think of himself as a chief and everyone else as his servants, and one day his pride would kill somebody. Lee told the story in a short essay with the ironic title “Eating Christmas in the Kalahari.”

That scene contains the entire exchange economy of hunter-gatherers. There is no buying or selling and no money, but there are strict rules: what must be shared, with whom, in what order—and how to prevent sharing from becoming power.

Begin with the central rule: large game is shared. Among many peoples, an animal belongs not to the person who killed it but, for example, to the owner of the arrow that struck it. Arrows are constantly lent and given away, so the “owner” of a carcass is often someone who did not join the hunt at all. That person distributes the meat according to kinship and previous obligations, after which every family that receives a share distributes some in turn. Within a few days, a piece of the same animal has reached every hearth in the camp. The custom of using someone else’s arrow is no accident: it separates the kill from the hunter and thereby denies a successful hunter the right to control it.

Why? The first reason is the insurance we have already discussed. Meat spoils, and luck is fickle. It benefits a hunter to give away today’s surplus, which he could not eat anyway, in exchange for a claim to a share tomorrow when his own hunt fails. There is no warehouse, but there is a “bank” that stores other people’s obligations. The second reason is less appealing to romantics. Australian anthropologist Nicolas Peterson called this practice “demand sharing”: people often do not so much give generously as yield to requests that are difficult to refuse. Refusal means conflict, gossip, and isolation. Sharing rests not only on kindness but on constant mutual pressure—on the ability to ask and the inability to say no.

This also explains something that initially seems strange: among many such peoples, thanking someone for food is almost unknown. To thank a person for a share of meat is to admit that they have done you a favor and that you now owe them. Here, receiving a share is not a kindness but a right. Danish explorer Peter Freuchen, who lived for many years among the Inuit of Greenland, recalled a hunter rebuking him for expressing thanks for meat: here, people help one another, while gifts make slaves just as whips make dogs. The scene became widely known after David Graeber retold it in *Debt*. The story may have been embellished, but its point is exact and has been confirmed repeatedly by ethnographers: debt is the seed of power, and a society of equals tries to prevent it from accumulating.

There was another kind of exchange, more like what we would recognize as gift-giving. Among the Ju/’hoansi, it is called hxaro. American anthropologist Polly Wiessner, who has studied it since the 1970s, described how people exchange ostrich-eggshell beads, arrowheads, and blankets over periods of years—not immediately, but after delays of months or years and without precise accounting of “value.” Hxaro is not really about objects. Each pair of partners is a relationship maintained through gifts, and the partners live in different places, sometimes a hundred or two hundred kilometers apart. When drought strikes your country, you go to your partner, who is obliged to receive you and give you access to water. By Wiessner’s calculations, this network gave people access to territory many times larger than their own. The beads were not money but a kind of receipt for hospitality—an insurance policy against local catastrophe.

In *The Gift* (1925), French sociologist Marcel Mauss described such an economy as a system of three obligations: to give, to receive, and to reciprocate. A gift is not free—it binds people together. That is both its strength and its weakness. The strength is that it creates trust and mutual aid where there are no courts or police. The weakness is that it works only among people who know one another. You cannot build a gift relationship with a stranger you will never see again.

Here we should dispel one remarkably persistent fiction. In *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), Adam Smith imagined that early humans exchanged directly—arrows for game, hides for fish—and that money arose to free them from the inconvenience of such barter. The picture has migrated from textbook to textbook. The problem is that anthropologists have never found a society in which barter was the principal form of exchange among neighbors. In 1985, Cambridge anthropologist Caroline Humphrey observed that no pure barter economy had ever been described; in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, David Graeber made this point a major line of argument. Within a group, people did not barter: they shared and gave. Barter existed, but between strangers, at boundaries, often amid distrust and with weapons at the ready. Where, then, did money come from? Graeber and a number of other anthropologists, drawing on the old credit theory of money, argue that it grew more from the accounting of debts and tribute than from exchanges of arrows for hides. This interpretation is itself disputed: many historians of money believe that debt accounting and market exchange developed in parallel and reinforced one another. We will return to the debate in Chapters 3 and 4.

Objects nevertheless traveled astonishing distances. Around 300,000 years ago, the inhabitants of the Olorgesailie Basin in Kenya used obsidian—a black volcanic glass that produces exceptionally sharp blades—from deposits 25–95 kilometers away from the site, according to a 2018 report by a team led by American paleoanthropologist Alison Brooks. That is far beyond an ordinary day’s journey. Perforated seashells, clearly worn as beads, have been found at Blombos Cave in South Africa, dating to around 75,000 years ago, and at Grotte des Pigeons in Morocco, dating to around 80,000 years ago; the Moroccan shells lay dozens of kilometers from the sea. In Ice Age Europe, shells from the Mediterranean and Atlantic traveled hundreds of kilometers inland. This was probably not trade conducted by caravans and merchants: objects passed from hand to hand along chains of gifts, from group to group, like hxaro beads.

Why does this matter to our argument? Because exchange of this kind is development in its purest form. It takes nothing away: each side receives something it lacks. Good obsidian from a distant deposit makes every cut of a knife more effective. And along the same networks as the objects traveled information, skills, and marriage partners. The wider the network, the more minds participate in the common preservation of knowledge and the smaller the chance that an invention dies with its maker. Some archaeologists explain puzzling losses of technology precisely as the result of broken networks. When Tasmania was separated from Australia by the sea 10,000–12,000 years ago, its small population appears over time to have lost certain skills, including the manufacture of bone tools. The interpretation is disputed, but the logic is clear: a small isolated population loses knowledge faster than it can create it.

So where did the surplus go in this exchange system? Almost entirely into insurance and connections. A successful hunt became not the wealth of one family but obligations of gratitude spread throughout the camp. Rare stone and beautiful shells became a network of friends extending across hundreds of kilometers. This was an economy in which accumulation was both impossible and improper, and the highest-value asset was something no sack could carry: the right to approach another person and receive help. The price of this system was constant dependence on the opinions of others, the pressure to “give,” and the difficulty of moving beyond the circle of familiar people. Its achievement was the near-total absence of extraction. Within a few thousand years, granaries would appear, and the question “who owes whom” would become a question of power. But first, let us see how hunter-gatherer society prevented power from arising at all.

## Power and Institutions

The Ju/’hoansi had people whom neighboring pastoralists and early European observers called “chiefs.” Richard Lee asked one of his informants whether they had chiefs. According to Lee, the man laughed: of course they did—every one of them was his own chief. Formally, a group had an “owner” of its waterhole and the surrounding land, the person through whom agreements were made about who could live there. But he could not give orders. He collected no tribute, passed no judgments, and controlled no one else’s labor. If his advice seemed poor, people simply ignored it; if he grew self-important, they left to join relatives in another group.

How, then, were rules enforced? Every society, even one of thirty people, must settle disputes, punish violators, and prevent the strong from subjugating the weak. Hunter-gatherers possessed a set of mechanisms that American anthropologist Christopher Boehm called a “reverse dominance hierarchy” in his book *Hierarchy in the Forest* (1999). Among the great apes, our closest relatives, a powerful male subordinates the rest. In small human groups, the opposite occurred: the majority joined together to keep any would-be dominant individual in check. Boehm collected ethnographic descriptions of dozens of such societies and found the same escalating set of tools in each.

The first tool is ridicule. We have already seen it in the story of the Christmas ox: success is belittled and the braggart mocked. It is a cheap and gentle measure, and usually it is enough. The second is gossip and public opinion: greed and bossiness are discussed around every fire, and the offender’s reputation—and therefore his insurance against hunger—melts away. The third is refusal to cooperate: people stop sharing with him and stop taking him hunting. The fourth, and most important, is departure. Hunter-gatherer groups are fluid: families continually move between camps, joining the wife’s or husband’s relatives. If someone tries to command others, the simplest response is to leave him, until he has no one left to command. Economists would call this freedom of exit, and it may be the most powerful check on authority in history. It works as long as the dissatisfied have somewhere to go and do not have to abandon accumulated possessions—and there were almost no possessions to abandon.

Finally, in extreme cases, force was used. A person who repeatedly broke the rules, threatened others, and resisted both mockery and isolation could be expelled or killed—often with the tacit agreement of the entire group, sometimes by his own relatives. Lee recorded roughly twenty killings among the Ju/’hoansi in the decades before his arrival; by his account, some were precisely such collective executions of dangerous individuals. This is the unpleasant side of a “society of equals”: its equality was defended not only by kindness but by constant vigilance, and sometimes by blood.

Hunter-gatherers did have institutions, then—just not in the form of buildings, offices, and written laws. Their institutions were customs shared by everyone. An economist would say that enforcement was decentralized: there was no specialist who received a share of the common product in exchange for maintaining order. Everyone was part police officer, part judge, and part jury. This was very cheap; there was no apparatus to support. But the system had costs of its own: pressure on anyone who stood out, including through talent; difficulty making agreements with outsiders; and an inability to undertake large common projects requiring hundreds of people to maintain discipline over long periods.

We should not, however, imagine the era as 300,000 years of frozen equality. In *The Dawn of Everything* (2021), David Graeber and archaeologist David Wengrow argue that people in the past consciously experimented with arrangements of power. Their favorite examples are seasonal changes. Among a number of Indigenous peoples of the North American Great Plains, “buffalo police” were appointed during the great summer bison hunt and given real authority to punish anyone who frightened the herd too soon; after the hunt, that authority disappeared. In Marcel Mauss’s classic account, Inuit communities followed different property rules and social arrangements in winter and summer. Hierarchy, then, could be switched on and off for a specific task. Graeber and Wengrow’s book has drawn extensive criticism for its loose handling of evidence, but this idea is persuasive: power began as a tool that people knew how to put away again.

There were also hunter-gatherers with enduring inequality. On the Northwest Coast of North America, the Kwakiutl, Tlingit, and their neighbors lived in permanent villages supported by enormous salmon runs. Fish could be dried and stored; noble families held hereditary titles; and many peoples of the coast kept slaves captured in raids. Their famous potlatch feast, during which elites gave away—and sometimes ostentatiously destroyed—mountains of blankets and valuable copper plates, is one of history’s most vivid examples of wealth burned for status. Earlier still, around 34,000 years ago at Sungir near Vladimir, two children and an adult man were buried in clothing sewn with thousands of mammoth-ivory beads. American archaeologist Randall White reproduced the bead-making process and estimated that each bead required about an hour of work. One child’s grave alone contained more than ten thousand beads—more than ten thousand hours of labor. Whether these were a chief’s children who inherited status at birth or exceptional burials created for an exceptional occasion remains disputed. What is clear is that long before agriculture, people already knew how to elevate someone above the rest and invest immense labor in doing so.

What unites these exceptions? Almost everywhere that hunter-gatherers developed durable inequality, we find a resource nearby that could be stored and defended: dried salmon, rich fishing grounds, or sometimes abundant seasonal herds. Where wealth can be stored and a valuable place cannot be abandoned, the principal check on power—freedom of exit—grows weaker. Anyone who leaves forfeits a share of the stores and access to the river. This is the key to the next chapter. Reverse dominance hierarchy rested on poverty in possessions and the mobility of people; once there is a granary that cannot be carried away, future rulers have leverage.

The answer to our recurring question in this section is this: almost all the surplus associated with power went not toward supporting power but toward restraining it—into time spent gossiping, arguing, moving between camps, and maintaining constant mutual vigilance. These were the costs of protection against extraction, and they were small compared with the later cost of palaces, armies, and tribute collectors. Exceptions such as the potlatch show that even in this world, surplus could be consumed by prestige once enough of it existed.

## What Drove Development

In late June 1861—winter in Australia—two Europeans were dying of exhaustion in the continent’s interior near Cooper Creek. Robert Burke and William Wills led the first major expedition to cross Australia from south to north, but they could not complete the return journey. In the same country around them, the Yandruwandha people lived reasonably well. Aboriginal people even fed the exhausted newcomers fish and cakes made from nardoo, the seeds of an aquatic fern. The Europeans learned to gather nardoo themselves but did not know how to prepare it properly: Aboriginal people soaked and rinsed the flour for a long time, breaking down a substance that interferes with the absorption of vitamin B1. Burke and Wills ate large quantities of nardoo and continued to weaken by the day. In all likelihood, it was this vitamin deficiency that finally killed them. Only a third member of the party, John King, survived after the Yandruwandha took him in.

American anthropologist Joseph Henrich examines this story in one of the opening chapters of his book *The Secret of Our Success* (2015). Its lesson is simple and unexpected. Strong, educated, well-equipped men died not for lack of intelligence or strength but for lack of knowledge that no individual could have invented alone. The nardoo recipe was the product of hundreds of generations of trial and error, and most of the people who followed it probably could not explain why every step was necessary. The knowledge was stored not in any one mind but in tradition—in the sum of many minds.

This is the central answer to the question of what drove the hunter-gatherer economy forward. It was not any single invention, but the mechanism that prevented inventions from disappearing.

The first element of this mechanism was **cumulative culture**. American psychologist Michael Tomasello calls it the “ratchet effect”: a ratchet is a toothed wheel with a pawl that lets it turn forward but prevents it from slipping backward. Chimpanzees invent things too: they crack nuts with stones and fish for termites with twigs. But their discoveries are rarely built upon—each generation begins at roughly the same point. Humans, by contrast, can copy another person’s skill precisely without fully understanding it, then add a small improvement. One hundred generations, one small improvement at a time, and a striking stone becomes a composite dart used with a spear-thrower. The slow history of the hand axe and the faster history of the past hundred thousand years, discussed in the section on energy, tell the story of the ratchet gathering speed.

The second element was **language and teaching**. Language turns private experience into common knowledge: you do not need to step on a snake yourself to learn where its nest is. It allows people to describe what is not before their eyes—water three days’ journey away, last year’s drought, the behavior of an animal the listener has never seen. Teaching stretched across a long childhood turns this knowledge into skill. We have already seen that a hunter’s output peaks around age thirty or forty: society extends “credit” to the young for decades in order to produce a master later. In the terms of our framework, this is a direct investment of surplus in development—in human capital, the knowledge and skills that yield returns for years.

The third element was **the size and connectedness of the network**. The more people participate in preserving knowledge and the more often they interact, the more inventions appear and the less often they vanish. Henrich calls this the “collective brain.” In 2010, American researchers Michelle Kline and Robert Boyd compared fishing gear and other tools in ten traditional Oceanian societies and found that toolkits were richer and more complex on islands with larger populations and closer contact with their neighbors. Tasmania, discussed in the section on exchange, shows the same relationship in reverse. Long-distance connections—beads passed from hand to hand across hundreds of kilometers, hxaro partnerships, shared seasonal camps—were therefore not ornaments of life but its infrastructure. Shells traveled through them, but so did skills.

The fourth element was **trust and mutual insurance**. Sharing the kill, as discussed above, was not merely an ethic but a technology: it allowed a group to survive an unsuccessful week without creating stores it could not carry. And where insurance is reliable, people can take risks—spend days hunting large game, enter unfamiliar country, try something new. A society in which failure means starvation cannot afford experimentation. A society in which the unsuccessful person will be fed can. British psychologist Robin Dunbar even proposes that language grew largely out of “gossip”: the exchange of information about who was honest and who was greedy. If so, the first language was also a system for tracking reputation—the ancestor of the credit history.

The fifth element was **low-cost institutions**. Hunter-gatherer rules were upheld by everyone at once and cost almost nothing. There were no tribute collectors, guards, or priests to feed. Freedom of exit prevented anyone from converting the common product into private rent. Nearly all of a group’s productive capacity, modest though it was, therefore worked for its members rather than for those who governed them. Later, when people settled on the land, they gained the ability to undertake great projects, but paid for it with a growing share of surplus flowing upward.

Finally, development was driven by what might be called **knowledge of nature as a commons**. A map of water sources, a calendar of ripening fruit, techniques for detoxifying poisonous plants, the knowledge of when to burn grass—all of it belonged to no one and was available to every member of the group. No one could patent nardoo or post a guard along a “songline.” This does not mean knowledge never had owners: some rituals and information were secret, available only to initiates—to men, women, or elders. But knowledge on which life depended was spread as widely as possible, because every death of one of its bearers was a loss to everyone.

The limitations of this list should be stated honestly. All five mechanisms worked slowly: over millennia, not decades. Small groups repeatedly lost what they had discovered, and most of the inventions made over those 300,000 years have surely vanished without a trace. And, as we have seen, almost all the accumulated gain went into population growth and geographic expansion rather than wealth per person. Even so, this was where the foundation for all subsequent history was assembled: fire, tools, language, teaching, exchange networks, and trust among people who were not required to submit to one another.

Reduced to an answer to the book’s recurring question, this section says: the developmental share of the era’s surplus went almost entirely into knowledge and connections. Free time became the education of children and stories by the fire; a successful hunt became insurance and trust; a rare stone became a network of friends through which skills could flow. It added no material possessions. But it was the invisible capital without which neither the first field nor the first city could have appeared.

## What Caused Destruction

In the mid-1960s, American archaeologist Fred Wendorf was excavating an ancient cemetery on the bank of the Nile near Jebel Sahaba in Sudan. The Aswan Dam was about to flood the valley, and the rescue expedition was racing against time. The graves held roughly sixty people—men, women, and children. Two-thirds bore evidence of injury on their bones, according to a 2021 reexamination; the original study had estimated about one-third. Stone points were embedded in ribs and vertebrae, while arms showed the characteristic fractures sustained when a person raises them against a blow. Redating published in 2021 by a team led by Isabelle Crevecoeur showed that the remains were at least 13,000 years old. This was still a world of hunters, fishers, and gatherers, before any agriculture. Judging by older, healed wounds, this was not a single massacre but a series of clashes repeated over years.

The previous section concerned what accumulated the strength of human societies. This one concerns what destroyed it. In the language of our framework, destructive uses are anything that destroys people, knowledge, trust, or capital. Hunter-gatherers had almost no objects to destroy, but they still had much to lose: lives, accumulated skill, and natural capital—the wealth not created by human beings but capable of feeding them, such as herds, fish-filled rivers, and fertile lands.

**Violence between people.** The share of people in that era who died at other people’s hands is one of the most heated disputes in contemporary scholarship. In 2009, American economist Samuel Bowles combined archaeological and ethnographic data and estimated that warfare and raids accounted for an average of roughly 14 percent of deaths in hunter-gatherer societies. In *The Better Angels of Our Nature* (2011), Steven Pinker made similar figures one of his central arguments: in his view, life without the state was far more dangerous than life today. If these estimates are correct, the share of violent deaths was higher than in twentieth-century Europe, despite its two world wars.

Critics object, and their arguments deserve a hearing. Archaeologists note that famous “battlefields” such as Jebel Sahaba were places where people had already become sedentary beside rich resources and therefore had something to defend. In 2013, Douglas Fry and Patrik Söderberg examined homicides in twenty-one mobile hunter-gatherer societies and found that most were committed by individuals for personal reasons—jealousy, revenge, quarrels—not in wars between groups. The famous site at Nataruk near Lake Turkana in Kenya, where at least twenty-seven people were killed around 10,000 years ago, has likewise received different interpretations: some see it as the earliest evidence of warfare, while others question whether all the remains belong to a single event.

Where does the truth lie? Most likely, both sides are right under different conditions. Where people traveled in small groups and could always separate, war in our sense was rare, but murders arising from quarrels were not. Richard Lee himself calculated that the homicide rate among the Ju/’hoansi in the first half of the twentieth century was comparable to that of troubled American cities—though in a population of only a few hundred, every killing became an event remembered for decades. Where a valuable resource bound people to one place—a fish-filled river, an oasis—organized conflict appeared as well. This is the same pattern we saw in the section on power: a store that cannot be carried away makes possible both domination within the group and war between groups.

Economically, violence destroyed several things at once. It killed primarily young men—the very people in whose education society had invested twenty years and who were only approaching their peak productivity. It severed the ties between groups through which rare materials and skills traveled. And it forced people to spend time and effort on vigilance: choosing camps for defensibility rather than access to food, staying together, avoiding strangers. Every such precaution was a small tax on productivity and exchange.

**The extinction of megafauna.** The second form of destruction was slower and less visible, but larger in scale. Megafauna are large animals, usually those weighing more than 40–45 kilograms. Over the past fifty thousand years, the planet has lost most of them. In Australia, shortly after humans arrived around 45,000–40,000 years ago, the giant marsupials disappeared: the rhinoceros-sized diprotodon, the marsupial lion, kangaroos taller than a person, enormous flightless birds. Around 13,000–11,000 years ago, the Americas lost mammoths and mastodons, giant sloths, native horses, and camels—by common estimates, roughly three-quarters of their genera of large mammals. Eurasia lost the woolly rhinoceros, cave bear, and mammoth, the last of which survived on Wrangel Island until about four thousand years ago—by then, the Egyptian pyramids were already standing.

Were humans responsible? In the 1960s and 1970s, American paleontologist Paul Martin proposed the “Pleistocene overkill” hypothesis: hunters with spear-throwers and fire entered new lands where animals did not know or fear humans and wiped them out within a few centuries. His opponents point to the rapid climatic changes at the end of the Ice Age, which reduced pasture in their own right. The debate remains unresolved, and the answer probably differs from place to place: climate was decisive in some regions, humans in others, and most often the two acted together. But two observations are difficult to explain without human involvement. First, the wave of extinctions on each continent and island tracks human settlement remarkably closely, rather than climatic boundaries. Second, Africa lost the fewest large animals—the one place where wildlife had lived alongside hunting humans and their ancestors for hundreds of thousands of years and had time to learn fear.

The clearest example comes not from the Stone Age but from our own Middle Ages and is therefore well studied. Polynesians, the ancestors of the Māori, settled New Zealand around 1300. The islands were home to nine species of moa, flightless birds, the largest taller than a human. Aside from a giant eagle, moa knew no enemies. Radiocarbon evidence published in 2014 by Richard Holdaway and colleagues shows that the moa disappeared no more than a century and a half after people arrived, when the human population is estimated to have numbered only a few thousand. Climate played no role. A small number of hunters and birds that had never learned to flee were enough.

Why this happened is understandable without invoking malice. Every hunter faced a simple calculation: the animal I do not kill today will go to a neighboring group. No one owned the mammoth herd, and no one could forbid hunting it. Economists call this an open-access problem: when anyone may use a resource and no one is responsible for preserving it, each individual rationally takes as much as possible, and together they ruin the resource. In 1968, American ecologist Garrett Hardin called this logic “the tragedy of the commons,” though Elinor Ostrom—awarded the Nobel Prize in Economics partly for her work on this question—showed that communities long established in one place can usually agree on rules for careful use. People on new, unfamiliar land had no such rules yet; they had no experience from which to develop them.

The consequences of this destruction lasted for millennia. Megafauna were enormous natural capital: a stock of meat, fat, hide, and bone that reproduced itself. By exterminating them, hunters consumed, in an accountant’s language, not income but principal. In *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, Jared Diamond points to a still more distant consequence: native horses and camels disappeared in the Americas—relatives of the species later domesticated in the Old World. The future farmers of the Americas inherited far fewer candidates for domestication, which Diamond considers one reason their economies developed without draft animals and without the wheel in practical use. The disappearance of large game may also have helped push people toward plant foods and ultimately agriculture, as we will discuss in the next chapter.

**Loss of knowledge.** We have already encountered the third form of destruction. When a group died out from drought, disease, or attack, everything its elders knew died with it. In a world where knowledge was stored only in heads and hands, every such loss was a library fire. The history of Tasmania shows that even without violence, the isolation of a small population could erase skills accumulated over centuries.

To summarize in the language of the book’s recurring question: the destructive share of this era’s surplus was small in objects—there were almost no objects—but large in people and nature. Violence took some of the most valuable workers and broke exchange networks. Hunting unafraid giants looked like abundance and was therefore not perceived as waste: a few centuries of successful hunting were paid for with millennia of a poorer diet. This is history’s first lesson in a pattern that will recur again and again: consuming capital while mistaking it for income is easiest precisely when the capital seems inexhaustible.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

The Meriam live on Mer Island in the Torres Strait between Australia and New Guinea. They are farmers and fishers who still hunt sea turtles. In the 1990s, American anthropologists Eric Alden Smith and Rebecca Bliege Bird calculated the cost of such a hunt. A boat must be equipped and fuel purchased, while during nesting season a turtle can simply be collected from the beach by anyone, with almost no risk or expense. Yet before major feasts, young men put to sea and hunt turtles with harpoons, giving chase at great risk and exertion. They distribute the meat among all the guests and keep almost none for themselves. The point, the researchers concluded, lies precisely in the expenditure: the entire island sees who can afford such extravagance and remembers it.

In 1975, Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi called this the “handicap principle”: a peacock’s tail is convincing precisely because it makes life harder and cannot be cheaply faked. Anthropologists adopted the idea under the name “costly signaling.” We encountered it in the section on labor organization, where Kristen Hawkes proposed that part of Hadza men’s hunting was display rather than the most efficient way to feed a family. We should now ask directly: was that share of labor wasteful spending?

Recall our measure. Wasteful spending consumes resources without making anyone more productive or improving life overall. The key word is “overall.” If one hunter rises in the esteem of his companions, another falls by the same amount: respect is a relative good, and its total within the group hardly increases. In *Social Limits to Growth* (1976), British economist Fred Hirsch called such goods positional: their value lies in having more than others. Competition for a positional good resembles spectators in a stadium standing up to see better; once everyone stands, the view is the same as before, but standing is more tiring. We will later encounter the same logic in arms races, status consumption, and advertising wars. Its earliest and most modest example appeared around the hunters’ fire.

Even so, display hunting cannot be called pure waste. The meat did not disappear; people ate it. The signal conveyed real qualities—strength, skill, knowledge of the sea or savanna—and therefore helped the group choose reliable partners and allies. This is a classic mixed case: part of the effort goes into status, but it also produces food and information. More important is the scale. As long as the kill was shared with everyone, display remained a display of generosity, and those who lost the contest still ate. Its costs were limited to extra hours and extra risk.

Hunter-gatherers also possessed cheap brakes on this competition. Ridiculing a kill, as Richard Lee described, distributing meat through the owner of the arrow rather than the archer, and the obligation to share all prevented status rivalry from accelerating. Egalitarian morality can be called history’s first restraint on wasteful spending. It cost almost nothing—a few barbs and a few hurt feelings—and saved a great deal.

**When the brakes weakened.** Where those restraints failed, the picture changed. We have already mentioned the Sungir burials with their thousands of mammoth-ivory beads and the potlatch of the Northwest Coast of North America. In our framework, these are two different forms of spending. The Sungir beads went into the ground with the dead: thousands of hours of skilled labor were permanently withdrawn from circulation. This does not mean the beads were meaningless to the people who made them—the burial ritual sustained collective memory and social bonds—but the stock of knowledge and tools did not increase. The potlatch was expenditure in public: blankets, fat, copper plates, and sometimes even canoes were distributed or ostentatiously destroyed. Some of what was given away served redistribution and insurance between families and villages, another mixed case. But what was destroyed was surplus literally burned in a competition: one chief had to surpass his rival, who then had to answer with still more.

The conclusion of the potlatch story is revealing. In 1884, the Canadian government outlawed it as wasteful and “uncivilized”; the ban formally remained in effect until 1951. It destroyed not only the competitive destruction but the entire fabric of exchange, redistribution, and memory sustained by these ceremonies. This is an early lesson we will need later: the wasteful part of a custom cannot always be cut away without damaging its developmental part, and an outside power deciding for others what counts as waste may itself become destructive.

**Monuments at the threshold.** There is one final and very late example. Around 11,500 years ago in what is now southeastern Turkey, people who did not yet sow grain began building Göbekli Tepe—circles of carved stone pillars more than five meters high. German archaeologist Klaus Schmidt, who directed excavations from 1995, regarded it as a sanctuary built by hunter-gatherers. After his death in 2014, new excavations led by Lee Clare uncovered evidence of dwellings, water cisterns, and large-scale processing of wild grain. Many archaeologists now see the site less as a temple in an empty landscape than as a settlement with ritual buildings. Quarrying, shaping, and erecting multi-ton pillars was an immense undertaking for a society without metal or draft animals. Was it wasteful spending? By a direct measure, yes: the pillars fed no one. But construction brought hundreds of people together, required food stores, and, in Schmidt’s view, may have helped drive the transition to agriculture. That is the subject of the next chapter, where projects of this kind become commonplace.

**Conclusion.** This section answers the question of where the era’s surplus went as follows: the wasteful share was small, for two reasons. The first was poverty in possessions: a mobile way of life prevented people from accumulating things that could be burned for status. The second was egalitarian morality, which cheaply and reliably dampened competition for prestige. Waste appeared where both restraints weakened—where stores arose and where a person could rise permanently above others rather than merely for the duration of a hunt. This foreshadows the central story of the chapters ahead. Once the granary becomes normal and the brakes weaken, positional competition will grow from extra hours around the fire into palaces, tombs, and armies—and the share of surplus consumed by prestige will increase by orders of magnitude.

## Lesson of the Era

Three hundred thousand years—more than ninety-five percent of the history of our species—passed without fields, cities, or money. It is easy to dismiss this time as prehistory, when “nothing happened.” But for the question of this book, it is invaluable: this is the only long era in which we can observe an economy with almost no extraction, almost no prestige spending, and almost no accumulation. What does it tell us? Let us reduce what has been said to five propositions.

**First: the principal form of capital is knowledge, not things.** Hunter-gatherers possessed almost no property, yet year after year they grew stronger because their skills were not lost. The story of Burke and Wills, who starved to death beside people living on the same land, shows that the difference between death and plenty may lie entirely in the mind rather than the storeroom. Fire, the recipe for nardoo, a map of water sources, the ability to burn grass in the right month—all of these functioned as capital long before there was a word for capital. This gives us our first yardstick for every era that follows: we must look not only at how many things a society produces, but also at how much knowledge it preserves and passes on.

**Second: the return on knowledge grows with the network.** Isolated Tasmania lost tools; the connected islands of Oceania accumulated them. Beads and obsidian passing from hand to hand across hundreds of kilometers were roads along which skills flowed. The hunter economy demonstrates what trade routes, universities, and the internet would later confirm: the more people participate in a shared exchange of knowledge, and the cheaper that exchange becomes, the faster the ratchet turns. A retreat from openness—whether voluntary or imposed—slows down even the most gifted society.

**Third: low-cost institutions are possible as long as exit remains possible.** Egalitarian morality, ridicule of the braggart, sharing on demand, and above all the freedom to leave for another group kept power in check at almost no cost. Nearly all the modest surplus worked for members of the group rather than for those who governed them. But this order rested on two pillars: poverty in possessions and human mobility. Wherever dried salmon, a rich river, or an oasis appeared—resources that could not be carried away—hereditary nobles, slaves, and organized raids appeared as well. This is perhaps the most important bridge to the next chapter: inequality and extraction begin not with bad morals, but with a store of wealth from which one cannot walk away.

**Fourth: the most dangerous expenditure is consuming capital while calling it income.** The destruction of the megafauna and the moa looked like abundance: meat was plentiful, the animals were unafraid, no one owned the herd, and no one prohibited hunting. Descendants paid for a few centuries of easy prey with millennia of a poorer diet. The mechanism—open access without rules—is not confined to the Stone Age; we will encounter it in the depleted soils of Mesopotamia, in felled forests, and in seas fished empty. The hunters’ lesson is that such rules must be devised before the resource is exhausted—and, as Elinor Ostrom showed, communities long settled in one place are usually capable of doing so.

**Fifth: the pursuit of status is eternal, but the scale of its costs depends on the brakes.** Hunting turtles for glory, spending thousands of hours on burial beads, smashing copper plates at a potlatch—the desire to stand above others existed even in the most egalitarian societies. As long as ridicule and compulsory sharing checked it, and the kill still went to everyone, its cost was limited to extra hours and extra risk. Where the brakes weakened, the wasteful share grew. The future history of palaces, tombs, and arms races is not the appearance of a new desire, but the removal of old restraints.

If we combine these propositions into an answer to the book’s recurring question, it sounds like this. The surplus of the hunter-gatherer era was small and rarely took the form of things. Its developmental share went into knowledge, the education of children, exchange networks, and trust; its destructive share went into violence and, above all, the depletion of natural capital; its wasteful share was small because there was almost nothing to burn and because morality dampened the race for prestige. But most of the gain did not become greater wealth per person at all; it went into population growth and settlement across the planet. We discussed this trap—where every improvement is “consumed” by population growth—in the introduction, and the next chapter will show it at full strength.

The chief limitation of these conclusions should also be stated honestly. Our knowledge of hunter-gatherer life comes from two incomplete sources: mute archaeological remains and observations of nineteenth- and twentieth-century peoples who lived on the margins, displaced by farmers and states, and who had long traded with them. Neither the Ju/’hoansi nor the Hadza offer an unfiltered window into the Paleolithic. Each proposition above is therefore not a law but a pattern to be tested against the eras that follow. If it is valid, we will see it again—in the temples of Sumer, the mines of Rome, and the factories of Manchester.

## Figures

Figures for the hunter-gatherer era are the least reliable in this entire book. There were no censuses and no writing, and everything below consists of estimates reconstructed from bones, genetics, and observations of twentieth-century peoples. They should be treated as orders of magnitude, not precise data.

**Population.** By the end of the era, around ten thousand years ago, estimates place the population of the entire Earth between one and ten million; four to five million is the figure most often cited. Growth was almost zero: expressed annually, population increased by hundredths of a percent. By comparison, in the twentieth century the world’s population grew by roughly one to two percent per year—a hundred times faster. Among mobile foragers, a woman bore an average of four to six children, at intervals of about three to four years—as Richard Lee and Nancy Howell observed among the Ju/’hoansi. This limit was imposed not by rules but by prolonged breastfeeding and the need to carry a child.

**Life expectancy.** In 2007, anthropologists Michael Gurven and Hillard Kaplan compiled data from several modern hunter-gatherer groups. Average life expectancy at birth ranged from approximately twenty-one to thirty-seven years. But this figure is misleading: infant and child mortality pulls it downward. According to the same survey, about forty percent of children did not survive to age fifteen. Those who did survive childhood often lived into their sixties or seventies; the modal age of adult death was around seventy. Elderly people were a common presence in camp—and, as we have seen, an important part of the economy: grandmothers fed grandchildren, while elders preserved knowledge.

**Labor.** According to Lee’s calculations, obtaining food occupied about twenty hours per week for each Ju/’hoansi adult; including toolmaking, food processing, and domestic tasks brought the total to around forty to forty-five hours. Figures for the Hadza and other groups vary, and in bad seasons the work became harder and took longer.

**Energy.** In 1971, American geologist Earl Cook estimated the daily energy consumption of a hunter at about three thousand kilocalories from food and two thousand from fire, for a total of roughly five thousand. Other authors, including historian Ian Morris, give foragers a range of two to five thousand. By comparison, Cook estimated that a resident of a wealthy industrial country in the second half of the twentieth century used about two hundred thirty thousand kilocalories per day once fuel, electricity, and transport were included. The difference is nearly forty- to fiftyfold—and is perhaps the most honest measure of how profoundly the economy has changed since then.

**Income.** The Maddison Project does not reach so far into the past, and any estimate of hunter-gatherer “income per person” is necessarily hypothetical. Economists generally describe it as subsistence: enough to feed oneself and raise the next generation. In *A Farewell to Alms* (2007), British economic historian Gregory Clark went further, arguing that the ordinary English person around 1800 lived no better in material terms than the average hunter-gatherer. The claim is disputed, but it conveys the scale: tens of thousands of years and the entire agrarian era barely changed the consumption of the majority. What changed was population, not prosperity.

**Knowledge.** The Acheulean handaxe changed scarcely at all for more than a million years. Then, over the last tens of thousands of years, the toolkit began changing more rapidly: needles, harpoons, spear-throwers, bows, and nets appeared. If a single figure grew during this era, it was the number of skills passed from one generation to the next. It cannot be counted—but it, rather than stores of things, was the wealth of these people.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

For foragers, this calculation is the hardest of all: no palaces, no accounts, no crops. Everything below is the author’s estimate with wide margins of error; what matters is the order of magnitude.

I convert food into a grain equivalent by calories (one kilogram of grain is about 3,500 kilocalories, a day’s ration). According to Richard Lee’s observations, a Ju/’hoansi adult spent about twenty hours per week obtaining food while also feeding dependents; with processing and bad seasons included, that comes to between one and three hours for each day of sustenance. A sack—fifty days of food—cost between fifty and one hundred fifty hours, several times more than for farmers: there was neither field nor granary. A working life amounted to about seventy thousand hours.

### The Sungir Beads

Around 34,000 years ago at the Sungir site, an adult man and two children were buried with approximately thirteen thousand beads made from mammoth ivory, according to Otto Bader’s excavation reports. Randall White, who replicated their manufacture, estimated the labor at roughly one hour per bead: ten to fifteen thousand hours in all. Feeding the craftsperson throughout that time required another twenty-five to fifty sacks, or just over one thousand to seven and a half thousand hours of food procurement. Together, the total comes to eleven to twenty-two thousand hours—between one-eighth and one-third of a working life. For a community of twenty to thirty adults, that was between one-fifth and nearly one-half of the entire group’s annual labor. Those hours could not build a granary; the alternatives were time and knowledge—the education of children, expeditions for flint, warm clothing, rest. Some of the hours may have returned in the form of cohesion: the beads may have been worn by the living. But what went into the ground was a pure withdrawal from the stock of labor.

### The Stone Circle at Göbekli Tepe

A circle of T-shaped pillars weighing up to ten or twenty tons each. Klaus Schmidt estimated that the work required at least five hundred people; the duration is unknown, so I assume two to four months per circle—between 240,000 and 600,000 hours. Feeding the workers required the equivalent of 600 to 1,200 sacks of real grain, though it was wild: excavations uncovered evidence of large-scale processing. Jack Harlan tested for himself that a flint sickle could harvest about one kilogram of wild wheat per hour; with threshing and grinding, a sack cost the same fifty to one hundred fifty hours. The total therefore comes to 270,000 to 800,000 hours—between three and thirteen working lives per circle, and there were about twenty circles.

That grain would have fed a community of one hundred people for a year to a year and a half. But according to Schmidt’s hypothesis, which not all archaeologists accept, it may have been precisely for projects of this kind that people began storing and sowing grain—an apparently wasteful expenditure that helped drive the most developmental invention of the era.

### Violence: Accounting in Actual Lives

I do not combine destructive expenditure with wasteful expenditure. If Samuel Bowles’s estimate is correct that raids accounted for approximately one death in seven—critics regard this share as too high—then every twenty-year-old killed took with him about sixty thousand hours of future labor: more than all the beads of Sungir, and lost beyond recovery.

**Conclusion.** The most conspicuous wasteful expenditures of foragers amounted to fractions of a working life or single-digit numbers of lives; the hypothetical tomb in the introduction cost about four hundred. The difference lay not in morals but in the stock available: one can spend on prestige only as much as one manages to store. The next chapter concerns what happened when the granary appeared.
