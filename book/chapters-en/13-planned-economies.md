# Chapter 13. Planned Economies

## Portrait of the Era

The southern Urals, early 1930s. On the bare steppe beside Magnitnaya Mountain, where only a few years earlier stood the yurts of Kazakh herders and a pair of Cossack villages, the country’s largest iron and steel complex is under construction. Its model is the American city of Gary, built around U.S. Steel. American engineers are designing the project; some of the equipment is being bought abroad with foreign currency earned by selling grain. Winter temperatures fall below thirty degrees Celsius, and thousands of people live in tents and barracks. John Scott, a young American who came to the Soviet Union as a worker, later described it in *Behind the Urals* (1942): riveting in the wind, frostbitten hands, shortages of everything from mittens to bread—and at the same time the enthusiasm of people certain they were building a new world. Nearby, behind barbed wire, worked “special settlers”—families exiled here during the campaign of “dekulakization.” Magnitka’s first blast furnace produced pig iron in 1932. Within ten years, a city that had not existed would become one of the principal arsenals of the war.

Moscow, the same years. In the offices of the State Planning Commission—Gosplan, established back in 1921—hundreds of economists and statisticians reduce to tables what millions of buyers and sellers once decided: how many tons of steel, cement, calico, and nails the country needs; how many tractors to send to which districts; at what price the factory in Gorky will receive metal from Magnitogorsk. The First Five-Year Plan (1928–1932) is declared fulfilled in four years and three months. The slogan “The Five-Year Plan in Four Years!” hangs in every factory. This is an attempt without precedent in history: to replace the market with a single calculation—to decide from one center where the surplus of an entire country will go.

Now the same country and the same years, but in the countryside. Collective farms promise to pool equipment and labor and provide access to tractors and agronomic expertise. The transition is carried out in haste and, in many places, by force. The harvests of 1931 and 1932 are poor: adverse weather, plant diseases, losses during harvesting, and disruptions in farm management all take their toll. Grain procurements in 1932/33 are reduced from the previous year, but the grain left in the countryside is not enough to feed the population. In the winter and spring of 1933, millions die of hunger in Ukraine, the North Caucasus, and the Volga region. In Kazakhstan the famine began earlier; there, the forced settlement of nomads and the confiscation of livestock also contributed to the disaster. The death toll from the Soviet famine, still disputed by historians, is usually estimated at five to eight million. Poor harvests and government decisions act together; the catastrophe cannot be reduced to a single cause.

Move forward thirty years. On April 12, 1961, Yuri Gagarin orbits the Earth aboard *Vostok*. Four years earlier, a Soviet satellite had become the first to enter orbit, and American newspapers wrote of the “Sputnik shock”: a country where more than half of all adults had been unable to read in 1917 had overtaken the world’s richest economy in the most sophisticated technology of the age. Many Western economists then seriously believed that a planned system could outpace a market system in economic growth—and textbooks from the 1960s contain graphs in which the lines for the USSR and the United States are about to cross.

China, autumn 1958. Mao Zedong proclaims the Great Leap Forward: catch up with Britain in steel output within fifteen years—and then sooner still. Across the country, peasants build hundreds of thousands of small “blast furnaces” in their yards and melt pots, hoes, and door hinges in them; what comes out is mostly brittle metal, useless for anything. The harvest remains in the fields because people are tending the furnaces, while local officials, fearing accusations, report records that never occurred. The state takes grain on the basis of these fabricated figures. In 1959–1961, famine follows—regarded by most researchers as the deadliest in human history, with estimates of the death toll ranging from fifteen to forty-five million.

Belgrade, June 1950. Yugoslavia, having broken with Stalin two years earlier, passes a law transferring factories to the management of their work collectives. Workers’ councils elect directors themselves and decide how to divide the enterprise’s income. This is a third way—neither a market of private owners nor commands from the center—and in the 1960s economists from around the world will visit Yugoslav factories.

Santiago, Chile, 1972. In a room resembling the set of a science-fiction film stand seven chairs with buttons in their armrests, while the walls are covered with screens displaying graphs. The British cybernetician Stafford Beer, commissioned by Salvador Allende’s government, is building the Cybersyn system: data from nationalized factories is to arrive daily by telex at a center, where a computer looks for deviations and flags problems before they become crises. The project will not have time to become fully operational: on September 11, 1973, a military coup puts an end to both it and Allende’s government.

And the final scene: any Soviet city in the early 1980s. A line outside a store: sausage or Yugoslav boots have been “put out” for sale. The word “shortage” has long since ceased to be merely an economic term and become part of everyday speech. People have money and jobs—officially, there is no unemployment—but cannot use that money to buy what they want. Factories fulfill the plan in tons and units; warehouses are packed with things nobody needs, while stores lack what everyone needs. The country produces more steel and tractors than the United States, yet imports grain from abroad.

Sixty years separate Magnitogorsk from the queue. In the mid-twentieth century, a significant share of humanity lived in countries where the main economic decisions were governed by planning; by 1980, roughly one-third did. These countries deliberately directed resources into factories, schools, and science. Results varied across periods and sectors: rapid growth in production and education coexisted with shortages, coercion, and severe crises. How these results compare with their costs, and how that relationship changed over time, is the subject of this chapter.

## Energy and Technology

From its first day, the planned economy understood itself as an energy project. In December 1920, amid the devastation of the Civil War, the Eighth Congress of Soviets approved the GOELRO plan, prepared by the State Commission for the Electrification of Russia: thirty regional power stations were to be built within ten to fifteen years. It was then that Lenin pronounced the formula that would appear on posters for decades: communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the entire country. The English writer H. G. Wells, who visited Lenin that same year, called him the “dreamer in the Kremlin” and did not believe in these plans. Yet by the early 1930s, the plan had largely been fulfilled, and its electricity-generation target exceeded. For our framework, this is an important point: the first thing in which the new government decided to invest the meager surplus of a devastated country was the same force that drove all the preceding chapters—energy per person.

The symbol of this effort was the Dnieper Hydroelectric Station (DniproHES), the dam on the Dnieper near present-day Zaporizhzhia, commissioned in 1932. The American engineer Hugh Cooper, who had built hydroelectric stations in the United States, served as a consultant, while General Electric supplied the first generators (the rest were assembled from its blueprints by Leningrad’s Elektrosila). This was not a chance detail but the essence of early Soviet industrialization: for the most part, technology was purchased. The firm of the Detroit architect Albert Kahn, which had designed Ford factories, designed hundreds of industrial facilities for the USSR between 1930 and 1932. The Stalingrad Tractor Plant was manufactured in sections in the United States and assembled on the Volga; the automobile factory in Nizhny Novgorod (then Gorky) was built under an agreement with Ford and produced a copy of the Ford Model A. Payment came in grain, timber, oil, and gold. Grain exports continued during the famine years, though at a reduced volume. Some of the hard-currency revenue paid for equipment, but this does not exhaust the causes of the famine: the food available in the countryside was also affected by the size of the harvest, grain procurements, and the distribution of aid.

The plan’s chief priority was written in two letters. Industry was divided into “Group A”—the production of means of production (steel, coal, machine tools, and electricity)—and “Group B”—the production of consumer goods (clothing, footwear, furniture, and food). Group A was supposed to grow faster, and for a long time it did. This meant postponing part of current consumption for the sake of future capacity. According to Abram Bergson’s calculations, discussed by Robert Allen in *Farm to Factory* (2003), the share of investment rose from roughly one-eighth of national product in 1928 to one-quarter by the end of the 1930s. Comparison with other countries would require a consistent definition of investment and an adjustment for differences in starting income; no such comparison is made here.

Measured by “energy and steel,” the result is impressive. The oil fields of Baku were supplemented by the “Second Baku” in the Volga and Urals regions and, from the 1960s onward, by the fields of Western Siberia, where the giant Samotlor field was discovered in 1965. Then came gas from Urengoy and pipelines to Europe. The atomic project, launched to build a bomb (first tested in 1949), also produced a civilian by-product: in 1954, the world’s first nuclear power station to supply electricity to a conventional grid began operating in Obninsk. In the 1970s, the USSR became the world’s largest producer of steel and oil. A country that had produced several times less steel than the United States in 1913 was producing more by the 1980s.

The volume of steel, oil, and tractors is not the same as people’s income: what matters is how much was produced per worker and how much reached the consumer. Economists distinguish between extensive growth—achieved by adding workers, resources, and productive capacity—and intensive growth, in which output per unit of labor and capital rises. In the USSR, heavy investment and the movement of people from the countryside into industry made it possible to increase heavy-industrial output rapidly. Later, the returns on additional investment declined. William Easterly and Stanley Fischer attributed the slowdown in growth partly to these diminishing returns, though the pattern does not describe every industry and decade equally well.

Why did intensive growth fail to materialize? Not for lack of inventors. Soviet science was strong and, in some fields, the best in the world. A classic example is the continuous casting of steel, in which molten metal is turned directly into billets, bypassing the casting and rolling of ingots: the process saves both metal and energy. Soviet engineers were among the first in the world to build such installations, as early as the 1950s. Yet by the mid-1980s, according to widely cited estimates, less than one-fifth of Soviet steel was continuously cast, compared with the overwhelming majority of Japanese steel. The invention existed; its diffusion did not.

The reason lay in the structure of incentives, which the following sections will examine in greater detail. A factory director received plan targets in tons, units, and rubles of “gross output.” A new technology meant shutting down a workshop for refitting, risking failure to meet this year’s plan and, if everything went well, facing a higher target the next year. There was almost no reward for taking the risk, while punishment for failure was nearly guaranteed. Factories therefore resisted innovations imposed from above and asked for more familiar equipment. They used more metal per unit of output than factories in the West: as Soviet economists themselves observed, Soviet machine tools and machinery were often heavier than their Western counterparts, and a plan measured in tons rewarded that very weight. By the 1980s, according to International Energy Agency estimates from the early 1990s, energy use per unit of output was one and a half to three times higher than in Western Europe, depending on how rubles were converted and how the cold climate was taken into account. By the measure of “energy per person,” the USSR was rich; by the measure of “what each unit of energy delivers to a person,” it was wasteful.

One technological choice concerned computers. In the 1950s and 1960s, Sergei Lebedev’s Soviet BESM series included some of the fastest computers in Europe. The mathematician Viktor Glushkov proposed OGAS, a network of computing centers that would use current data for planning. The project was debated for more than a decade but was never implemented as envisioned; the historian Benjamin Peters describes both the disagreements among government agencies and the question of cost. In the late 1960s, the decision was made to develop the ES EVM series, compatible with the IBM System/360, while curtailing some of the country’s own computer lines. Without a separate comparison, neither this decision nor the lag in microelectronics can be generalized to every Soviet technology. The history of OGAS also shows that a computer network alone could not resolve who set the plan and who verified the data.

China repeated the Soviet pattern—and produced its caricature. The First Five-Year Plan (1953–1957) followed Soviet designs and relied on Soviet specialists: the USSR helped establish around 150 major industrial projects. The Great Leap Forward then attempted to obtain steel without technology at all—through willpower and sheer numbers. Backyard furnaces burned timber and coal, drew labor away from the fields, and produced metal that often had to be discarded. This is an extreme case of what this book calls wasteful spending turning destructive: a vast quantity of energy and labor consumed not even for prestige, but for a figure in a report.

The section’s conclusion can be stated as follows. Planned economies handled very well the first stage that all the preceding chapters associated with growing wealth: they gave people more energy, more machines, more electricity, and more steel. They handled the second stage poorly—the task of making that energy work more intelligently year after year. Surplus went into capacity, but not improvement; into quantity, but not quality; and above all, into what mattered to the center (defense, heavy industry, and records), rather than what consumers needed. Why this happened becomes clear from the way labor was organized in this system.

## Organization of Labor

The planned economy promised something no previous system had promised: a job for everyone. And it kept that promise—with qualifications that form the subject of this section. Unemployment, the nightmare of the Great Depression in the previous chapter, officially disappeared in the USSR in the early 1930s; labor exchanges were closed in 1930. But the absence of unemployment also concealed an absence of choice: the state was virtually the only employer, and it also decided who would live where, how much they would be paid, and whether they could leave.

Let us begin with the countryside, because that is where the hands for industrialization came from. Collectivization, launched in 1929, had brought about two-thirds of the roughly twenty-five million peasant households into collective farms by 1933, and nearly all of them by the end of the decade. Joining was often compulsory. A collective farm received land for its use and pooled a large share of its livestock and equipment, while state machine-tractor stations supplied machinery. A large farm could make mechanization and the work of agronomists easier. But the transition to large farms did not in itself raise yields: before collectivization, many regions used the three-field system, and the hurried consolidation of fields in the early 1930s disrupted existing crop rotations. More complex rotations were introduced later and unevenly.

The collective farm’s harvest went first toward compulsory deliveries to the state at low fixed prices. A collective farmer received not a wage but “labor-days”—notional units of time worked—with whatever remained after deliveries distributed at the end of the year in proportion to those units. In bad years, very little remained: a labor-day might earn only a few hundred grams of grain. Regular cash wages for collective farmers were introduced only in the second half of the 1960s. The collective-farm system combined the possibility of joint investment in machinery with the extraction of a substantial share of the harvest for the benefit of the cities and industry.

A private household plot helped people live on what remained—a small plot beside the house with a vegetable garden, a cow, and chickens. According to widely cited estimates, such plots occupied around three percent of the sown area but produced a substantial share of output, especially potatoes, vegetables, meat, and milk. These two shares cannot be compared directly: arable acreage does not account for family labor, livestock feed, differences among crops, or output from pasture. Household plots show that personal incentives mattered, but by themselves do not measure how much those incentives increased productivity relative to the collective farm.

Leaving a collective farm was not easy. In late 1932, the USSR introduced internal passports and the *propiska*—mandatory registration at one’s place of residence. Urban residents received passports; most collective farmers did not and, without permission from the farm’s management board, could not legally move to a city. The mass issuance of passports to rural residents began only under the 1974 regulations and continued into the early 1980s. In 1958, China introduced a similar system, the *hukou*: a registration system that divided the population into urban and rural categories, with different entitlements to rations, housing, and jobs. Its traces remain visible in the Chinese economy today. Both countries thus tied a significant share of their populations to the land for decades—a policy we encountered in feudal Europe in Chapter 7. The comparison is not exact: a collective farmer did not belong to a landlord, and his children could leave to study or join the army and never return. But the economic logic is similar: people are bound in place when those in power do not want to let them go where they would be better off.

The arrangement in the cities was different. Workers received wages, and in some industries they were paid piece rates based on output. Beginning in the early 1930s, wage differentials were deliberately widened: in 1931, Stalin publicly condemned equal pay as “leveling.” The famous example is Alexei Stakhanov, a miner from the Donbas who, on the night of August 31, 1935, extracted 102 tons of coal, according to official figures, against a norm of about seven. The record was prepared: Stakhanov was assisted by timbermen, and the coalface was readied in advance. Record setters received bonuses and fame; at the same time, new achievements could become grounds for raising output norms. This gave some workers an incentive to conceal their reserve capacity. A single record cannot tell us how most enterprises operated throughout the Soviet era.

The end of the 1930s brought a tightening of discipline. The decree of June 26, 1940, moved the country to an eight-hour workday and a seven-day week with one day off and, most importantly, prohibited workers from leaving an enterprise without authorization and made tardiness of more than twenty minutes a criminal offense. According to archival data, millions of people were convicted under this decree during the years it was in force—most sentenced to corrective labor at their place of employment with part of their wages withheld, but many to imprisonment. It was not fully repealed until 1956. During this period, an urban worker’s right to change jobs was restricted by law; the number convicted in different years cannot be converted into the share of all workers who were unfree.

The extreme point of unfreedom was the Gulag. In the early 1950s, according to archival data, around two and a half million people were held in camps and colonies; more than two million others were special settlers. Prisoners felled timber, dug canals, mined gold and nickel, and built railways. The Gulag received production targets and took part in a number of major construction projects, but these population figures do not show its share of the country’s total labor or capital construction. Guards, supplies, illness, and mortality increased the cost of forced labor. After Stalin’s death in 1953, the camp system began to be reduced; the historian Oleg Khlevniuk notes that economic considerations were among the reasons.

There was, however, another, developmental side to the Soviet organization of labor, without which the picture would be unfair. First was the mass entry of women into paid employment. By the 1970s, women made up around half of everyone employed in the USSR—a share that most Western countries reached later. Domestic work and waiting in lines still fell to women, however, so Soviet sociologists’ time-use studies found their working day to be markedly longer than men’s—the “double shift.” Second came literacy and skills. In two generations, the country turned illiterate peasants into workers, technicians, and engineers; by the 1980s, the USSR ranked among the world leaders in engineers per capita. This was an enormous investment of surplus in human skills—something that clearly belongs on the developmental side of our framework. The question is how those skills were used.

This brings us to late Soviet labor, which many readers remember firsthand. By the 1960s and 1970s, mass terror had receded into the past, and labor was generally free: a person could change jobs, although the *propiska* and employer-provided housing could tie them down strongly. But the employment guarantee remained—it was difficult to dismiss a worker, and an enterprise could not go bankrupt. The Hungarian economist János Kornai called this a “soft budget constraint” in *Economics of Shortage* (1980): if a factory spent more than it earned, the state would rescue it anyway—extend a loan, write off a debt, or raise its prices. A director facing a soft constraint had no reason to economize on labor. On the contrary, extra workers were useful: they could be thrown into a “storming” drive at the end of the month when the plan was in danger, or sent “to the potatoes” to help a collective farm. Soviet enterprises therefore kept more workers than they needed while simultaneously complaining of labor shortages. Kornai showed that this was not an accidental inefficiency but a property of the system: under a soft constraint, enterprises’ demand for everything—metal, machinery, people—becomes nearly insatiable, and chronic shortages arise on their own.

A saying that circulated at the time—“They pretend to pay us, and we pretend to work”—was an exaggeration, but it accurately captured the gap between effort and reward. Pay depended little on results, and it was difficult to use it to buy what one wanted. Access and connections, meanwhile, were genuinely prized: a position close to scarce goods—in a store, a warehouse, or a special distribution center—was worth more than a high salary. At the same time, the “second economy” grew: the *shabashniki*, crews that semi-legally built cowsheds for collective farms for cash during the summer and worked so effectively that one could only marvel at how much the same person could accomplish when paid for results; private tailors, car repairs, and tutoring. The economist Gregory Grossman, who introduced the concept of the Soviet “second economy” into scholarly discussion in 1977, and his successors estimated its scale differently, but agreed that by the 1980s it was substantial.

Finally, there were the managers. Above the entire system stood the *nomenklatura*—a list of senior positions to which appointments could be made only with the approval of Party bodies. A factory director, collective-farm chairman, or head of a central directorate answered first and foremost to superiors for the plan, not to buyers for the product. A career depended on the report, and the report on figures. Hence the inflated figures, the “beating out” of resources, and deliberately understated claims about one’s own capacity—everything that, under political pressure in China in 1958, turned into deadly reports of unprecedented harvests. In the Chinese people’s communes created that same year, labor was assessed in “work points” similar to labor-days, with the same result: the diligent and the lazy received almost the same, and the incentive to exert oneself disappeared.

The conclusion for our question about surplus is this. The planned organization of labor knew how to mobilize: to move millions of people out of the countryside, educate them, put them to work at machines, and make employment universal. This was real development. At the same time, plan targets, lines, downtime, and rush jobs consumed time, but this chapter has no overall estimate of what share of all labor these losses represented. How the output that labor did produce was distributed is the subject of the next section.

## Exchange and Money

Imagine a Moscow bakery in the mid-1970s. The price of bread remained low and stable. At that ratio of prices, some households could buy bread to feed their livestock instead of compound feed. This practice was cited as an example of an undesirable incentive, but without data on its frequency we cannot judge what share of bread was used this way or how much the practice affected supply. The question is broader than a single example: how does a regulated price affect the choices of buyers and producers?

In a market economy, a price can convey information about supply and demand, though by itself it guarantees neither sufficient output nor access to the product for the poor. A planned economy had prices too: people received wages and bought goods with rubles. Pricing agencies set the prices of many products, taking costs and political goals into account. Affordable bread, housing, and transport were deliberate choices. If a regulated price fell below the level at which demand matched the available supply, lines or other forms of allocation emerged. But this depended on the particular product and period; a fixed price did not mean that every product was in short supply.

### The Calculation Debate

The Austrian economist Ludwig von Mises raised this question long before the bakeries of the 1970s. In his 1920 article “Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth,” he argued that without a market for the means of production—machine tools, metal, and land—they would have no prices, and without prices it would be impossible to compare which way of producing something was cheaper for society. A bridge can be built from steel or concrete; a road can go around a hill or pass through a tunnel. An engineer can say what is technically possible, but only a price can say what is sensible. In Mises’s view, socialism was doomed to operate blindly.

The Polish economist Oskar Lange replied in “On the Economic Theory of Socialism” (1936–1937). Lange proposed that a central planning board should set prices and then adjust them by trial and error—lowering the price if a product piled up and raising it if the product was scarce. Enterprise directors would follow simple rules as though they were competing in a market. In this way, the planner would reproduce the workings of the market, but without private owners or the inequality they generated. Many economists at the time concluded that Lange had won the debate. In “The Computer and the Market,” an essay published posthumously in 1967, he added that an electronic computer would solve the system of equations faster than the market could grope its way toward equilibrium.

The most profound objection came from Friedrich Hayek in “The Use of Knowledge in Society” (1945). Hayek shifted the question: the problem was not that the equations were complex, but that the knowledge they required did not exist in any one place. It was dispersed among the minds of millions of people—a foreman knew that a machine was malfunctioning, a purchasing agent knew that a supplier was unreliable, a housewife knew that this year she needed boots more than a coat. This knowledge was local, often unspoken, and quick to become obsolete. A market price, in Hayek’s account, is a mechanism that compresses this knowledge into a single number and conveys it to those who need it without requiring anyone to understand the entire picture. However much it calculates, the center cannot gather knowledge that exists only here and now.

To be fair, the debate is not closed. Advocates of planning reply that markets make mistakes too—and the Great Depression discussed in the previous chapter is terrible proof; that large corporations plan internally rather than trade; and that modern data and computing give the center capabilities of which Mises could not have dreamed. Opponents answer that the difficulty is not merely computational: people do not report the truth when their own plan depends on it—and the preceding section already showed how directors understated their capabilities. Soviet history provides evidence for both sides, but on prices it tended to confirm Hayek. Goskomtsen, the State Price Committee, did not conduct the “trial and error” Lange described: prices changed rarely because every increase became a political event.

### Novocherkassk, 1962

June 1962 showed just how political such a change could be. On June 1, the USSR raised retail prices for meat and butter—by roughly one-third and one-quarter respectively. The economic rationale was clear: the procurement prices paid to collective farms were being raised to revive livestock farming, and the difference had to come from somewhere. But shortly before this, piece rates—how much workers were paid for completed work—had been cut at the Novocherkassk Electric Locomotive Plant. Workers took to the streets; the demonstration outside the city Party committee building was dispersed with gunfire. According to records declassified after the collapse of the USSR, twenty-six people were killed, and several participants were later sentenced to death. The lesson drawn by the leadership was simple: prices for basic foods must not be touched. They remained untouched for almost three decades, while the gap between prices and costs was covered by subsidies that, by the 1980s, had become one of the largest items in the state budget.

### Two Kinds of Money

In effect, a planned economy had two kinds of money, and they scarcely mixed. People received cash rubles—as wages, pensions, and stipends—and spent them in stores. Noncash rubles existed only in enterprises’ accounts at Gosbank and served for accounting: a factory “paid” its metal supplier but could not choose to use that money to buy something absent from the plan. A noncash ruble meant little without “funds”—authorization to receive a resource. More important than money were a line in the plan, a signature at the ministry, and connections with a supply officer. Here money was not a key that opened any door, but a receipt for a decision already made.

Cash was peculiar in its own way. Wages rose in the 1960s and 1970s, while the supply of goods people wanted to buy grew more slowly. People saved—partly because it was customary and partly because there was nothing on which to spend. Kornai called this forced saving: money sat in a savings account not because its owner had decided to put it aside, but because buying a car required years on a waiting list and furniture had to be “obtained.” By the end of the 1980s, the population had accumulated a mass of rubles not backed by goods—what economists at the time called a “monetary overhang.” When prices were liberalized in 1992, this overhang collapsed into inflation and wiped out the savings of an entire generation within months. Chapter 15 returns to this story.

When goods were scarce, a monetary payment alone was not enough: a buyer might spend time in a line or search for the product through acquaintances. There were other channels of distribution as well—food orders, vacation vouchers, and apartments provided through enterprises, and *Beryozka* stores for holders of foreign currency and special certificates. The scholar Alena Ledeneva described *blat* as a network of personal favors. Lines and connections existed, but their prevalence varied by product, city, and year; these examples cannot yield the average time cost for all residents.

China took this to the limit: from the mid-1950s to the early 1990s, grain, oil, cloth, and many other goods were bought not only with money but with ration coupons. A food coupon was tied to one’s place of registration, and without one it was difficult even to have lunch in another city. Money without a coupon meant little—just like a noncash ruble without an allocation.

### Trade with the World

The state regulated foreign trade. The ruble was not freely convertible into other currencies, while trade within the socialist bloc was conducted through the Council for Mutual Economic Assistance (Comecon), established in 1949. Beginning in 1964, settlements used the transferable ruble—an accounting unit that could not be spent outside the system. Countries agreed on the volume and range of deliveries: Hungary supplied Ikarus buses, East Germany supplied railway cars and machine tools, and the USSR supplied oil and gas. This was international trade governed by planning agreements, not merely exchange among agencies within a single country.

From the 1970s onward, oil and gas became an important source of foreign-currency revenue for the USSR. The proceeds were used to buy grain, equipment, and consumer goods. The Kosygin reform of 1965 sought to expand the autonomy of enterprises, but its principal measures had already begun to be rolled back by the early 1970s. It would therefore be a mistake to attribute this reversal solely to the rise in oil prices after 1973. Export revenue may later have made foreign purchases easier, but its influence on the reforms cannot be determined without a separate study. The fall in world oil prices in 1986 reduced the country’s access to foreign currency.

### Where the Surplus Went

Plan targets, prices, government agencies, and networks of personal connections all played a part in allocating goods and resources. Access to goods and waiting times varied by place of residence and type of product. Lines consumed time, but this chapter does not calculate their total extent or their share of working time. Nor can subsidies and oil revenue be treated as entirely “consumed”: some went toward consumption, some toward investment and imported equipment. To judge their usefulness, specific items of expenditure must be compared with their results. Who made these decisions is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

In July 1959, the leadership of the Chinese Communist Party gathered in the resort town of Lushan, in the mountains of Jiangxi Province. The Great Leap Forward was in its second year, and famine was already beginning in the villages. Defense Minister Marshal Peng Dehuai, himself from a poor peasant family, visited his native district, saw empty granaries, and wrote Mao Zedong a personal letter. Its tone was cautious, almost deferential: the achievements were enormous, but there was “petty-bourgeois fanaticism,” inflated figures, and people at furnaces instead of in the fields. Mao responded with a public denunciation. Peng was declared the head of an “anti-Party clique” and removed from office, and a campaign against “right opportunists” swept the country. The lesson for every Chinese official was unambiguous: telling the truth about failure was dangerous; reporting records was safe. After Lushan, targets were raised still higher, and grain continued to be taken on the basis of fictitious harvests. The worst years of the famine—1960 and 1961—came after the truth had been spoken aloud and punished.

This scene is the key to the section. The preceding sections showed that a planned economy gathered surplus in one place and distributed it by decision of the center. Everything therefore depended on how the center itself was organized: who sat within it, how they learned what was happening, and who could correct them when they were wrong.

### One Pyramid Instead of Many

In the market economies of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries described in Chapters 10 and 11, decisions about surplus were dispersed among thousands of banks, companies, families, and local governments. This produced chaos, crises, and inequality, but it also provided insurance against a single great mistake—one person’s bad decision ruined one firm, not an entire country. The planned economy replaced this multitude with a single pyramid. At the top stood the Party’s political leadership: the Politburo in the USSR and the Politburo Standing Committee in China. Below were the Council of Ministers, Gosplan, and dozens of sectoral ministries: ferrous metallurgy, the coal industry, tractor manufacturing. Lower still were central directorates, trusts, and enterprises. A Party ladder ran parallel to every rung of the economic one: the regional Party committee oversaw the region, and the factory Party committee oversaw the plant.

The key institution of this pyramid was the *nomenklatura* mentioned earlier. It is important to understand how it differed from the bureaucracy familiar from previous chapters—from the Chinese officials who passed examinations (Chapter 6), or from nineteenth-century Prussian officials. There, despite all its flaws, selection followed formal rules, and the official served the law and the state. Here, a candidate was approved by a higher Party authority, and loyalty was the chief criterion alongside professional ability. Anyone in a position of consequence owed that position to those above, not to those below—not to the workers in the factory, the residents of the city, or the buyers of the goods. Information therefore flowed unevenly through the pyramid as well: orders went down, reports went up, and reports are written to look the way their recipient wants them to look.

The Yugoslav communist Milovan Djilas, himself once a member of the country’s top leadership, described this stratum in *The New Class* (1957), for which he served time in prison. His argument, important for this book, was that in a society without private property, control over the surplus itself becomes property: whoever decides where factories, apartments, and dachas go owns them in practice, though not on paper. This can be challenged—the privileges of the *nomenklatura* could not be passed on to children as a factory could, and they were modest compared with the fortunes of capitalists. But Djilas’s question remains: when private property is abolished, does the elite that extracts the surplus disappear—or does it merely change uniform?

### Fear as an Institution

Under Stalin, fear held the pyramid together. According to NKVD archival data studied by both Russian and Western historians after 1991, around 680,000 people were executed during the Great Terror of 1937–1938; hundreds of thousands more were sent to the camps. Among the dead were factory directors, engineers, planners, military commanders, and scientists. In economic terms, this was the destruction of capital in the most literal sense of our framework: experience, connections, and knowledge accumulated over two decades. But fear also distorted the work of those who survived. If failure could bring an accusation of “wrecking,” a rational director would not try anything new, and a statistician would not bring bad figures. The 1937 census, which counted around 162 million people instead of the nearly 180 million expected (partly because of the famine), was declared “defective,” and its leaders were repressed. A system that punished the truth deprived itself of the very information without which a plan—even Lange’s—could not work.

China repeated this in its own form. Lushan was one episode; the Cultural Revolution of 1966–1976 was another, on a far larger scale. Universities closed for years or nearly ceased to function, millions of urban schoolchildren and university students were sent to the countryside for “reeducation,” and scientists and engineers were humiliated and persecuted. If we recall that knowledge and its transmission are one of this book’s five criteria, it is difficult to find a more direct example of destruction in the twentieth century: a country deliberately tore a generation of educated people out of its own life.

### Attempts to Reconfigure the Pyramid

After Stalin’s death, mass terror ended, and the Soviet system began looking for a way to govern without fear. Here we can see how difficult that problem was to solve within a single pyramid. In 1957, Nikita Khrushchev abolished most of the sectoral ministries and transferred the management of industry to the *sovnarkhozy*, regional councils of the national economy. The idea was reasonable: ministries were closed worlds, and “departmentalism” prevented factories in different industries within the same city from cooperating. But the *sovnarkhozy* quickly produced the mirror-image disease—“localism”: each region pulled resources toward itself and reluctantly released them to its neighbors. In 1965, after Khrushchev had been removed, the ministries were restored. The pyramid could be turned sideways, but there was no way out of it: whoever allocated the surplus—an industry or a region—had no reason to send it where it would produce the greatest benefit for the country as a whole.

In the Brezhnev era, “stability of cadres” became the slogan: leaders were replaced less often than under Khrushchev. After the earlier upheavals, this was felt as a relief. By the early 1980s, the leadership had aged noticeably; from late 1982 to early 1985, three general secretaries died in succession. Keeping the same people in senior positions for long periods may have made it harder to change course, but their age cannot be used to assess the effectiveness of every institution or by itself explain the economic slowdown.

### Yugoslavia: Power from Below?

The Yugoslav experiment is interesting because it tried to change not the direction of the pyramid but its foundation. Under laws passed in the 1950s, and especially after the 1965 reform, an enterprise was managed by a workers’ council elected by the employees themselves. The council took part in appointing the director, approved the enterprise plan, and decided what share of income to distribute as earnings and what share to invest in development. Enterprises sold their products on the market and competed with one another. This was perhaps history’s largest-scale attempt to combine social ownership, markets, and workers’ self-management.

The results were mixed. In the 1950s and 1960s, the Yugoslav economy grew rapidly, and living standards were higher than in most countries of the Soviet bloc. Yugoslavs could travel abroad. In 1958, the economist Benjamin Ward proposed a theoretical model in which the existing workforce might prefer to divide income among themselves and limit hiring. This was a possible incentive, not proof that most Yugoslav workers’ councils behaved this way. Unemployment and labor migration did exist, but they cannot be explained solely by the design of the councils: demographics, regional differences, economic policy, and demand for labor abroad also mattered. There was an additional limitation: self-management existed within a one-party state. In the 1970s and 1980s, foreign debt and inflation rose. Researchers debate the contributions of self-management, government policy, and national tensions to this crisis.

### Cybersyn: A Different Flow of Information

The Chilean project deserves mention in this section as well, because Stafford Beer was thinking first and foremost not about computers, but about power. His cybernetic “viable system model” assumed that lower levels should decide as much as possible for themselves, while the center should intervene only when indicators moved beyond acceptable limits. Data did not flow upward so that every decision could be dictated from above, but so that deviations could be noticed in time. As the historian Eden Medina shows in *Cybernetic Revolutionaries* (2011), Beer seriously considered how to involve the workers themselves in the system, rather than managers alone. The only real test came during the truck owners’ strike of October 1972, which paralyzed supplies: the telex network helped the government redirect the few vehicles still operating and maintain supplies to the cities. This is far too little evidence to judge whether the project would have succeeded. But it raised a question that Moscow did not: could a flow of data be used not to strengthen the center, but to make it intervene less?

### Where Was the Feedback?

The Indian economist Amartya Sen linked the absence of major famines in democracies to an independent press and elections; historians debate both his conclusion and the limits of its application. In the case of the USSR in 1932–1933, it cannot be said that reports of the famine simply failed to reach the leadership: reports did arrive, but aid was insufficient and grain procurement continued. In China during the Great Leap Forward, inflated harvest reports and the suppression of criticism distorted the center’s decisions. These are two different failures of feedback, not proof that every famine in a planned economy arises in the same way.

This section shows how important reliable information and the ability to challenge a decision are to a plan. In the cases examined here, political pressure prevented errors from being corrected; in other circumstances, plans were reviewed and changed. Centralization made it possible to direct resources rapidly toward large tasks, but the results depended on the quality of decisions and feedback. What genuinely fostered development in planned economies is the subject of the next section.

## What Fostered Development

In the winter of 1919/20, a decree “On the Elimination of Illiteracy,” signed at the end of December 1919, spread through the villages and towns of Soviet Russia: every resident between the ages of eight and fifty who could not read and write was required to learn. Thousands of “literacy centers” appeared—in peasant huts, at factories, and in barracks. Lessons were taught by the light of kerosene lamps, from homemade primers and often from newspapers. The teachers were frequently yesterday’s students, who had only just mastered literacy themselves. The census of 1897 counted little more than one-fifth of the population of the Russian Empire as literate; in the Soviet census of 1959, almost everyone under fifty described themselves as literate. The census figures can be qualified—someone who could only sign their name and sound out a shop sign was also considered “literate”—but the direction is beyond doubt: in two generations, a country of peasants became a country of readers.

Planned economies invested resources in education, health care, energy, and infrastructure. To assess these investments, their results, costs, and share of total expenditure all matter. This chapter does not calculate the last of these for every period.

### People as Investment

First and foremost came education. The literacy campaign was followed by universal primary schooling, then seven-year schooling, and from the 1950s and 1960s onward, eight- and ten-year schooling. *Rabfaks*—workers’ faculties—were created to prepare adults without a secondary education for university; by the end of the 1930s, their graduates, the children of peasants and workers, had become engineers, directors, and people’s commissars. After the “Sputnik shock” discussed in “Portrait of the Era,” American commissions seriously studied the Soviet school system and its emphasis on mathematics and the natural sciences. In the early 1960s, physics and mathematics boarding schools for gifted children from the provinces opened in Moscow, Leningrad, Kyiv, and Novosibirsk—the Moscow school was founded by the great mathematician Andrey Kolmogorov. By the middle of the century, the Soviet school of mathematics was among the strongest in the world, and its graduates remain prominent in global science today.

Second came health. Demographers estimate that life expectancy in Russia at the end of the nineteenth century was around thirty years, largely because of high infant mortality. By the mid-1960s, according to Soviet statistics, it had approached seventy in the USSR. Better nutrition and sanitation, vaccination, medical care, and the spread of antibiotics all contributed to this result; no single aggregate figure can isolate the contribution of each measure. In 1958, the Soviet virologist Viktor Zhdanov proposed to the World Health Assembly that smallpox be eradicated. According to the WHO’s history of the program, in the early 1960s the USSR supplied around three-quarters of the vaccine the organization received from donor countries. This was a major contribution to the international campaign that culminated in the declaration of smallpox eradication in 1980.

In China, according to estimates discussed by Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen in *Hunger and Public Action* (1989), life expectancy rose from roughly thirty-five to forty years around 1949 to sixty-five by the end of the 1970s. Public health was affected by campaigns against infectious disease, the expansion of primary education, the training of rural health workers—the “barefoot doctors”—as well as changes in nutrition and other conditions. The contribution of each measure cannot be calculated from a single indicator. Drèze and Sen set this long-term improvement beside the catastrophic famine of the Great Leap Forward: both facts belong to the history of the same country.

Third came women. Their mass entry into employment was discussed in “Organization of Labor,” along with the “double shift.” One point should be added: women gained access to higher education and to careers as doctors, engineers, and scientists earlier and more broadly in the USSR and Eastern Europe than in most Western countries. Half the talent of society—kept apart from the production of knowledge through almost every previous era in this book—was admitted to it on this scale for the first time.

### Energy, Steel, and War

Industrialization was discussed in detail in “Energy and Technology”; what matters here is the debate over its causes and cost. In *Farm to Factory*, Robert Allen emphasizes the rapid growth of Soviet industry and the role of large-scale investment, but argues that collectivization was not necessary for that growth. Andrei Cheremukhin, Mikhail Golosov, Sergei Guriev, and Oleg Tsyvinski (2017) constructed a different model, in which continuing the New Economic Policy could have produced comparable growth with greater welfare. These are model results dependent on their initial assumptions, not measured outcomes from an alternative history. They cannot precisely separate the effect of planned investment from urbanization, education, and general technological development. The debate remains open.

The industry built in the 1930s became an important factor in Soviet wartime production. During the second half of 1941, according to Soviet figures, around fifteen hundred major enterprises were evacuated eastward. Chelyabinsk’s “Tankograd” and other factories increased tank output; from 1942 onward, the USSR produced more tanks than Germany. These facts demonstrate the capabilities of the industrial base and of mobilization, but the outcome of the war also depended on the army, the Allies, human losses, supplies, and many other factors.

### Science and Space

The atomic and space programs were military in origin, and their cost belongs to the section “What Was Wasteful Spending.” But their spillovers were real: the first nuclear power station at Obninsk, nuclear icebreakers that lengthened the navigation season along the Northern Sea Route, and communications and weather satellites. The physicists and mathematicians nurtured by these programs advanced fundamental science: the work of Lev Landau, Pyotr Kapitsa, and Andrey Kolmogorov belongs to all humanity.

Leonid Kantorovich is particularly important to this chapter. In 1939, while solving a practical problem for a Leningrad plywood trust—how to allocate work among machines in order to increase output—he laid the foundations of linear programming, the mathematics of optimal resource allocation. For this, he shared the 1975 Nobel Prize in Economics with the American Tjalling Koopmans, becoming the only Soviet economist to receive it. The irony is that Kantorovich’s ideas about “objectively determined valuations”—in essence, calculated prices showing how valuable each resource was within a given plan—struggled for years against suspicion: they sounded too much like the market. A method invented for planning found its widest application in Western corporations, airlines, and armed forces. It is another illustration of the preceding sections: knowledge was created, but spread best where someone was ready to take it up.

### Housing and Infrastructure

In 1957, a decree on mass housing construction was issued, and five-story buildings made of prefabricated concrete panels—the *Khrushchyovki*—began appearing across the country. For many families, a separate apartment with its own bathroom was a marked improvement over a barracks or a room in a communal apartment. According to official figures, more than one hundred million people saw their housing conditions improve between 1956 and 1965. This was a major result, but the figure encompasses different kinds of improvement and does not in itself mean that most of the population received a separate apartment during this period. Alongside housing, the state built metro systems, power lines, water-supply networks, schools, and outpatient clinics; the scale and quality of each should be assessed separately.

### What the Successes Had in Common

These examples show that planning could rapidly direct resources toward mass objectives: literacy, vaccination, electrification, standardized housing, and the training of engineers. Yet even in these areas, local needs and the quality of education and medical care could not be determined entirely from the center. Some benefits were initially available to far from everyone; they spread only gradually. These achievements demonstrate the capabilities of a large state program, but do not in themselves prove its superiority to every other form of organization.

Coordinating the variety of consumer goods, their quality, and demand for new products was more difficult. Here too, however, the general conclusion depends on the country, industry, and period. Other countries also expanded education, health care, and housing construction, using different combinations of state programs and markets. The achievements of planned economies therefore cannot automatically be attributed to coercion, nor can their costs be attributed to every public investment. The means used to gather resources and the decision about where to direct them must be assessed separately.

## What Was Destructive

In August 1940, NKVD officers arrested Academician Nikolai Vavilov in western Ukraine. He was on an expedition, collecting plant samples as he had done throughout his life across five continents. By then, the institute he had founded in Leningrad held one of the world’s largest collections of crop seeds: a living library of wheat, barley, and potatoes from which plant breeders could develop new varieties for decades to come. Vavilov was accused of “sabotage” in agriculture and sentenced to death; the sentence was commuted to imprisonment, and in January 1943 he died of starvation in a Saratov prison. A man who had devoted his life to feeding the world died of hunger. Meanwhile, in besieged Leningrad, the staff of his institute guarded the collection against rats—and several of them starved to death beside sacks of grain they refused to touch.

This story brings together almost every form of destruction discussed in this section. The man who drove Vavilov out of science, the agronomist Trofim Lysenko, promised the authorities rapid harvests without “bourgeois” genetics and won their support. At the August 1948 session of VASKhNIL—the All-Union Academy of Agricultural Sciences—genetics was effectively banned: laboratories were closed, textbooks rewritten, and many biologists dismissed. The ban lasted until the mid-1960s. In the years when the structure of DNA was being discovered and the “Green Revolution” in agriculture was beginning, a country with one of the world’s strongest schools of biology removed itself from the race. A similar, though shorter, campaign in the early 1950s branded cybernetics a “bourgeois pseudoscience.” In the framework of this book, this was destruction in its purest form: not the waste of surplus, but the destruction by political decision of knowledge already accumulated.

### Losses in the Countryside under Collectivization

The famine of 1932–1933 was discussed in “Portrait of the Era.” Here it is important to see the countryside’s other losses as well. In traditional village usage, the word *kulak* referred, among other things, to a moneylender who might demand labor in repayment of a debt, or to a proprietor who lived off hired labor. During “dekulakization,” property was confiscated from households classified by the authorities as kulak, and their families were deported. According to archival records, in 1930–1931 alone, about 1.8 million people were deported to special settlements in the Urals, Siberia, and Kazakhstan. The overall share of middle peasants among those dekulakized across the Soviet Union is unknown: in one OGPU report on the Volga region they made up 15 percent, but this local figure cannot be extrapolated to the entire USSR.

When livestock was collectivized, some owners slaughtered their animals rather than surrender them to the collective farm. Feed shortages and failures of management also contributed to the decline. According to Soviet statistics, the numbers of horses and cattle fell by roughly half between 1929 and 1933. At the time, horses remained the countryside’s principal source of draft power, and tractors could not yet make up for their loss. The country did not regain its late-1920s cattle population until the mid-1950s, while the number of horses never recovered. This was a major loss of productive capital in the course of restructuring agriculture.

The blow to Kazakhstan was especially severe. Nomadic pastoralists were forcibly settled, and their livestock was confiscated. According to historians’ estimates—notably Sarah Cameron’s in *The Hungry Steppe* (2018)—the famine of 1930–1933 there claimed about one and a half million lives, mostly Kazakh: nearly four out of every ten Kazakhs. Hundreds of thousands fled to China and neighboring republics. A way of life that had developed over millennia and was well adapted to the steppe was broken in three years, and for a long time nothing took its place.

### People as Expendable Material

The camp system discussed in “Organization of Labor” was not merely inefficient—it killed. According to the Gulag’s own departmental statistics, about one and a half million people died in camps and penal colonies between 1930 and 1953; many historians consider this figure understated because prisoners who were terminally ill were often released shortly before death so as not to spoil the statistics. Hundreds of thousands more died in special settlements. In 1943–1944, entire peoples—Chechens, Ingush, Crimean Tatars, Kalmyks, Balkars, and others—were deported to Kazakhstan and Central Asia on charges of “aiding the enemy.” In the first years of exile, historians’ calculations based on NKVD documents (by Pavel Polian and others) indicate that between one in five and one in four deportees died.

For our question, a less visible aspect of these losses also matters. Every death was not only a tragedy, but also a missing link in the chain of knowledge transmission: an engineer who trained no students, a peasant who did not pass experience on to his children, a scholar whose intellectual school came to an end. And the fear discussed in “Power and Institutions” destroyed what economists call social capital: trust. People raised in the age of denunciations learned to trust no one beyond their own narrow circle. Researchers comparing levels of trust across countries still find lower figures in former socialist countries; the extent to which this is a legacy of the planned era rather than of earlier history remains disputed. But if we recall the book’s fifth criterion—the costs of coordination and trust—the loss of trust makes every exchange more expensive for decades to come.

There was also the simplest way to lose people: let them leave. According to widely cited estimates, between 1949 and 1961 about two and a half to three million people left East Germany for the West; young people, doctors, engineers, and skilled workers were disproportionately represented among them. The Berlin Wall was built in August 1961 primarily to stop this exodus. The Wall was perhaps the frankest admission that human capital in this system had to be held in place by force.

### China: Chain-Reaction Destruction

During the Great Leap Forward, decisions by central and local authorities caused losses beyond agriculture. Fuel was gathered for backyard furnaces, including by cutting down trees; some houses were dismantled for materials, and metal objects were melted down. Historian Frank Dikötter describes these cases and offers a high estimate of the loss of rural housing. His estimate is disputed, however, and the examples he cites do not establish what share of the country’s total housing stock was destroyed. The scale of losses varied from place to place.

In 1958, as part of China’s campaign against the “Four Pests,” sparrows were killed on a massive scale. The birds ate not only grain but also insects, so the ecological outcome of the campaign may have differed from what was expected. But without regional estimates of bird and pest populations and crop yields, no specific share of the subsequent famine or harvest failure can be attributed to this campaign.

### Cambodia: Taken to the Limit

There is one case in which the attempt to replace the market and money entirely with the will of the center was taken to its logical conclusion. In April 1975, the Khmer Rouge, led by Pol Pot, captured Cambodia’s capital, Phnom Penh, and within days drove its entire population into the countryside. Money was abolished, the national bank building was blown up, and markets, schools, and hospitals were closed. The country was to become one vast agricultural commune, with rice rations distributed from a common pot. Educated people—doctors, teachers, engineers—were regarded as enemies. In less than four years, before the Vietnamese army invaded in January 1979, an estimated one and a half to two million people died—about one-fifth of the population. Most planned economies bore no resemblance to this regime. But it shows where an attempt to destroy at once every mechanism of exchange, every institution, and all of society’s dispersed knowledge—and replace them with a single command—can lead.

### Nature Without a Price

The final form of destruction concerns the accounting of environmental consequences. A departmental plan measured in tons and hectares poorly reflected the cost of polluted water, lost soil, and damage to health. Market prices likewise usually exclude such damage without special rules, while courts and public oversight operate differently from country to country. In the USSR, the state both managed enterprises and was responsible for environmental protection; this made independent assessment of damage more difficult, although environmental policy changed over time.

The largest-scale example is the Aral Sea. Beginning in the 1960s, water from the rivers that fed it—the Amu Darya and Syr Darya—was diverted on a massive scale to irrigate the cotton fields of Central Asia: cotton was the plan’s “white gold.” The sea, once one of the world’s largest lakes, lost most of its volume within a few decades and broke apart into separate bodies of water. Fishing settlements found themselves dozens of kilometers from the water, while wind carried salt and fertilizer residue from the exposed seabed. The gains in cotton were real, but they were paid for with an entire sea and the health of the people around it.

Finally, on April 26, 1986, an accident occurred at Reactor No. 4 of the Chernobyl Nuclear Power Plant. Its causes included features of the reactor’s design and actions taken during a test; responsibility for particular decisions remains a subject of debate. In the first days, the authorities did not give the public all the information known to them, and a May Day parade was held in Kyiv. This is an example of delayed public warning of danger. By itself, it does not show how often information failed to reach the leadership under other circumstances.

### The Common Root

The cases considered here show different forms of loss: violence, the destruction of knowledge, environmental damage, and failed decisions. The center could gather resources for electrification, education, or shared mechanization, but mistaken decisions could likewise affect many people. Accelerated coercion and limited opportunities to scrutinize the authorities’ actions aggravated particular crises. These cases do not yield an overall share of losses for all planned economies, but they show which consequences must be considered alongside output. The next section examines expenditures whose results were open to question.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

Moscow, December 1931: the Cathedral of Christ the Savior is blown up to make way for a Palace of the Soviets more than four hundred meters high. We already told the story of this project in Chapter Twelve: its foundation, its structural frame dismantled for defense needs in 1941, and the Moskva swimming pool built in the excavation pit. Here it serves as an emblem: surplus was spent twice—first to destroy one building and then to lay the foundation of another—without adding anything at all.

The destruction discussed in the previous section is conspicuous. Wasteful spending is less visible and therefore more enduring: it leaves no ruins; it simply dissolves the efforts of millions of people into things that do not make their lives better. In planned economies, it flowed through three main channels: weapons, prestige, and production for the sake of the report.

### Military Expenditure

Military expenditure was one of the major items. It is difficult to estimate precisely: some spending did not appear in the published defense budget. For the early 1980s, the CIA estimated it at roughly 15–17 percent of Soviet GNP; the result depended on how expenditure was defined and converted. The US share was lower in the same years. By the mid-1980s, according to estimates by the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*, the Soviet nuclear arsenal had reached roughly forty to forty-five thousand warheads. These figures show the scale of the military priority, but do not by themselves determine what share of the spending was necessary for defense or how much civilian output could have been obtained instead.

Here we must be honest about both sides. A country that had lived through 1941 had reason to fear a surprise attack, and nuclear deterrence, however one assesses it, kept the superpowers from direct war. As noted in “What Fostered Development,” some military spending produced spillovers, from nuclear energy to satellites. And the race had two participants: the American military-industrial complex, about which President Eisenhower warned in his 1961 farewell address, also absorbed an enormous surplus. But for an economy several times smaller than America’s, competing on equal terms meant devoting an incomparably larger share of life to the race. In the terms of the introduction, this was a classic arms race: each side spent ever more merely to stand still, while the gain for society was, at best, the absence of defeat.

### Construction Projects of the Century

The second channel was prestige projects whose symbolic significance outweighed their economic value. The White Sea–Baltic Canal was built by prisoners in twenty months, between 1931 and 1933, and celebrated in books and newspapers. But haste and economy left it shallow: large ships could not pass through it, and traffic remained modest for a long time. After the war, prisoners laid a railway from Salekhard to Igarka across the permafrost—the “Dead Road,” as it later became known. After Stalin’s death in 1953, construction was abandoned, leaving hundreds of kilometers of roadbed, rails, and derelict locomotives in the tundra. The Baikal–Amur Mainline was proclaimed the “construction project of the century” in 1974; its “golden link” was laid in 1984, but the entire railway did not enter permanent operation until 2003, and for many years it carried far less freight than expected.

Not every large construction project is wasteful spending. Siberian power stations and oil pipelines paid for themselves many times over, and today the Baikal–Amur Mainline carries far more traffic than it did in its opening year. The dividing line is not the size of a project, but who scrutinized it and how. One story that ended differently is revealing. Beginning in the 1970s, preparations were made to divert part of the flow of Siberian rivers to Central Asia—a vast system of canals intended to water the cotton fields and, it was hoped, save the Aral Sea. Scientists and writers, among them Sergei Zalygin and Valentin Rasputin, opposed the plan, and in 1986, at the beginning of *glasnost*, the project was halted. This was a rare case in which surplus found public defenders within a planned system, and the cost of a decision was debated before the money was buried in the ground.

China followed the same path on its own scale. From 1964 to 1980, under the “Third Front” program, a substantial share of state capital investment was directed into the country’s interior, into the mountains of Sichuan, Guizhou, and Gansu. Factories, railways, and defense plants were built far from the coast, which was considered vulnerable to an American or Soviet attack. Economist Barry Naughton, who studied this program in his 1988 article “The Third Front,” showed that many enterprises were placed in gorges, far from raw materials, markets, and one another; after 1980, a substantial share had to be closed or relocated. As in the USSR, defense logic proved stronger than economic logic.

### Production for the Sake of the Report

The third channel was the production of goods designed to fulfill the plan but poorly aligned with demand. In the 1980s, the USSR produced about eight hundred million pairs of shoes a year. Yet some models sat unsold, while people queued for shoes that were actually in demand. The Soviet statistical yearbook for 1986 reports that, in sample inspections by wholesale depots, about 9.6 percent of the footwear inspected was returned for correction or downgraded. This was the share of the inspected batch, not of all footwear produced; it demonstrates a quality problem but does not justify declaring the entire output unnecessary. Similar mismatches between volume and usefulness were discussed in relation to tractors and vegetable storage, but here too, without data on the share lost, one cannot say that the labor embodied in all the output was wasted.

Soviet reform economists called this mismatch between the plan and demand “production for production’s sake.” Kornai, discussed in “Organization of Labor,” explained one of the mechanisms: the soft budget constraint weakened the pressure on an enterprise even when its product sold poorly. This does not mean that machinery, steel, or footwear was useless as a whole: the output of consumer goods and the standard of living rose over many decades. In the early 1990s, economists proposed valuing individual lines of production at world prices and sometimes arrived at negative value added. Such calculations depend on the prices and industries selected; they cannot be extrapolated to the whole of Soviet industry.

The accounting apparatus belongs here as well. Gosplan compiled several thousand material balances, while Gossnab and the ministries handled tens of thousands more items; the country produced millions of types of goods. Reconciling plans consumed labor and time, as did queues for scarce goods. Their total cost has not been calculated here: the number of balances alone cannot tell us what share of the work was useful and what share excessive.

### Why Waste Became Permanent

Wasteful spending existed in every era covered by this book: pyramids, palaces, tournaments, status feasts. In a planned economy, the soft budget constraint could prolong the life of a loss-making enterprise or project: the responsible agency secured a new plan and fresh supplies, while closure meant admitting an error. Yet enterprises and construction projects were also closed or reconsidered; the cancellation of the river-diversion project is one example. The scale of excess expenditure varied across industries and years. The cases presented here explain a possible mechanism, but provide no basis for claiming that the share of wasteful spending rose every year. What follows from all this is the subject of the lesson of the era.

## Lesson of the Era

The Magnitogorsk blast furnace and the Moscow queue for sausage that opened this chapter show different sides of the Soviet economy. One construction project and one queue cannot provide an average assessment of the system. Evaluating its results requires expenditure shares, output per person, and comparisons across years. With these limitations in mind, five conclusions follow.

**First: a state program can rapidly expand access to mass benefits.** Literacy and access to housing increased in the USSR, while access to primary medical care increased in China; the Soviet contribution to the global vaccination campaign against smallpox was also substantial. These achievements are measurable, but the speed with which they were attained should be compared with other countries using the same indicators and taking initial conditions into account. The examples confirm that a state can direct resources toward education and health; they do not show that doing so requires coercion or is possible only in a planned economy.

**Second: the allocator of surplus cannot be abolished—it can only be replaced.** Planned economies stripped money and private owners of the right to decide where surplus would go, and in this they succeeded: large private fortunes disappeared. But the vacant place was taken by the plan, the agency, the nomenklatura, the queue, *blat*, and the foreign-currency store. Each of them allocated resources, and each was less visible from the outside than a price. Milovan Djilas’s question—whether control over surplus becomes a new form of property—proved to concern more than the privileges of those in charge. It is about whether society can see who decides and why. Where it cannot, extraction returns even if all the former extractors have been abolished.

**Third: the chief resource of any economy is truthful information, and fear destroys it.** The debate among Mises, Lange, and Hayek is often retold as an argument about whether the center had enough computing power. Soviet and Chinese history shows that the problem ran deeper. The equations might have been solved; what could not be obtained was honest input data. A director understated his capacity to secure an easier plan; a worker avoided exceeding the norm so it would not be raised; a commune chairman reported an unprecedented harvest to avoid being branded a “Rightist deviationist”; a census that produced an inconvenient figure was declared “defective.” Peng Dehuai at Lushan and the silence after Chernobyl are two points on the same curve. The famines of 1932–1933 and 1959–1961 had different causes. In the USSR, poor harvests were compounded by procurements that did not correspond to actual reserves and by inadequate relief for the starving; in China, false crop reports intensified the disaster. Both catastrophes demonstrated the price of suppressed feedback. Hence a conclusion to which this book will return: any system of allocation is only as good as the safety with which bad news can be reported within it. Projects such as Glushkov’s OGAS or Chile’s Cybersyn are interesting precisely because they attempted to build a different flow of information—but they too ran up against power rather than technology: who would see the data, and who would be able to change anything on its basis.

**Fourth: it is important to reconsider a failed project in time.** The soft budget constraint described by Kornai could weaken pressure on enterprises that produced poor results. Yet projects were reconsidered even in a planned economy: the diversion of Siberian rivers was canceled in 1986. Individual failed construction projects and unsalable goods demonstrate the risk, but without comparing all projects one cannot claim that most expenditure was useless or that its share rose without interruption.

**Fifth: gathering resources and scrutinizing how they are spent are different tasks.** The Yugoslav experience shows that worker self-management can be combined with a market, but its results also depended on banks, government policy, and regional differences. Household plots show that personal interest mattered; its contribution cannot be calculated from the shares of land and output alone. To evaluate any system, we need to know who makes decisions, how results are checked, and what share is accounted for by successful and unsuccessful cases.

Where did the resources of the planned economies go? The data show large investments in industry, education, housing, and health care, as well as substantial military expenditure. There were also severe losses from coercion, famine, failed projects, and shortages. But this chapter does not possess comparable calculations of the “developmental,” “destructive,” and “wasteful” shares for different decades. We therefore cannot draw an exact curve for these shares or claim that one of them declined steadily. It is more reliable to describe separately the measured outcome of each activity and the costs associated with it.

These conclusions have limits. The number of victims, military expenditure, and the scale of the informal economy are estimated by different methods. Comparisons among countries must take into account initial income, the consequences of wars, the structure of the economy, and external threats. State programs existed in market economies too, while planned economies differed from one another. The cases examined here demonstrate the importance of reliable information, the ability to correct mistakes, and incentives to work, but do not yield a single figure for the efficiency of planning across all countries and eras.

How many people lived and produced in planned economies, how did their income and life expectancy change, and how far did their fortunes diverge from those of their neighbors? The answers are in the section of figures.

## Figures

The statistics of planned economies are treacherous in their own way. They contained more figures than anywhere else: Gosplan counted everything that could be weighed. But the counting was done by those responsible for fulfilling the plan, rubles did not reflect value, and inconvenient data were classified or declared “defective.” Almost all the figures below are therefore estimates, and for many of them the differences among estimates are given.

**People.** At their peak, from the 1950s through the 1970s, countries with planned economies were home, by a simple count, to about a third of humanity—by 1980, roughly one and a half billion of the world’s four and a half billion people—most of them in China. The cost of building these systems in human lives is estimated as follows. The famine of 1932–1933 in the USSR killed between five and a half million and more than eight million people: the lower estimate is by Robert Davies and Stephen Wheatcroft, the upper by Michael Ellman. Of these deaths, Sarah Cameron estimates that about one and a half million occurred in Kazakhstan, including some 1.3 million Kazakhs—more than a third of the people. During dekulakization in 1930–1931 alone, about 1.8 million people were deported. During the Great Terror, NKVD archives record about 680,000 executions. Some eighteen million people passed through Gulag camps and penal colonies between 1930 and 1953, including repeat sentences—an archival estimate cited by Oleg Khlevniuk and other historians; according to Khlevniuk and other archival researchers, about two and a half million people were imprisoned at any one time in the early 1950s. Estimates of the death toll from China’s Great Leap Forward famine range from fifteen to forty-five million, depending on the method; many demographers put it at around thirty million (Basil Ashton and his coauthors, Yang Jisheng), while a number of historians, including Cormac Ó Gráda, consider Frank Dikötter’s upper estimate too high. In Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge regime killed between one and a half and two million people in less than four years—about one-fifth of the country’s population.

**Growth.** Soviet growth rates are a matter of dispute. According to CIA estimates, Soviet GNP grew by roughly five to six percent a year in the 1950s, about five percent in the 1960s, about three percent in the 1970s, and about two percent in the first half of the 1980s. Soviet economist Grigory Khanin arrived at markedly lower figures, especially for the later decades. These series show slowing growth despite continued large investments; researchers assess both its causes and exact pace differently. William Easterly and Stanley Fischer attributed part of the slowdown to declining returns on invested capital.

**Income per person.** In Angus Maddison’s tables, measured in “1990 international dollars,” the USSR had an income per person of about $2,840 in 1950—roughly one-third of the American level. By 1973, it had risen to $6,060, or nearly two-fifths of the US level: these were the system’s best years. By 1990, it had reached only about $6,900, while the American figure rose from $16,700 to $23,200 over the same period. The gap, which had narrowed for a quarter of a century, widened again to its former size over the next seventeen years. In the same tables, China started at $450 in 1950 and had less than $1,000 by Mao’s death in 1976, after the Great Leap Forward and the Cultural Revolution—only slightly more than the level of Western Europe at the beginning of the sixteenth century.

**Steel and bread.** In 1928, the USSR produced about four million tons of steel; in 1940, about eighteen million; and by the end of the 1980s, more than 160 million tons—almost twice as much as the United States. But the country that had been one of the world’s largest grain exporters in 1913 began buying grain in 1963, and in some years in the early 1980s imported more than forty million tons. In those same years, it produced about eight hundred million pairs of shoes annually—more than any other country—while people queued for imported boots.

**Knowledge and health.** According to the 1897 census, about one-fifth of the Russian Empire’s inhabitants were literate; according to the 1959 census, the figure among Soviet citizens aged nine to forty-nine was about 98 percent. China’s life expectancy, according to figures cited by Jean Drèze and Amartya Sen, rose from roughly thirty-five to forty years around 1949 to sixty-five by the end of the 1970s; this is an estimate for the entire country, not only for those who received state assistance. According to official figures, more than one hundred million people in the USSR saw their housing conditions improve between 1956 and 1965. In the Russian SFSR, male life expectancy, which had been about sixty-four years in the mid-1960s, fell to roughly sixty-two by the early 1980s. This figure applies to men in the Russian SFSR; it does not show that every Soviet health indicator declined at the same rate.

**Defense.** The CIA estimated Soviet military expenditure in the 1980s at roughly 15–17 percent of GNP; some later estimates reach as high as one-quarter. The US figure in the same years was about six percent. By the mid-1980s, according to estimates by the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*, the USSR possessed about forty to forty-five thousand nuclear warheads, the United States just over twenty thousand, and the world as a whole about seventy thousand—a stockpile many times greater than any conceivable military requirement.

**Neighbors.** According to different estimates, between two and a half and three million people—roughly one resident in six—left East Germany between 1949 and 1961, before the Berlin Wall was built. In 1991, after reunification, labor productivity in the eastern states stood, depending on definitions and estimates, at roughly one-third to 45 percent of the West German level. This difference reflected the condition of enterprises at the time of transition and the consequences of the transition itself; it does not isolate the effect of planning. Yugoslavia grew rapidly in the 1950s and 1960s, but by the early 1970s about a million of its citizens were working abroad, and in 1989 inflation exceeded one thousand percent. These figures likewise do not isolate the contribution of self-management from other causes.

Taken together, these series show rising literacy, output, and income per person; slowing growth in the later decades; a heavy military burden; and divergent trends in health. They must be compared within the same country and period, without turning a single figure into a description of an entire system. A number of market economies also grew rapidly after the war; they are the subject of the next chapter.

## Limits of Calculation

The cost of the White Sea–Baltic Canal cannot be measured in money alone. Tens of thousands of prisoners worked on its construction in 1931–1933; their number changed from month to month. Archival data on the average population of Belbaltlag give about 64,000 prisoners in 1931, 99,000 in 1932, and 85,000 in 1933. These figures represent the camp population, not a constant number of people working directly along the canal route. Mortality estimates differ and depend on which camps and years are included. It is therefore impossible to multiply the peak population by all twenty months and then convert the result into numbers of schools or “working lives.” It is more reliable to name the consequences that are independently confirmed: forced labor, the deaths of construction workers, and the limited capacity of the canal that was built.

Defense expenditure likewise requires comparable bases. The estimate of 15–17 percent of GNP reflects the definition of expenditure and the prices used by CIA analysts for the early 1980s. The share changed between 1960 and 1990, and other researchers arrived at different figures. The American figure of six percent cannot be declared a sufficient level for the USSR: threats, military structures, prices, and economies differed. The share of expenditure in GNP is also not the same as the share of labor employed. Without these data, we cannot calculate how many people might have been transferred to agriculture or construction, or how much grain or how many apartments they would have produced. We can establish the scale of the priority given to defense, but not derive the precise value of forgone civilian goods from a single percentage of GNP.
