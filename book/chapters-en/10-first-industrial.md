# Chapter 10. The First Industrial Revolution

## Portrait of the Era

Derbyshire, 1771. In the valley of the River Derwent, beside the village of Cromford, where lead had previously been mined and sheep grazed, a five-story building of rough stone is rising. Its many windows are large: light is needed inside. A stream runs beside the building; its water is channeled to an enormous wheel, and through shafts and belts the wheel turns hundreds of spindles at once. The owner is Richard Arkwright, a former barber and wigmaker from Lancashire, a man without education or pedigree. Two years earlier, he had patented a spinning machine nicknamed the “water frame” because it was powered by water. Whether he invented it himself or skillfully assembled other people’s ideas was disputed even in his lifetime, and in 1785 a court revoked his patent. But what he built at Cromford was unquestionably new.

It was a factory in the modern sense: not a workshop where a few craftspeople worked with their hands, but a building constructed around machinery and a single source of power. Work followed the clock, not the sun or anyone’s inclination. The gates opened and closed at the sound of a bell, and latecomers were fined. Labor was scarce in the remote valley, so Arkwright built housing for his workers—whole streets of stone houses that still stand—hired entire families, and readily employed children: their fingers handled thread deftly, and they could be paid less. He did not sell his machine. Unlike many inventors, he became an industrialist himself and sold yarn. By the end of his life, the former barber had been knighted and left a fortune immense by the standards of the age. Today, the Derwent Valley Mills are a UNESCO World Heritage Site, like the Egyptian temples and Roman aqueducts discussed in earlier chapters.

Let us move forward several decades and a few dozen miles northwest. Manchester in the 1840s was a city contemporaries called “Cottonopolis.” In eighty years, it had grown from a market town of several tens of thousands into a metropolis of hundreds of thousands. Above the roofs stood a forest of factory chimneys; along the canals and the new railway to Liverpool came bales of cotton from America, while bolts of cloth departed for sale around the world. A young German, Friedrich Engels, the son of a co-owner of one of the local mills, walked through the working-class districts and recorded what he saw: cellars inhabited by entire families, open sewers running down the middle of streets, children working twelve-hour days. His book *The Condition of the Working Class in England* appeared in 1845. During the same years, in those same districts, other observers saw an abundance of cheap cloth unlike anything before it—cloth that nearly everyone could afford.

Both pictures are true, and this entire chapter is about how they coexisted.

What happened between Cromford and Manchester? In brief, humanity for the first time put to work on a mass scale energy stored in the earth millions of years earlier. In the previous chapter, England was already burning coal in fireplaces, forges, and breweries. Now coal began to turn machines. The crucial step was taken by the Scottish instrument maker James Watt: in 1769, he patented a steam engine with a separate condenser that consumed several times less fuel than the older pumps used to drain mines. Together with the Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton, he began producing engines in the 1770s, and by the 1780s had taught them to turn a wheel. A factory no longer needed a mountain stream: it could be built in a city, close to coal, workers, and a port. In 1830, the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened, the first to carry passengers and freight on a timetable using locomotives alone. Twenty years later, thousands of kilometers of track covered Britain.

The scale of the change is clearest in cotton. Around 1760, Britain imported roughly two and a half million pounds of raw cotton a year; by the end of the 1780s, more than twenty million, and by the middle of the nineteenth century, hundreds of millions—figures from Phyllis Deane and W. A. Cole’s classic *British Economic Growth*. Cotton cloth, once a luxury imported from India, became the cheapest clothing in the world. And within a few decades, the Indian weavers whose fabrics were described in Chapter 6 as a glory of world trade lost out to the machines of Lancashire—partly in fair competition, partly because of tariffs and colonial power.

This era is conventionally called the Industrial Revolution. The name was coined in hindsight, and historians still debate how accurate it is. As Chapter 9 noted, average income in Britain rose slowly—by fractions of a percent per year—and until the middle of the nineteenth century, most English people worked not in factories at all, but on the land, in workshops, and in domestic service. Even around 1830, steam engines supplied British industry with roughly as much energy as waterwheels, according to John Kanefsky’s calculations. The revolution advanced not as an explosion, but as a slow fire spreading from one industry to the next. Yet the difference between 1760 and 1850 is astonishing: for the first time in history, output per person grew faster than the population and continued to grow decade after decade without striking the Malthusian ceiling discussed in previous chapters.

And once again the book’s central question arises: where did the surplus go? This time, the answer is unusual. A substantial share of it—not all, but more than in any previous era—went not into palaces, wars, or the treasury, but into new machines, factories, canals, mines, and railways. Manufacturers such as Arkwright lived richly, but reinvested the lion’s share of their profits in the business: competition demanded it, while few banks were willing to lend to factories. Economists would later call this reinvestment, and here lies the difference from a Roman senator or Spanish grandee who put wealth into land and prestige.

But the same question has a darker side. While profits flowed into machinery, workers’ earnings scarcely rose for decades. Historian Robert Allen calls this “Engels’ pause”: by his calculations, from the 1780s to the 1840s, output per worker in Britain rose markedly while real wages remained nearly flat; only after the middle of the century did wages begin to follow productivity. The surplus existed and went into development—but at first, development belonged to the few. Why machines appeared specifically in England, who stood at them, how the Luddites rebelled against machinery, and how Adam Smith and David Ricardo tried to explain the new world—these are the subjects of the sections that follow.

## Energy and Technology

The Industrial Revolution is often imagined as a single invention—the steam engine. In reality, it was a chain in which each link pulled the next. Speed up one task and a bottleneck formed elsewhere; inventors then rushed toward it, because that was where the money lay. This chain is clearest in textiles.

Let us begin with the bottleneck. Weaving a piece of cloth requires yarn, and for centuries yarn was spun by hand—on a spinning wheel, one thread at a time, usually by women and children in village homes. A weaver worked faster than the spinners, and several spinners were needed for every weaver. In 1733, the Lancashire craftsman John Kay devised the “flying shuttle”: with a pull of a handle, the weaver shot the shuttle across the loom instead of passing it by hand. Weaving became faster, and the shortage of yarn more acute. Thread grew more expensive—which meant that whoever learned to spin faster would grow rich.

The answer came in the 1760s. The weaver James Hargreaves built the “jenny,” a hand-powered spinning machine on which one worker turned eight spindles at once, and later dozens. The machine was simple, made of wood, and small enough for a home; within a couple of decades, thousands were in use across England. Next came Arkwright’s water frame, encountered in the opening scene: it made strong but somewhat coarse yarn and required waterpower, which meant a factory. In 1779, Samuel Crompton combined the virtues of both machines in the “mule”—the name meant just that, a hybrid of two breeds. The mule produced fine, strong yarn from which muslin could be woven, something previously within the reach of Indian artisans alone. Crompton did not take out a patent; he revealed his secret to manufacturers in return for a sum raised by subscription and, by his own bitter account, received a mere pittance—only in 1812 did Parliament award him five thousand pounds. The invention quickly became common property—and perhaps for that very reason spread so widely.

The speed of the change is shown by a calculation textile historians—Harold Catling, followed by Robert Allen—cite as a benchmark. Spinning one hundred pounds of cotton by hand, in the Indian manner, required about fifty thousand hours of labor by their estimate. On Crompton’s mule it took about two thousand. On a steam-powered mule at the end of the eighteenth century, about three hundred; on Richard Roberts’s self-acting mule of 1825, fewer than one hundred and fifty. These are estimates and should not be taken as accurate to the hour, but no one disputes the order of magnitude: a several-hundredfold difference in half a century. No previous industry had seen a leap like it.

The bottleneck now shifted back to the weaver. Yarn became abundant, and around the turn of the nineteenth century handloom weavers briefly prospered: there was more work than workers. In 1785, the country clergyman Edmund Cartwright, who by his own account had never before seen a loom, patented a power loom. But the machine remained temperamental for a long time, and weaving factories did not spread on a mass scale until the 1820s and 1830s. Then Britain’s hundreds of thousands of handloom weavers were transformed within a single generation from members of a prosperous craft into practitioners of one of the poorest trades in the country. We will return to their story in the section on destruction.

The bottleneck moved beyond Britain as well. The machines demanded ever more raw cotton, and separating the seeds by hand was slow. In 1793, the American Eli Whitney built the cotton gin, which performed this work many times faster. An invention harmless in itself made it profitable to grow cotton across vast expanses of the American South—and enslaved people grew it. The number of enslaved people in the United States rose from roughly seven hundred thousand in 1790 to four million in 1860. A machine in Lancashire and a plantation in Mississippi were links in the same chain; this must be remembered when we calculate where the surplus went.

The second chain was iron. In the previous chapter, we saw how Abraham Darby learned to smelt pig iron with coke. But cast iron is brittle, while machines, rails, and bridges require strong wrought iron, which was still produced slowly and with charcoal. In 1784, Henry Cort patented puddling—a process in which molten pig iron was stirred in a furnace to burn off excess carbon, then passed through rollers. Now the entire iron industry could run on fossil fuel. By widely cited estimates, British pig-iron output rose from several tens of thousands of tons around 1760 to more than two million by 1850—a manyfold increase. In 1779, the world’s first large cast-iron bridge rose over the River Severn at Coalbrookdale; it still stands, and the neighboring village bears its name: Ironbridge.

Iron and steam needed each other. Watt, with his separate condenser, struggled for years to make the piston fit tightly inside the cylinder: the cylinders came out uneven, and gaps had to be plugged with felt and oiled tow. Help came from the ironmaster John Wilkinson, who in 1774 patented a machine for boring cannon barrels precisely—and began using it to bore cylinders for Watt. Watt wrote with delight that Wilkinson’s cylinder deviated from a true circle nowhere by more than the thickness of an old shilling. Military technology had turned civilian, as it would many times again in this book. In the 1780s, Watt fitted his engine with a mechanism that converted the rocking of the beam into rotation. From that moment, steam could turn not only a pump, but any shaft—in a spinning mill, a brewery, or a flour mill.

Watt’s engine operated at low pressure: atmospheric air pushed the piston, while steam merely created a vacuum beneath it. Watt distrusted high pressure—boilers often exploded in those days—and while his patent, extended by Parliament until 1800, remained in force, building other kinds of engine was risky. The episode is still cited in debates over patents: did the patent protect the inventor, or hold the industry back for a quarter-century? Michele Boldrin and David Levine argue for the latter in *Against Intellectual Monopoly* (2008); their opponents reply that without long protection Boulton would not have invested so much money in the enterprise. As soon as the patent expired, the Cornishman Richard Trevithick built a compact high-pressure engine—light enough to mount on wheels. In 1804, his locomotive became the first to haul wagons of iron along rails in South Wales. And in 1829, George and Robert Stephenson’s *Rocket* won the locomotive trials held before the Liverpool and Manchester Railway opened.

The third chain consisted of machines that made machines. Everything discussed above was initially assembled by hand, from wood and metal shaped with a file. Every machine was slightly different, and only its maker could repair it. Around 1800, the London craftsman Henry Maudslay perfected the slide-rest screw-cutting lathe, which could cut the same thread again and again. From his workshop came James Nasmyth, inventor of the steam hammer, and Joseph Whitworth, who later proposed a uniform standard for screw threads. It sounds dull, but the standard was precisely what allowed a screw from one factory to fit a nut from another. This is what economists call a reduction in coordination costs: less fitting, fewer disputes, more exchange.

Finally, there were the routes. Goods had to be transported. In 1761, as we saw in the previous chapter, the Duke of Bridgewater opened a canal from his coal mines at Worsley to Manchester—and according to contemporary accounts, the price of coal in the city fell by roughly half. It was followed by “canal mania”: by the 1830s, thousands of kilometers of canals cut across England, and a single horse could tow a barge carrying a load that would have required dozens of carts on the road. Then came the railways, and by 1850 Britain already had around ten thousand kilometers of track.

What united all these chains? Energy. In previous chapters, every increase in production eventually ran up against the land: more firewood meant less arable acreage; more horses meant less grain. Coal removed this contradiction. British output rose from roughly fifteen million tons in 1800, according to the estimate of coal historian Michael Flinn, to more than fifty or sixty million by mid-century, according to Roy Church’s estimates; the first official figure, for 1854, was about sixty-five million. In *Energy and Civilization*, Vaclav Smil emphasizes that Britain long remained the world’s largest consumer of coal, with several times more energy available per person than in any previous society. Muscle ceased to be the principal motor; human beings increasingly became not the engine, but the operators beside it.

Why did all this happen here? In *The British Industrial Revolution in Global Perspective* (2009), Robert Allen offers a simple calculation. Hargreaves’s jenny cost money but saved labor. In England, where a woman worker earned comparatively high wages while coal and iron were cheap, the machine paid for itself quickly. In France, where labor was cheaper, it did so much more slowly. In India, almost never. It therefore made sense to invent where hands were expensive and fuel was cheap; machines then grew cheaper, and within a few decades it became profitable to install them everywhere. Allen’s critics—Jane Humphries and Benjamin Schneider, for example—challenge his evidence for high spinners’ wages, while Joel Mokyr observes that prices explain where people searched, but not why they found. The debate remains unresolved, and we will return to it.

The question of this book sounds especially clear here. The era’s surplus—the profits from the first machines—went chiefly into more machines, new workshops, mines, and canals. Each new machine made the next one cheaper, while cheap coal reduced the cost of the iron from which it was built. This is what the introduction called the developmental use of surplus, almost in its purest form: income became the capacity to produce more in an hour of labor. But the chain also had links that museums leave out: an enslaved plantation at one end, a ruined weaver and a damp cellar at the other. The next section turns to the people who stood at these machines.

## Organization of Labor

Before the factory, English wool and cotton were already big business, but business scattered across hundreds of villages. A cloth merchant bought raw material and distributed it among homes: carding in one cottage, spinning in another, weaving in a third. A week later, he made the same rounds, collected the finished work, and paid by the piece—for a pound of yarn, for a length of cloth. Historians call this the “putting-out system,” or domestic industry. The whole family worked: the father at the loom, the mother and daughters at the spinning wheel, small children sorting and cleaning wool. They worked long hours, but kept their own schedule. The custom of “Saint Monday”—after Sunday’s rest, workers returned to the loom only on Tuesday, then made up the time by working late toward the end of the week—was described by many contemporaries, and not only in England.

The system had its virtues: the family stayed together, work could be combined with tending the garden and making hay, and no one was breathing down anyone’s neck. But it also had its faults, especially from the merchant’s point of view. He could not see how people worked or make them work faster. Small amounts of yarn were concealed or dampened to increase their weight; cloth was delivered late; during the harvest everyone went into the fields. The merchant paid for raw materials that lay in other people’s homes for weeks. Most importantly, a spinning wheel and handloom could be placed in a cottage; a waterwheel or steam engine could not.

The factory gathered work into one building, bringing with it something previously known almost nowhere outside the monastery, army, and navy: the discipline of the clock. In his famous essay “Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism” (1967), historian Edward Palmer Thompson showed how the very perception of time was changing. Peasants and craftspeople measured work by the task: milk the cows, finish a length of cloth, bring in the hay before it rained. The factory measured it in hours. The machine turned at the same speed for everyone, and one person’s lateness halted the work of others. Hence the bell at the gate, fines for minutes of lateness, prohibitions on talking, whistling, and leaving one’s place, and rules posted on the wall. To the first generation of factory workers, this seemed almost like slavery; many left, and manufacturers complained about turnover no less than idleness.

Who, then, went into the factory? At first, those who had no choice. Arkwright, as we have seen, hired whole families. Samuel Greg, who built Quarry Bank Mill near the village of Styal outside Manchester in 1784, took “parish apprentices”—children from workhouses whom parishes were glad to send away rather than feed. The children lived together in a house beside the mill, received food, clothing, and a little schooling, and in return worked for years without wages. By the standards of the time, Greg was considered a good master; elsewhere, such children were treated far worse.

Child labor was not invented by the factory: children had always worked in the countryside. What was new was the long day in one place, under supervision, among machines that maimed the inattentive. In *Childhood and Child Labour in the British Industrial Revolution* (2010), Jane Humphries drew on hundreds of workers’ autobiographies from the period to show that in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries children began working earlier than their parents had—often at eight or nine years old, sometimes even younger. According to economist Douglas Galbi’s calculations, workers under eighteen made up between one-half and two-thirds of the labor force in the cotton mills of Manchester, Stockport, and Preston in 1816–1819; by 1835, they still accounted for about two-fifths. Adult men in spinning mills were often employers themselves: a mule spinner was paid by output and hired his own assistants, frequently his own children, paying them out of his earnings. The family workshop had moved inside the factory, hierarchy and all.

Women, too, made up a large share of textile workers—and generally earned between one-half and two-thirds of men’s wages, according to historian Joyce Burnette’s calculations. For many of them, factory wages were the first money that belonged personally to them rather than to a father or husband. Contemporary moralists saw this as a threat to the family; the women themselves often spoke of independence in letters and memoirs. Both sides were partly right, and this debate will recur in different forms in the chapters ahead.

People also worked longer. In *Time and Work in England, 1750–1830* (2000), Hans-Joachim Voth used witness testimony from court cases, in which people casually mentioned what they had been doing at a given hour, to estimate that the average working year in England grew by roughly one-fifth over those eighty years—from fewer than 2,800 hours to more than 3,300. Saint Mondays and numerous holidays gradually disappeared. The estimate rests on ingenious but indirect evidence, and historian Judith Bennett’s calculations using the same method show a much smaller increase for women; other sources nevertheless confirm the direction of change. The Dutch historian Jan de Vries called this shift the “industrious revolution”: families chose to work more because they now had things on which to spend their earnings—tea, sugar, calico, and crockery. Industriousness was partly a choice and partly imposed: the factory simply offered no short working day.

It is important not to exaggerate the factory’s reach. Even in the census of 1851, Britain had more domestic servants than cotton workers; most people still labored on the land, in small workshops, on construction sites, and in shops. But the factory set a pattern that others moved toward. It was here that a profession scarcely known before came of age: the manager. In *The Genesis of Modern Management* (1965), Sidney Pollard showed how the first generation of manufacturers groped their way toward inventing accounting, supervision, and systems of penalties and rewards—because for the first time they had to manage hundreds of people who were neither soldiers, serfs, nor members of a family.

Adam Smith, who wrote *The Wealth of Nations* (1776) just as these changes were beginning, opened the book with his famous example of a pin workshop: ten people, dividing the work into roughly eighteen operations, made about forty-eight thousand pins a day, he wrote, whereas working alone each could scarcely have made twenty. The division of labor, in Smith’s view, was the principal source of the wealth of nations. Yet the same Smith—something remembered less often—warned in Book V that a person who spent a lifetime performing a few simple operations risked becoming dull-witted, and proposed that society provide the children of ordinary people with basic schooling for a small fee. He saw the gain in productivity and the harm to the worker at the same time.

Some tried to prove that the one did not necessarily entail the other. Robert Owen, who managed the mills at New Lanark in Scotland from 1800, shortened the working day, stopped employing small children, opened a school for them—one of the first in Britain for workers’ children—and established a store where goods were sold at close to cost. The factory remained profitable, and visitors came from across Europe to see it, among them the future Russian emperor Nicholas I. Owen’s partners regarded his spending as excessive, and he had to find new, more patient ones. New Lanark showed that decent conditions did not necessarily lead to ruin. But most manufacturers reasoned differently: if your neighbor keeps children at the machines for twelve hours and you do not, his yarn is cheaper than yours.

Change therefore came not through goodwill, but through law. The acts of 1802 and 1819 concerning children in cotton mills were scarcely enforced: there was no one to inspect the factories. The turning point came with the Factory Act of 1833. It barred children under nine from textile mills, limited the working day of children under thirteen to nine hours, and—most importantly—created a corps of government factory inspectors, initially just four for the entire country. In 1847, the Ten Hours Act limited the working day of women and young people in textile mills; because a mill could not operate without them, men’s working day was shortened in practice as well. Meanwhile, workers themselves were learning to organize: the Combination Acts—laws against “combinations,” or trade unions—passed in 1799–1800 were repealed in 1824, beginning a long series of attempts, often defeated but never abandoned, to bargain collectively with employers over wages.

Where, then, did the surplus created by these hands go? In the terms of this book, the picture is double-edged. The organization itself—gathering work under one roof, dividing it into operations, placing a person beside a machine—was a powerful developmental step: output per hour of labor increased many times over. But during the first decades, the worker received little of the gain, while the price—a long day, a childhood beside a machine, the loss of control over one’s own time—was high. Almost everything above wages went into profit and then back into machinery. This was better than the palaces and wars of earlier eras, but it was not yet a gain for the majority. For the surplus to begin reaching those who produced it required inspectors, schools, trade unions, and almost half a century. The next section examines how exchange, money, and the credit on which factories were built changed in the meantime.

## Exchange and Money

Anglesey, an island off the coast of Wales, 1787. At Parys Mountain copper mine, the largest in Europe at the time, payday has arrived. The mine owner Thomas Williams pays his miners not in royal coins—there are simply not enough of them—but in his own copper tokens. On one side of the token is the profile of a druid crowned with oak leaves; on the other, the company’s promise to exchange it for real money in London, Liverpool, or on the island itself. The tokens have been struck by machines, beautifully and precisely; they are difficult to counterfeit, and local shopkeepers and innkeepers accept them readily. A private company is doing what the state cannot: supplying people with small change.

The scene looks like a curiosity, but behind it lies one of the era’s principal monetary problems. In the previous chapter, we saw how Britain had put its large-denomination money in order: reliable government debt, the Bank of England, the gold guinea. But a factory operated differently from an estate or a merchant house. A landowner paid laborers by the season; a merchant settled bills of exchange worth hundreds of pounds. Every week, a manufacturer had to give hundreds of people a few shillings and pence each. Yet the Royal Mint struck copper reluctantly and in small quantities: from the 1770s until 1797, almost no copper coins were issued, and old, worn pennies and crude counterfeits circulated instead. According to contemporary complaints, the fakes may nearly have outnumbered the genuine coins.

Entrepreneurs improvised as best they could. Some, like Williams, issued tokens—thousands of varieties appeared between 1787 and 1797, from factories, mines, shops, and towns. In *Good Money* (2008), economist George Selgin treats this episode as a rare experiment in private coinage and argues that on the whole it worked fairly well: good tokens drove out bad ones because people quickly learned whose promises to trust. Other manufacturers took a worse path. They paid not in money but in vouchers for their own shop, or directly in goods—flour, cloth, beer—often at inflated prices. This practice was called the “truck system,” from an old English word for barter. The worker was tied to the employer’s shop as a peasant had once been tied to the lord’s mill: part of his earnings returned to the master a second time. The Truck Act of 1831 required most industries to pay in legal coin, but enforcement was weak, and complaints about company shops continued into the second half of the century.

The problem of small change was eventually solved by the same technology that drove the spinning machines. Matthew Boulton, Watt’s partner, built a mint in Birmingham with steam-powered presses—at first for tokens and East India Company contracts; in 1797, he received a government order for copper pennies and twopenny pieces. Heavy and broad-rimmed, they were nicknamed “cartwheels.” Around 1810, steam presses were installed in the Royal Mint’s new building in London as well. Coinage, made with a hammer for two thousand years and with a hand-powered screw press for the preceding century and a half, became industrial production.

### Paper Instead of Gold

The year 1797 brought another, much larger event. Britain had been at war with revolutionary France for four years; in February, a small French force landed on the coast of Wales, and panic swept the country. People rushed to exchange banknotes for gold, and the Bank of England’s reserves dwindled. At the end of February, William Pitt the Younger’s government ordered the suspension of gold convertibility. It was supposed to be brief. The suspension—known as the “Bank Restriction”—lasted almost a quarter-century.

The result was what we would now call paper, or fiat, money—money sustained not by metal, but by trust in its issuer. The Bank of England could lend more to the government, and Britain financed its war against Napoleon without declaring bankruptcy, as its enemies and allies repeatedly did. But prices rose markedly during the war years, and by 1810 gold cost considerably more in banknotes than it had before the war. This began what historians of economic thought call the “bullion controversy.” David Ricardo, a stockbroker who had made a fortune trading government securities, published the pamphlet *The High Price of Bullion, a Proof of the Depreciation of Bank Notes* in 1810. His argument was simple: the Bank had issued too much paper, so its value had fallen—and that was the rise in prices. His opponents replied that the war, failed harvests, and military expenditure abroad were to blame. The parliamentary Bullion Committee of 1810 largely agreed with Ricardo, but Parliament rejected its conclusions in 1811. The dispute over whether prices rise because there is too much money or too little merchandise has never ended and will return in several chapters of this book.

After victory, Britain returned to metal. The Coinage Act of 1816 made gold the sole full standard of value, and in 1821 the Bank of England resumed convertibility. The return was painful: the quantity of money contracted, prices fell, and debtors—farmers and small manufacturers—repaid debts contracted in cheap money with money that had grown dear. But thereafter the pound remained “as good as gold” for almost a century, and this trust would become one of the foundations of the first globalization, the subject of the next chapter.

### Credit, Panics, and Bubbles

As we saw in the previous chapter, factories were built chiefly with the owners’ own money and that of their relatives. Historian François Crouzet, who studied the financing of British industry, concluded that its principal source of growth was profit reinvested in the business again and again. Banks provided short-term credit: they discounted bills of exchange and lent money for raw materials and wages. But there were many banks, and they were small. The law forbade any bank in England other than the Bank of England to have more than six partners, and hundreds of country banks rested on the capital of one or two families. Each issued its own banknotes. When business was good, paper multiplied; when something went wrong, depositors rushed to withdraw coin, and the small bank collapsed.

In December 1825, this happened to many at once. The London public had become enamored of shares in mining companies in the Latin American republics newly liberated from Spain and of loans to those republics—including, as it turned out, the nonexistent state of Poyais, invented by the adventurer Gregor MacGregor. The bubble burst, bankruptcies followed, and dozens of banks closed within weeks—more than seventy by widely cited estimates. At the height of the panic, the Bank of England began lending to anyone who brought sound bills. Half a century later, in *Lombard Street* (1873), the journalist Walter Bagehot would turn this experience into a rule for central banks: in a panic, lend freely, against good collateral, and at a high rate. The panic also prompted reform: from 1826 onward, joint-stock banks with any number of owners were permitted more than sixty-five miles from London. The Bank Charter Act of 1844, introduced by Prime Minister Robert Peel, tied the Bank of England’s note issue to its gold reserve and forbade new banks to issue their own notes. Thus a system gradually took shape in which one central bank issued money while the others extended credit.

The most vivid example of how the era’s credit worked was the “railway mania” of the mid-1840s. After the success of the Liverpool and Manchester Railway, railway shares looked like a sure thing. In 1845–1846, Parliament authorized hundreds of new companies and thousands of kilometers of track; the capital required equaled a significant share of the entire country’s annual income. Physicians, clergymen, widows, and writers bought shares—the Brontë sisters were among the investors, according to their biographers. The “Railway King” was George Hudson, a York draper who at one time controlled about a quarter of Britain’s railways and, after the crash in 1849, was found to have paid dividends to old shareholders with money from new ones. Thousands of investors lost their savings. Yet this was a strange bubble: a substantial share of the lines authorized at the height of the mania were built and remain in service today. Private savings burned away, but the rails remained—a double-edged case in its purest form.

### Where the Surplus Went

Let us reduce the era’s monetary picture to the question of this book. According to estimates by Charles Feinstein, the leading authority on British statistics of the period, and Nicholas Crafts, the share of national income invested in new buildings, machinery, canals, and roads rose from roughly 6–8 percent in the mid-eighteenth century to 10–12 percent by the 1830s and 1840s. The figures appear modest, but behind them lay a turning point: for the first time, a society devoted an ever larger share of its income to development for decades, not on a ruler’s command, but through thousands of private decisions.

But the surplus also flowed through other channels. The Corn Laws of 1815 barred imports of cheap grain until the domestic price rose to a high threshold. The landowners who sat in Parliament benefited: their rents remained high. The cost was borne by townspeople and workers, who spent most of their earnings on bread, and by manufacturers, who had to pay wages sufficient to buy expensive bread. The same Ricardo explained the mechanism in *On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation* (1817): as the population grows and ever poorer land must be brought under the plow, grain becomes more expensive, while the owners of the best land receive rising rents without doing anything to earn them. For Ricardo, rent was income from ownership, not from labor or investment; in the terms of this book, it was extraction. In the same work, he set out the idea of comparative advantage: a country benefits from trade even if it produces everything better than its neighbors—it should make what it does best relative to other goods and buy the rest. His example of English cloth and Portuguese wine became perhaps the most famous example in economic thought.

The struggle over the Corn Laws was the first great clash between industrial capital and landed rent. The Anti-Corn Law League, founded in Manchester in 1838 and led by the manufacturer Richard Cobden and John Bright, raised money, printed pamphlets, and sent speakers around the country. In 1846, with famine already in its second year in Ireland, Robert Peel carried repeal and split his own party. In fairness, manufacturers were not motivated solely by humanitarian concern: cheap bread allowed them to avoid raising wages, and the Chartists—the workers’ movement of those years—viewed the League with suspicion. But for the first time, two ways of disposing of surplus openly confronted one another: rent that was merely collected, and profit that was invested. This time, the latter prevailed. The next section asks who made such decisions, and how.

## Power and Institutions

Manchester, August 16, 1819. On St Peter’s Field, historians estimate, some sixty thousand people gather—weavers, spinners, their wives and children, many in their Sunday best, accompanied by bands and banners. The banners read “Liberty and Fraternity,” “Universal Suffrage,” and “No Corn Laws.” They are waiting for the speaker Henry Hunt, who demands parliamentary reform. Frightened by the crowd, the local authorities send volunteer cavalry drawn from nearby shopkeepers and small manufacturers to arrest him, followed by hussars. Sabers are used against people who have nowhere to run. Within minutes, the field is empty; by varying estimates, fifteen to eighteen lie dead and hundreds wounded. In a bitter allusion to the recent victory at Waterloo, the newspapers call it “Peterloo.”

The scene reveals the era’s paradox. The country where the factory was born, where surplus first flowed into machines on a mass scale, was governed as though factories did not exist. Manchester, one of the kingdom’s largest cities, sent no member to Parliament. Tiny “rotten boroughs,” meanwhile—Old Sarum, for example, a hill with almost no inhabitants—sent two each, and those seats effectively belonged to local landowners. By widely cited estimates, only about one adult man in ten had the vote. Power belonged to the land.

### A State That Kept Out of the Way—and Helped

How can this be reconciled with the previous chapter’s argument about Britain’s “good institutions”? The answer is that institutions useful for development and institutions fair to the majority are not the same thing, at least at first.

The British state of the late eighteenth century did a great deal for industry. It protected property and enforced contracts: a manufacturer could be confident that neither the king nor a local lord would seize his factory. It granted patents, though they were expensive and tangled in procedure. Through private acts, Parliament authorized the construction of canals, roads, and later railways, giving companies the right to buy land from unwilling owners. It maintained a navy that protected maritime trade and tariffs that protected the market. Advocates of free trade are reluctant to recall this last point: the Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 prohibited first the import and then the wearing of printed Indian cottons, and it was under this protection that English producers learned to do what Indians already knew how to do. Britain proclaimed free trade after it had become the strongest.

What this state did almost nothing to do was refrain from intervening in relations between master and worker—except on the master’s side. In *Why Nations Fail* (2012), Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson call British institutions after the Glorious Revolution “inclusive”—open to new people and new ventures. With respect to entrepreneurs, this is largely true: Arkwright, a former barber, could become a knight. For workers the picture is different, and an honest account must acknowledge it: openness extended upward, not downward.

### The Luddites: The Machine Against the Law

The best-known clash between workers, the new power of machinery, and the state that stood behind it occurred in 1811–1816. In Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire, and Lancashire, groups of people broke into workshops at night and smashed stocking frames, cloth-shearing machines, and power looms. They signed threatening letters in the name of the imaginary “General Ludd,” or “King Ludd”—hence their name.

Today, a “Luddite” means someone who fears and does not understand technology. Historians paint a more complicated picture. As early as 1952, Eric Hobsbawm called such disturbances “collective bargaining by riot”: in a country where trade unions were banned, a broken machine was a way to force an employer to discuss rates. In *The Making of the English Working Class* (1963), Edward Palmer Thompson showed that Luddites often did not attack every machine, but only those belonging to employers who cut pay, hired untrained youths instead of apprentices, or produced cheap defective goods that damaged the trade’s reputation. Yorkshire cloth finishers—skilled, well-paid craftspeople—saw that machinery would take from them not only their earnings, but their place in the world. They were not mistaken: within a generation, their trade had nearly disappeared.

The state responded harshly. In 1812, Parliament made machine breaking a capital crime. The young Lord Byron opposed the bill in the House of Lords—his maiden speech—but it passed nevertheless. Troops were sent to pacify the northern counties—around twelve thousand soldiers by a frequently cited estimate. Many writers add that this was more than Wellington then had in Spain; the comparison is striking, but depends on whom and when one counts, so it should be treated cautiously. After the attack on Rawfolds Mill and the murder of the manufacturer William Horsfall, both in the spring of 1812, a trial was held at York in January 1813, and seventeen people were executed. The movement was suppressed.

Were the Luddites right in their central claim—that machines would deprive people of work? We will return to this question in the section on destruction. What matters here is something else: the people whose trades technology was destroying had no lawful means of influencing how its gains would be distributed. They had no vote, no trade union, no relief. All that remained was the sledgehammer—and the gallows in reply.

### Reform from Above and Pressure from Below

A situation in which the newly rich paid taxes but possessed no political power could not last. The middle class, not the workers, won its demands first. After two years of political crisis, mass meetings, and unrest—in Bristol, the crowd burned buildings—Parliament passed the Reform Act of 1832. The rotten boroughs were abolished, and the industrial cities—Manchester, Birmingham, Leeds, Sheffield—received members of Parliament for the first time. The vote was granted to men who owned or rented property above a specified value. By widely cited estimates, the electorate grew from roughly four hundred thousand to six hundred and fifty thousand—about one adult man in five now had the vote. The workers, many of whom had taken part in the struggle for reform, remained outside the threshold.

The reformed Parliament quickly showed whose interests it now heard. In 1833, it passed the Factory Act with its inspectors, discussed above, and the act abolishing slavery in the British colonies. Britain had prohibited the slave trade as early as 1807, after a twenty-year abolitionist campaign—one of the first mass public campaigns in history, with petitions, boycotts of plantation sugar, and testimony from formerly enslaved people. Yet emancipation in 1833 came with a condition that is astonishing today: compensation went not to those freed, but to the owners. They received twenty million pounds—about forty percent of annual government expenditure, historians estimate. The freed people themselves were required for several more years to work without pay for their former owners as “apprentices.” Using archival records, University College London’s *Legacies of British Slave-ownership* project, led by Catherine Hall, reconstructed who received this money and showed that part of it went into banks, railways, and insurance companies. Surplus wrung from enslaved labor was invested in development twice: first through the plantations, then through the compensation.

A year later, in 1834, Parliament passed the New Poor Law. The previous system, under which parishes supplemented poor families’ wages—known as Speenhamland, after the village where it had been introduced in 1795—seemed too expensive and morally corrupting to reformers. The new law left the able-bodied poor only one recourse: the workhouse, where conditions were deliberately made worse than those of the poorest laborer outside. Families were separated, inmates were given uniforms and monotonous food, and forced to break stones or pick apart old ropes. The calculation was that no one would enter without the most desperate need. Charles Dickens portrayed this world a few years later in *Oliver Twist*. From the perspective of this book, the workhouse is a telling case: society spent considerable resources not on making the poor more productive, but on making assistance sufficiently humiliating. This is close to what the introduction called wasteful spending.

The response from below was Chartism—the world’s first mass political movement of workers. In 1838, the “People’s Charter” appeared with six demands: the vote for all adult men, the secret ballot, equal electoral districts, salaries for members of Parliament, abolition of the property qualification for them, and annual elections. Three times—in 1839, 1842, and 1848—the Chartists submitted enormous petitions to Parliament; the second bore more than three million signatures, according to the organizers themselves. Each time Parliament refused to consider them. The movement faded in the 1850s, and contemporaries regarded it as a defeat. But five of the Charter’s six demands eventually became law; only the last—annual elections—failed to take root.

### Where the Surplus of Power Went

If we ask what the British state spent its resources on during this era, the answer frustrates simple narratives. War and debt consumed most expenditure: from 1793 to 1815, the country was almost continuously at war with France, and after Waterloo, more than half of government expenditure went to interest on the debt by widely cited estimates. Bondholders were wealthy, while the taxes—chiefly indirect levies on beer, salt, soap, and candles—were paid by the majority. Part of the surplus therefore flowed through the Treasury from the poor to the rich. The state spent very little on schools, hospitals, or sewers during these years.

Yet this is not the section’s principal conclusion. The state of the era created a framework within which private surplus could flow into machinery—and in this respect it was developmental. But it created no mechanism through which the gains from machinery could reach those who stood beside it. That mechanism—the vote, trade unions, factory inspection, schools—had to be forced from below, through Peterloo, the Luddites’ sledgehammers, and rejected petitions. Engels’ pause, discussed at the beginning of the chapter, was not only an economic phenomenon but a political one: while the majority had no voice, it had no share. The following sections ask what in all this genuinely fostered society’s development, and what merely appeared to do so.

## What Fostered Development

Birmingham, the 1780s. Once a month, on the Monday nearest the full moon, a group gathered at Matthew Boulton’s house in Soho, jokingly calling themselves the “Lunar Society”: the full moon was needed for a safe journey home after dinner along dark roads. We looked in on these dinners in the previous chapter; by the 1780s, the circle was in its prime. At the table sat Boulton himself and James Watt; the potter Josiah Wedgwood; the chemist and clergyman Joseph Priestley, discoverer of “dephlogisticated air,” or oxygen; and the physician and poet Erasmus Darwin, grandfather of the future author of the theory of evolution. They talked of steam engines and gases, pottery glazes and electricity, canals and the education of children. None held a university chair; none was paid a penny for these conversations. Yet patents, commissions, and joint ventures emerged from such dinners—along with the habit of testing ideas in practice.

This scene is a convenient place to begin discussing what genuinely fostered society’s development during the era. The machines described above were the visible part; the invisible part was how knowledge passed from heads to hands, and from one pair of hands to another.

### Knowledge That Became Common Property

In *The Enlightened Economy* (2009), Joel Mokyr calls this the “Industrial Enlightenment.” His argument is that earlier eras produced inventions, but they remained isolated and were often forgotten because a chasm separated scholar from craftsperson. The scholar wrote in Latin and had never held a file; the craftsperson knew how, but not why, and kept the secret within the family or guild. In eighteenth-century Britain, both sides began filling that chasm. Scholars gave public lectures on mechanics and chemistry in provincial towns; handbooks, encyclopedias, and technical journals appeared; societies such as the Lunar Society and the Society of Arts, founded in 1754, offered prizes for useful inventions. What mattered was not only that knowledge was discovered, but that it became cheap to find.

There were humbler links without which scholarly ideas would still have remained on paper. After studying the biographies of hundreds of British inventors of the period, Mokyr and economist Ralf Meisenzahl concluded that a substantial share of improvements belonged not to celebrated names but to obscure craftspeople—mechanics, clockmakers, locksmiths—who made other people’s machines work: improving, repairing, and adapting them. Britain was rich not so much in geniuses as in skilled hands. In the 1820s, “mechanics’ institutes” began opening for such people—evening schools where apprentices and master craftspeople attended lectures on mechanics, chemistry, and drawing and borrowed books from a library. The first appeared in Edinburgh in 1821 and London in 1823; by mid-century there were hundreds. They were attended more often by masters and clerks than by factory workers, but the principle itself—knowledge for those who worked with their hands—was new.

Knowledge also escaped the country despite prohibitions. For years, Britain banned the export of textile machinery and even the emigration of skilled craftspeople. In 1789, the young apprentice Samuel Slater, who had worked for several years at a mill belonging to one of Arkwright’s partners, sailed for America without writing anything down: he carried the machines’ design in his memory. A little more than a year later, the first water-powered spinning mill in the United States began operating in Rhode Island. The ban on craftspeople’s emigration was lifted in 1824; the ban on machinery exports, in 1843. From a British manufacturer’s perspective, this was leakage; from humanity’s perspective, it was the diffusion of knowledge—the principal means of turning one country’s discovery into a gain for many.

The era’s purest example of developmental knowledge came not from a factory at all. In 1796, Edward Jenner, a country physician in Gloucestershire, inoculated a boy with cowpox and then showed that he did not contract smallpox. Jenner did not keep the method secret or try to extract a monopoly from it; within a few years, vaccination was being used throughout Europe and far beyond. Smallpox, which by widely cited estimates took a substantial share of children during the eighteenth century, began to retreat. The invention cost almost nothing and earned its author almost nothing in money, but in lives saved it probably outweighed many of the machines in this chapter.

### Networks That Reduced the Cost of Exchange

The second force for development was networks. Canals and railways are often described as means of transporting coal, and so they were, but their effect was broader. When transport becomes cheaper, a local market merges into a national one. Wedgwood, who personally invested in the Trent and Mersey Canal, opened in 1777, could carry fragile pottery to ports without losing half of it on the road, and bring clay to his Etruria works. An earthenware plate, previously available to few, appeared on the tables of shopkeepers and craftspeople. The producer gained a large market, the buyer a cheap object, and both benefited from the same reduction in costs.

Communication grew cheaper after freight. Before 1840, a letter in Britain was normally paid for by its recipient, under a complicated tariff based on distance and number of sheets; for a working family, word from a son who had gone away might cost a substantial share of a day’s wages. The reforming civil servant Rowland Hill argued that most postal expense lay not in transport but in calculating and collecting the charge, and proposed a uniform rate: one penny for a letter anywhere in the country, prepaid with an adhesive stamp. Thus the world’s first postage stamp, the “Penny Black,” appeared in 1840. According to frequently cited figures, the number of letters more than doubled in the first year alone and continued to rise. This was a reduction in coordination costs in its purest form: merchants, manufacturers, families, and educational societies exchanged information more cheaply than ever before.

Railways contributed something else, far less obvious: common time. Previously, each town lived by its own sun; noon arrived in Bristol roughly ten minutes later than in London. This was a torment for train timetables, and from the 1840s railway companies adopted Greenwich time one after another. It sounds trivial, but it was the same kind of step as Whitworth’s screw-thread standard: an agreement that cost each individual almost nothing and saved everyone together an immense amount of effort.

### Trust from Below

Finally, development was also fostered by what people built for themselves, without the state or manufacturers. In an era when the state gave almost no help to the sick or elderly, workers formed “friendly societies”—mutual-aid funds to which each paid a few pence a week; if illness, injury, or the death of a breadwinner struck, the family received a benefit. By the middle of the nineteenth century, historians estimate, hundreds of thousands and later millions of people belonged to such societies—more than to trade unions and political movements combined. This was insurance invented from below and sustained by trust among neighbors and workmates.

In 1844, in Rochdale, a textile town near Manchester, twenty-eight people—mostly weavers and other craftspeople—each contributed a pound and opened a shop on Toad Lane. They sold honestly weighed flour, butter, sugar, and oatmeal without adulteration—in an age when chalk in bread and sand in sugar were commonplace—and divided the profit among the customer-members in proportion to their purchases. Each member had one vote, no matter how many shares he held. The principles of the Rochdale Equitable Pioneers became the foundation of the cooperative movement, which later spread around the world. This was a direct response to the truck system discussed above: if the employer’s shop took part of a worker’s earnings a second time, the cooperative returned it.

### Conclusion: What Fostered Development, and for Whom

If we apply the five measures introduced at the beginning of the book, the era’s picture looks like this. Labor productivity and energy per person rose as never before—this is the central and indisputable fact. Knowledge accumulated faster and traveled farther: through societies, lectures, journals, mechanics’ institutes, and even craftspeople who fled across the ocean. The costs of exchange and coordination fell sharply—canals, railways, a uniform postal system, common time, standards. The remaining two measures lagged behind. The life of the majority improved slowly and unevenly, and those who stood beside the machines were not the first to receive the fruits. Resilience to shocks, as the panics of 1825 and 1847 and the Irish famine showed, depended heavily on a person’s class and on which part of the kingdom they inhabited.

Even so, this era offers an unusually hopeful answer to the book’s central question. For the first time in history, surplus flowed into development not at the will of a single ruler, but through many channels at once: profit into machinery, curiosity into journals and lectures, workers’ pennies into mutual-aid funds and cooperatives. None of these channels had been designed for the common good, but together they produced a self-sustaining movement unlike anything known in earlier eras. What this movement cost and what it broke are the subjects of the next section.

## What Destroyed

Bolton, Lancashire, around 1800. Imagine a handloom weaver—there are thousands like him nearby—sitting at a loom in the cellar of his own cottage, where the damp air keeps the thread from breaking. Yarn from the new mills is abundant, cloth is in great demand, and he earns more than any farm laborer in the district, wears a good coat on Sundays, and can afford not to work on Monday. Thirty years will pass, and this weaver’s son will sit at the same loom for fourteen hours a day, earning several times less—and will still lose to the power loom in the factory by the river. This story, spanning a single generation, shows better than any other what an era that created so much also destroyed.

### Weavers: The Price of Transition

In the section on power, we promised to return to the Luddites’ question: were those who feared that machines would take people’s work away correct? The answer depends on the time horizon.

Over the long run, the Luddites were wrong. Machines did not leave Britain without work: by mid-century, more people were employed than ever before, while cheap cloth, iron, and coal created entire industries that had not previously existed. But for those who lived through the transition, the answer is different. Britain had an estimated quarter-million handloom weavers in the 1820s, historians believe, and with their families they numbered in the hundreds of thousands. In *The Handloom Weavers* (1969), Duncan Bythell traced how their earnings declined for decades, even before the power loom finally triumphed: ruined peasants and Irish migrants flooded into a trade that required no long training, and piece rates slid downward. The weavers clung to familiar work, cut back on food, sent their children to factories—and still lost. By the 1850s, the trade had nearly disappeared. The parliamentary commissions that questioned weavers in the 1830s left some of the bitterest documents of the era.

It is telling that even David Ricardo, an advocate of machinery and free trade, added a chapter “On Machinery” to the third edition of his *Principles* (1821), where he acknowledged that the introduction of machinery could be detrimental to workers, at least for a time, and that the working class’s fears on this score were not founded on prejudice. It was a rare case of a major economist publicly changing his mind. Society as a whole receives the gain from a machine, while a particular person bears the loss; in an era without relief, retraining, or the vote, that person faced the loss alone. In the terms of this book, this was destruction of a special kind: what perished was not machines or buildings, but a skill accumulated over generations and the life of the person who possessed it.

### Dhaka and Lancashire

The same story unfolded on a far larger scale on the other side of the world. Chapter 6 discussed Bengali muslin—cloth so fine that it was compared to morning mist. Dhaka was the center of its production. In the early nineteenth century, British machine-made cloth and later yarn flooded the Indian market, while high tariffs blocked Indian cloth from entering Britain. British cotton-cloth imports into India multiplied many times over within a few decades, while Indian exports nearly ceased. According to estimates by British officials at the time, Dhaka’s population fell severalfold during the first third of the century; historians consider the figures exaggerated, but the direction is beyond dispute.

Historians debate the causes. In *Why Europe Grew Rich and Asia Did Not* (2011), Prasannan Parthasarathi emphasizes colonial power and unequal tariffs. Economists David Clingingsmith and Jeffrey Williamson reply that the decline of Indian crafts began with the collapse of the Mughal Empire and was due above all to the machine being hundreds of times more productive than the hand: no tariff could have saved a spinner with a spinning wheel. The honest answer is that both are true. Productivity decided the outcome, while colonial power made the transition rapid and one-sided: India lost a craft and did not receive factories of its own in return. The first mechanized spinning mill in Bombay began operating only in the 1850s. The destruction here lay not in the old losing to the new, but in the gains from the new going entirely to another country.

### The Plantation at the Other End of the Chain

Slavery in American cotton was already discussed in the section on energy and technology. Here we must add how close the connection was. By the middle of the nineteenth century, according to generally accepted figures, most of the cotton processed in Lancashire—about three-quarters—came from plantations in the American South. In *Empire of Cotton* (2014), Sven Beckert calls the basis of this system “war capitalism”: land was taken from Indigenous peoples by force, labor was obtained by force, and only then did the market and factory enter the picture. Historians still debate how much the profits of slavery and the slave trade accelerated British growth. The Trinidadian historian Eric Williams considered them decisive in *Capitalism and Slavery* (1944); his critics—Stanley Engerman and Patrick O’Brien, for example—calculated that these profits made up only a modest share of all British investment. But the debate concerns how necessary slavery was to the Industrial Revolution, not whether it formed part of it. It did. Millions of people whose labor supplied the raw material for cheap cloth received nothing from it except harder work: as demand for cotton grew, planters demanded ever more from enslaved workers.

### The City That Killed

Let us return to Engels’ Manchester. The new industrial cities grew faster than anyone could build houses, paved streets, water mains, or sewers in them. Working families lived in cellars and back-to-back houses without through ventilation; one privy served dozens of residents; water came from the same rivers into which sewage and dye works’ effluent were discharged. Cholera reached Britain for the first time in 1831–1832 and killed tens of thousands; the epidemic of 1848–1849 claimed more than fifty thousand lives in England and Wales alone.

In 1842, the civil servant Edwin Chadwick published his *Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population of Great Britain*. Its most famous figure was this: the average age at death in Manchester was around thirty-eight in the families of professionals and the well-to-do, but around seventeen in working families. The figure is misleadingly terrifying because an average age at death is pulled down by infants—but infant mortality was precisely the chief calamity of these districts. Recalculating the evidence, historians Simon Szreter and Graham Mooney showed that life expectancy at birth in England’s largest cities did not rise in the 1830s and 1840s and in some places fell: in Liverpool and Manchester, it dropped to roughly twenty-five to twenty-seven years, almost fifteen years below the national average.

There is another piece of evidence that is harder to dispute: human height. Adult height depends heavily on childhood nutrition and disease. In *Height, Health and History* (1990), Roderick Floud, Kenneth Wachter, and Annabel Gregory, and later John Komlos using army and prison records, found that the average height of Britons born in the second quarter of the nineteenth century declined relative to those born earlier. The magnitude and precise timing of the decline are disputed, but the fact itself appears strange: the economy grows, while children grow shorter. Komlos called this the “early-industrial-growth puzzle.” The answer seems to be that expensive bread, urban disease, and hard labor from childhood consumed the gains in income. Average income per person rose, while the bodies of the majority told a different story.

### Famine in the Next Room

The era’s most terrible destruction occurred not in a factory town, but in the part of the kingdom that factories had scarcely reached. In Ireland, millions of poor people lived on tiny plots and ate almost nothing but potatoes. In 1845, a disease—potato blight—struck the crop, and harvests failed for several years in succession. Between 1845 and 1852, according to generally accepted estimates, around one million people died of hunger and disease and another million emigrated. The island’s population fell by roughly one-fifth in a decade, from 8.2 million to 6.6 million, and with continued emigration by one-quarter by the mid-1850s; it never returned to its former level. In *Black ’47 and Beyond* (1999), Cormac Ó Gráda shows that the catastrophe was not solely natural. Grain and livestock continued to leave Ireland while people died. Ó Gráda himself warns, however, that from 1847 onward Ireland imported far more grain than it exported, and that an export ban alone would not have ended the famine; what mattered was how much and how quickly the state was prepared to spend on relief. Government aid was meager, hedged with conditions, and often late, because influential officials believed that intervention in the market corrupted the poor and that famine itself would “correct” Irish poverty. In the judgment of Ó Gráda and many other historians, the richest country in the world failed to save its own subjects—not because it could not, but because this was how it understood the proper order of things.

### War and Smoke

Two more items must be added to the ledger. The first is war. For nearly a quarter-century, from 1793 to 1815, Britain fought France, and the wars of those years took several million lives across Europe by varying estimates. By 1815, according to generally accepted figures, Britain’s national debt exceeded twice the country’s annual income. In 1984, economist Jeffrey Williamson suggested that the war had “crowded out” private investment: savings that could have gone into factories and roads flowed into war loans, and British growth during those years was slower than it might have been. Others disputed this—Joel Mokyr, Carol Heim, and Philip Mirowski argued that factories lived on their own profits and scarcely depended on the loan market, so crowding out was smaller than Williamson claimed. But the logic is clear: surplus given to cannon cannot go to machines.

The second item was something almost no one regarded as destruction at the time. Smoke hung over Manchester and Birmingham, rivers such as the Irwell became sewers, and chemical works making alkali released caustic gas that killed nearby trees and crops; Britain would not pass its first law against such emissions until 1863. And there was another harm no one could see: the coal burned in furnaces had begun to alter the composition of the atmosphere. The quantity was negligible then, but this is where the story begins that the book will return to in Chapter 17.

### Conclusion

Reduced to the book’s central question, the era’s destruction falls into two kinds. Some was old and familiar from earlier chapters: war, slavery, the forcible extraction of the fruits of other people’s labor. The industrial age did not invent these things; it merely incorporated them into a new chain. Other forms were new: the ruin of skills and lives during a transition, cities built without regard for those who lived in them, and a natural world that no one counted as valuable. Both kinds shared one feature. The era’s surplus flowed into development faster than ever before, but the price of that development fell on those who had neither voice nor share: the weaver, the enslaved worker, the Irish tenant, the child in the cellar. Here development and destruction did not alternate; they advanced simultaneously through the same channels. The next section turns to what was neither one nor the other, but simply burned resources.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

Wiltshire, December 21, 1825. On the hills near the village of Fonthill Gifford stands England’s strangest building—Fonthill Abbey. It is not a monastery, but a private house built in the form of a Gothic cathedral, with long cruciform galleries and an octagonal tower that different accounts put at eighty-five to ninety meters high. The architect James Wyatt had been building it since 1796 for William Beckford, a writer, collector, and one of the richest men in the country. Beckford inherited his money from his father, who had made it from sugar plantations in Jamaica worked by enslaved people. The patron was impatient: laborers worked in shifts, day and night, by torchlight, and built in haste. The tower collapsed during construction—first one, then another; it was rebuilt each time, finally in stone. By the early 1820s, even Beckford’s fortune could no longer bear the expense, and he sold the abbey. In December 1825, the tower collapsed once more and buried most of the house beneath it. Only a small fragment remains on the site today.

Fonthill is almost too obvious a parable. Surplus wrung from enslaved labor on the far side of the ocean went not into machines or canals, but into a stage set that did not survive even one generation. And this happened in the very years when, a few hundred kilometers to the north, former barbers and weavers reinvested every shilling of profit in new spindles.

### An Old Form of Spending in a New Era

It would be a mistake to think that the Industrial Revolution abolished older ways of disposing of wealth. Even in the mid-nineteenth century, Britain’s greatest fortunes belonged not to manufacturers but to landowners, and they were spent largely in the old manner. There were country palaces with parks, where entire villages were sometimes moved for the sake of a view of a lake; stables and kennels; the London season, with balls and card games in which estates were lost; and the “Grand Tour,” a young aristocrat’s years-long journey through France and Italy, from which he returned with statues, paintings, and often debts. The Prince Regent, the future George IV, rebuilt his Royal Pavilion at Brighton in an Indian and Chinese style, with domes and minarets; work to John Nash’s design continued from 1815 to 1822—the very years when Peterloo was being dispersed in Manchester. More than once, Parliament assumed the prince’s debts; as we saw in the section on power, they were paid chiefly by indirect taxes on beer, salt, and candles.

At the end of the nineteenth century, the American economist Thorstein Veblen would coin a name for such behavior: “conspicuous consumption”—spending whose principal purpose is not utility, but the display of status. In the terms of this book, it is classic wasteful spending: resources are consumed and craftspeople receive work, but society’s capacity to produce more does not increase.

In fairness, two qualifications are needed. First, not every landowner was Beckford. The Duke of Bridgewater, whose canal began the “canal mania,” was an aristocrat; many lords invested in mines, roads, and agricultural improvement, and historians long ago abandoned the image of an idle aristocracy opposed to an industrious bourgeoisie. Second, luxury created demand for fine craftsmanship, and fashion sometimes became a bridge to mass production. In *The Birth of a Consumer Society* (1982, with John Brewer and John Plumb), Neil McKendrick showed how Josiah Wedgwood, after receiving a commission from Queen Charlotte, named his creamware “Queen’s Ware” and sold it to the middling ranks, who wanted to eat from the same dishes as the court. Imitation of the aristocracy became an engine of demand for factory goods. This is a double-edged case: vanity turned into a market for machinery.

### “Old Corruption”

The second kind of wasteful spending was less spectacular, but possibly more expensive. The radical journalist William Cobbett called it simply “the Thing”; historians later gave it the name “Old Corruption.” It was a system of sinecures—offices that brought income but required no work—pensions distributed for political loyalty, and posts that could be bought or inherited. Customs, the Treasury, and the courts listed officials who scarcely appeared at work and hired deputies for a small fraction of their own income. Some offices were paid not a salary but a share of the sums that passed through them; during the war with France, when government spending multiplied, the income of their holders grew with it—without any increase in labor. In 1820, the journalist John Wade published his *Black Book*, naming the recipients of sinecures and pensions; it sold in enormous numbers.

Army commissions were bought and sold—a system that would survive until 1871. Seats in Parliament also cost money: rotten boroughs were traded almost openly, while elections in large constituencies became contests of wealth—with feasts for voters, transport to the polls, and outright bribery. Accounts of the Yorkshire election of 1807 often say that the candidates spent a combined total of roughly a quarter-million pounds. The figure should be treated as an estimate, but it conveys the scale.

As in earlier chapters, this was rent in its purest form: income from position rather than contribution. It matters, however, that the era did something about it. Beginning in the 1780s, after defeat in the American War of Independence, governments and then William Pitt the Younger began “economical reform”: abolishing sinecures, introducing salaries in place of fees, and putting accounts in order. Historians William Rubinstein and Philip Harling have shown that by the middle of the nineteenth century “Old Corruption” had largely been dismantled—partly under pressure from radicals, and partly because the new industrial and commercial taxpayers did not wish to support idlers. This is a rare example in the book of wasteful spending being reduced without a revolution.

### Costs That Created Nothing

The third kind was what would now be called bureaucratic costs. We have already seen how expensive and complicated an English patent was. Before the reform of 1852, an application passed through numerous offices, each of which charged a fee; for an English patent alone, excluding Scotland and Ireland, an inventor paid around one hundred pounds by widely cited estimates—more than a craftsperson earned in a year. In 1850, Charles Dickens ridiculed the procedure in his essay “A Poor Man’s Tale of a Patent,” whose blacksmith hero goes from office to office and pays at each one. The money went not to examining the invention, but to maintaining officials. Two years later, Dickens would begin *Bleak House*, a novel about an endless suit in the Court of Chancery that consumes an entire inheritance until the last of it disappears into legal costs. The writer heightened the colors, but did not invent the problem: cases in that court really did drag on for decades.

Some expenditure during the railway mania belongs in the same column. Every new line required a separate act, and companies competed to hire lawyers, engineer-witnesses, and agents to shepherd their project through parliamentary committees and block a rival’s. Some authorized lines duplicated one another; some were never built. Unlike the money invested in rails, the money burned in these battles left nothing behind.

The workhouse discussed in the section on power can be placed here as well: expenditure designed not to make a poor person more productive, but to make assistance unbearable.

### How Much Was It?

The era’s wasteful spending cannot be totaled exactly: no one kept such a category. But its order of magnitude can be imagined through comparison. As we have seen, Charles Feinstein estimated investment in machinery, buildings, and transport at 10–12 percent of national income by the 1830s and 1840s. Judging by the social tables of the period—for example, the one compiled in 1803 by the London magistrate Patrick Colquhoun—landowners’ incomes were comparable to or greater than this share, and a substantial part went to consumption. This is a rough comparison, not a calculation, but its conclusion is clear: the era’s developmental channel had already grown broad, yet beside it still flowed an equally full river of rent and prestige.

The difference from earlier eras was not that wasteful spending disappeared, but that it ceased to be the principal channel of surplus and for the first time came under systematic criticism. In *The Wealth of Nations*, Smith called servants, the court, and most public officials “unproductive labor”—not as a reproach to the people, but in the sense that their work left nothing that could be put to work again. Ricardo explained rent, Wade listed sinecures, and Cobbett mocked “the Thing.” Society had at least learned to see where its surplus was leaking away. The next section considers what conclusions follow for the era as a whole.

## The Lesson of the Era

Less than a human lifetime separated the bell at Cromford’s gates from cholera in Manchester’s cellars. This era did what no previous one had managed: it set growth in motion and did not stop. It also showed that growth alone tells us nothing about who receives it. Let us reduce the chapter to five propositions.

**First: for the first time, wealth broke free of the land.** Every previous economy lived on energy supplied by the sun within a single year: grain for people, hay for horses, firewood for hearths. Each of these uses took land from the others. In *Energy and the English Industrial Revolution* (2010), the British historian Edward Wrigley called the transition to coal a shift from an “organic” to a “mineral-based” economy: coal is sunlight stored over millions of years, and extracting it did not require taking hectares from the fields. The steam engine turned this store into motion. Hence a central conclusion that will recur through the rest of the book: sustained growth in income per person is impossible without growth in energy per person. But this conclusion contains a qualification. The store is not infinite, and the smoke from Manchester’s chimneys was the first, as yet uncounted, bill for that growth. The book will return to it in Chapter 17.

**Second: what matters is not the size of the surplus, but its channel.** England in 1760 was not the richest country in the world, and its surplus was no larger than that of the Yangtze Delta or Bengal. The difference was where it went. Charles Feinstein estimated that the share of income invested in machinery, buildings, and transport rose from roughly 6–8 to 10–12 percent. A difference of a few percentage points, repeated year after year, changed the country more in half a century than any palace or campaign. Who made the investment decisions also mattered. It was not a ruler or a temple, but thousands of manufacturers, craftspeople, and canal shareholders, each of whom risked his own money on a mistake. This decentralization was a strength: unsuccessful decisions went bankrupt and successful ones were copied. It was also a weakness: the same decentralization produced the panics of 1825 and 1847 and the railway mania, when thousands of decisions moved in the same direction at once.

**Third: productivity growth does not reach the majority by itself.** This is the era’s most painful lesson. Engels’ pause—the half-century during which output grew while real wages remained almost flat—lasted exactly as long as workers had no vote, unions, or factory legislation. Here the debate must be presented honestly. Historians have long divided into “optimists” and “pessimists”: as early as the 1960s, Max Hartwell and Eric Hobsbawm debated in the pages of *The Economic History Review* whether workers’ lives had improved before 1850. The optimists point to cheaper cloth, tea, and sugar, and to the fact that people left villages for factories of their own accord, because village life was no better. The pessimists point to children’s height, mortality in the cities, and the lengthening working day. Feinstein’s 1998 calculations, regarded as among the most careful, found a modest improvement: by his estimate, real wages rose by less than one-third between 1780 and 1850, while output increased considerably more. The debate continues, but its main dividing line is no longer whether growth occurred; it is why the majority’s share lagged for so long. Both sides, with different emphasis, give the same answer: distribution was decided not by the machine, but by the power of the parties.

**Fourth: the price of transition is paid by those it catches unprepared.** The handloom weavers were not mistaken in their fear: their trade really did disappear, and within a generation their earnings fell severalfold. Their mistake lay elsewhere—in believing that machinery would leave society as a whole without work. A few decades later, British industry employed more people than ever before. Ricardo himself, initially convinced that machinery benefited everyone, acknowledged in the third edition of his book that the transition could ruin workers. Both truths hold at once, and this is the most important lesson for any era in which machines take on new work. Society as a whole gains, but gains and losses go to different people, and there was no bridge between them—no relief, no retraining, no share in the new profit. Its absence turned a beneficial change into a tragedy for a generation, and the Luddites’ sledgehammers into a response to that tragedy.

**Fifth: before surplus can change channels, its leakage must be made visible.** The era did not eliminate wasteful spending: Fonthill, the Brighton Pavilion, sinecures, and bought elections existed alongside factories. But for the first time, such spending began to be measured and named. Smith separated productive labor from unproductive; Ricardo explained rent; Wade printed lists of pension recipients; Chadwick counted deaths in the slums. Each of these books gave a weapon to those who wanted change: Old Corruption was dismantled, the Corn Laws repealed, the Factory Acts passed. Transparency is no substitute for struggle, but without it struggle has nothing to seize upon.

Now for the answer to the book’s recurring question. Where did the surplus of the first industrial age go? More than ever before, it went into machinery, mines, canals, and rails, and this share generated new surplus in turn. A substantial part, as before, went into landowners’ rents, interest on war debt, and palaces. Some was extracted far from England—on plantations in the Caribbean and the American South, and in Bengal. And for a long time, those who stood beside the machines received the least. The era showed that surplus could be directed toward development without orders from above. It also showed that development left without a mechanism of distribution enriches the few first and forces everyone else to fight for a share.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. Britain was first, and its path is not necessarily a model: the countries that followed—Germany, Japan, and later China—relied far more heavily on the state and banks. Many figures in this chapter are estimates, and every one is disputed. And the answer to whether the game was worth the candle depends on whom one asks: the weaver’s grandson who became an engine driver, or the weaver himself.

How many people lived in Britain, how income, wages, height, and life expectancy changed, and how far it had drawn ahead of India and China by 1850—all this appears in the section of numbers.

## The Numbers

For the first time, historians have a foundation for this era that did not exist before: British population censuses were conducted every ten years from 1801 onward, customs officials counted cotton imports, and parliamentary commissions counted children in factories and deaths in slums. But income, wages, and output are still reconstructed in hindsight, and many of the figures below remain disputed.

**Population.** According to the censuses, Great Britain had about ten and a half million inhabitants in 1801 and about twenty-one million in 1851: the population doubled in half a century, and for the first time growth on this scale did not lead to famine. The 1851 census marked another threshold: more than half the people of England and Wales lived in towns. Manchester grew from roughly seventy-five thousand inhabitants in 1801 to more than three hundred thousand by mid-century. Beside this stands the opposite figure: Ireland, with more than eight million inhabitants in the 1841 census, lost around one-fifth of its population by 1851—Cormac Ó Gráda estimates that about one million people died of hunger and disease and roughly the same number emigrated.

**Income per person.** In Angus Maddison’s figures, GDP per person in Britain rose from roughly 1,700 “1990 international dollars” in 1820 to 2,300 in 1850 and about 3,200 in 1870. For comparison, China and India remained at around 500–600 dollars in his tables over the same years, while China even declined slightly by 1870. Around 1700, the gap between England and Asia had been roughly twofold; by 1870, it was five- to sixfold. This was the beginning of what historians call the Great Divergence.

**Wages and output.** According to Robert Allen’s calculations in “Engels’ Pause” (2009), output per worker in Britain rose by roughly 46 percent between 1780 and 1840, while real wages increased by only about 12 percent. Charles Feinstein found real-wage growth of less than one-third between 1780 and 1850. The distance between the two curves directly measures how the era’s surplus flowed into profits and new investment rather than workers’ pockets. Only after the 1840s did the curves begin to converge.

**Energy and machines.** Coal output, around fifteen million tons in 1800, reached more than fifty or sixty million by mid-century. According to John Kanefsky’s estimates, the power of steam engines rose from several tens of thousands of horsepower around 1800 to several hundred thousand by 1850; waterwheels yielded primacy to steam only in the 1830s. Raw-cotton imports—around two and a half to three million pounds a year in the 1760s—exceeded six hundred million by 1850: an increase of more than two hundredfold in ninety years. Britain had around one hundred miles of railway in 1830 and more than six thousand in 1850, or roughly ten thousand kilometers.

**World industry.** In Paul Bairoch’s well-known estimates (1982), Britain’s share of global industrial production rose from roughly two percent in 1750 to almost twenty percent in 1860. Over the same period, India’s share fell from about one-quarter to less than one-tenth, and China’s from one-third to one-fifth. Bairoch’s figures are rough and have been repeatedly challenged, but no one disputes the direction of the shift: Asian handicraft yielded to the Lancashire factory.

**Length of life.** According to Wrigley and Schofield, life expectancy at birth in England was around forty years in 1800 and had scarcely changed by mid-century. The average conceals a chasm: in Liverpool and Manchester during the 1840s, it fell to twenty-five to twenty-seven years according to registry data recalculated by Szreter and Mooney. Edwin Chadwick’s 1842 report presented a famous calculation: the average age at death among Manchester workers was seventeen, compared with thirty-eight among local “gentlemen.” The difference came chiefly from children who died, but it says more about the city than any income summary.

**People outside the circle.** According to the United States censuses, the country’s enslaved population rose from roughly seven hundred thousand in 1790 to almost four million in 1860; they grew most of the cotton spun in Lancashire. When the British state abolished slavery in 1833, it paid twenty million pounds in compensation—not to those freed, but to the owners—about forty percent of annual government expenditure.

The chapter’s conclusion in numbers is this. The population doubled; income per person grew steadily for the first time; energy and output multiplied. But the majority’s earnings caught up with these curves a generation late, while life in the factory towns was shorter than in the countryside from which people came. How the second wave of machinery—steel, electricity, railways, and steamships—carried this growth around the world, and what it meant for those who stood beside the new machines, is the story of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; errors by a factor of one and a half to two are possible. From this chapter onward, the measure changes: not grain, but an hour of a typical worker’s labor. No one calculated median wages at the time, so I use the earnings of the most numerous groups—farm laborers, day laborers, and women spinners. By my estimate from Robert Allen’s and Gregory Clark’s series, in the 1810s–1830s they earned twelve to fifteen shillings for a sixty- to seventy-hour week, or two to three pence an hour—this requires verification. One pound sterling therefore equaled about one hundred hours. A working life, as before, was seventy thousand hours; sacks of grain at the prices of the period are included for reference.

### The Wars with France, 1793–1815

**Input data.** Over twenty-two years of war, Britain’s debt rose, by varying calculations, from roughly 240 million pounds to 700–850 million. Estimates of the war’s full cost range from 800 million to 1.5 billion, depending on what is included. I use approximately one billion.

**Hours and lives.** About one hundred billion hours—one and a half million working lives, with a range from one to two and a half million. In a country with ten and a half million inhabitants in 1801, every worker on average gave several years to the war. In grain, at wartime prices, this was about one billion sacks—enough bread for all of Britain for twenty years. Literal lives require a separate count: by widely cited estimates, more than two hundred thousand soldiers and sailors died, most from disease—this requires verification.

**Alternative.** The entire railway network built by 1850 cost an estimated 200–250 million pounds—this requires verification: the war equaled four such networks. Britain did not wage this war alone or purely by choice, and defeat might have cost more; that is what distinguishes destructive spending from wasteful spending. Yet interest on the war debt continued to consume around half of government expenditure for decades, while it was paid chiefly through indirect taxes—by the same farm laborers and women spinners.

### Compensation to Slave Owners, 1833

**Input data.** Twenty million pounds paid to slave owners—around forty percent of annual government expenditure. Most of the money was borrowed, and according to the British Treasury the final payments on this loan were made in 2015.

**Hours and lives.** About two billion hours—twenty-five to thirty-five thousand working lives. In grain, twenty-five to thirty million sacks.

**Alternative.** In the same year, 1833, Parliament appropriated money for schools for the poor for the first time: twenty thousand pounds, one thousand times less. Around eight hundred thousand people were freed; the same sum given to them would have amounted to about twenty-five pounds per person—nearly a year’s earnings for an English farm laborer. Instead, they worked for their former owners for several more years as “apprentices.”

**Conclusion.** Measured in hours, we can see who paid. The war was paid for by millions of people earning two or three pence an hour; compensation went to owners and their creditors, while those freed received nothing. Canals and rails—the developmental part of the era—cost several times less than either expenditure.
