# Chapter 18. Patterns Across the Ages

## Summary Table: Where the Surplus Went

If we place side by side all the graphs that economic historians have constructed for the past, one of them will be familiar to almost anyone who has ever opened a textbook. It is the curve of income per person, based on estimates by Angus Maddison and his successors. For thousands of years, it lies almost flat: by these estimates, the average inhabitant of the Roman Empire, a Chinese peasant under the Song dynasty, and an English plowman in Elizabethan times lived at roughly the same level—with differences of several times, but not tens of times. Around 1800, the line begins to rise, and by the end of the twentieth century it climbs so steeply that it has been nicknamed the “hockey stick.” Life expectancy, which remained near thirty years for millennia—mainly because of child mortality—had surpassed seventy-three years on average worldwide by the middle of the 2020s, according to the UN.

This graph is honest, but it shows only the outcome. It does not tell us why the hockey stick lay flat for so long, why its shaft rose at that particular place and time, or why billions of people remained at the bottom even after the ascent. The book’s seventeen chapters answered these questions differently, but with the same instrument: each time, we asked where the era’s surplus went—what remained beyond the necessities of survival—and sorted its uses into three baskets. Development-enhancing expenditure raised output per hour of labor, accumulated knowledge, expanded access to goods, and made society more resilient. Destructive expenditure destroyed people, capital, knowledge, or trust. Deadweight expenditure consumed resources without changing productivity or the lives of the majority.

It is time to gather the answers in a single table. An immediate qualification: this is not a measurement, but a compressed judgment. For no era can we calculate precisely what share of the surplus went into development and what share into prestige or war; for antiquity, we lack even a reliable figure for the surplus itself. The table is a map drawn from descriptions, and every cell can be disputed. But this is precisely why it is useful to see it whole: a coincidence in one chapter looks like a curiosity; repeated in ten, it looks like a pattern.

What becomes visible if we read the table by columns rather than rows?

First, the “What fostered development” column is surprisingly often filled by things that were not their creators’ principal goal. Writing was invented to account for temple grain, but became the memory of civilization. The alphabet was a convenience for Phoenician merchants. Monasteries copied books for the salvation of souls. The World Wide Web was created to exchange data among physicists. As Chapter 3 observed, the most enduring investments are those that escaped their owner: they could not be locked in a treasury, and they spread across the world. This does not mean that development is always accidental. Irrigation, universal schooling, running water, and the eradication of smallpox were consciously designed and did exactly what they were intended to do. These examples show that an invention’s consequences can extend far beyond its original purpose. The table does not show what share of all development-enhancing investment such cases represented.

Second, the “What was deadweight” column is never empty. No era escaped prestige expenditure, rent, and races, and, as Chapter 9 showed, growth did not require their disappearance: eighteenth-century Britain maintained sinecures and built palaces—and still pulled ahead. The table shows examples of such expenditure, but does not provide comparable shares across eras. Accountability may have helped identify and stop it; how much it changed the overall share must be studied separately.

Third, the “destroyed” and “deadweight” columns are more closely connected than they appear. The naval arms race before 1914 was deadweight while the dreadnoughts remained in port and became destructive when they went to sea. The mercantilist belief that the world’s wealth was fixed and could only be taken from a neighbor turned trade into war. Many catastrophes in the table began as deadweight expenditure that no one stopped in time.

Fourth—and perhaps most important—behind the three baskets in every row lies the same question: who decided where the surplus would go? The chief, priest, king, senate, baron, merchant guild, parliament, shareholder, planner, voter, asset market—the stewards changed, and the channel changed with them. The table can be read as a history of changing stewards of the surplus. The remainder of the chapter therefore concerns not so much technologies as mechanisms: what compelled the stewards to invest, and what enabled them to extract.

Finally, an honest qualification. The table inevitably encourages the idea of progress as a ladder humanity climbs rung by rung. This book makes no such claim. The rows follow the order of time, not an order of improvement: in some respects the Inca were organized more reliably than the Spain that destroyed them, while the Europe of the early 1930s was more dangerous than the Europe of 1913—and, in Germany and Austria during the worst years of the Depression, poorer as well. The hockey stick’s ascent over the past two centuries is not a law of nature, but the result of particular decisions that could have been left unmade or reversed. Those decisions are the subject of the next section.

## Mechanisms of Development

If the table is a map, the mechanisms are the rivers along which water flowed across it. The seventeen chapters repeatedly showed the same six channels through which surplus became development. None worked alone, and none was a guarantee. A combination of several conditions often accompanied prolonged growth, but the table itself cannot separate their contribution from other causes.

**The first channel is energy per person.** Everything people do, they do through energy, and for a long time its principal sources were their own muscles and those of their animals. In *Energy and Civilization*, Vaclav Smil presents history as a succession of energy rungs: fire, which made more food digestible; agriculture, which gathered sunlight into grain; the waterwheel and windmill; coal; oil and electricity. Each rung increased the amount of work one person could direct toward human purposes. A medieval plowman with a team of oxen commanded about one or two horsepower; a modern farmer with a tractor, hundreds.

But energy itself is only an opportunity. Rome knew the watermill and Hero’s steam toy, yet, as Chapter 5 explained, cheap slave labor created no need to replace people with machines. Economic historian Robert Allen explained why the steam engine went to work specifically in eighteenth-century England—not everyone agrees with him; critics believe craftsmen’s skills mattered more than the price of their labor (Chapter 10): labor was expensive there and coal cheap, so the machine paid. By his calculations, the same machine would have lost money in France or China at the time. Energy becomes a channel of development only when someone has an interest in using it to ease labor rather than merely access to it. The reverse is also true: cheap energy spent on a naval arms race or heating empty palaces adds nothing. The oil wealth of many twentieth-century countries became not growth, but rent—income received not for creating, but for owning.

**The second channel is knowledge and its free diffusion.** The book’s most persistent conclusion is that a society’s wealth is determined not by how much it knows, but by how many people can use that knowledge. Hunter-gatherers (Chapter 1) held all their capital in their heads, and it grew with the size of the exchange network: isolated groups, according to anthropologist Joseph Henrich’s hypothesis, lost skills over time—this was how he explained Tasmania, though some archaeologists see adaptation to the environment instead. Tang and Song China invented woodblock and movable-type printing, gunpowder, and the maritime compass, but much knowledge remained within a narrow stratum of officials and workshops serving the court (Chapter 6). After Gutenberg, Europe did not acquire better inventions, but cheaper diffusion: by 1500, estimates say that between 8–12 million copies of books (Eltjo Buringh and Jan Luiten van Zanden) and 20 million (Lucien Febvre and Henri-Jean Martin) had been printed.

Joel Mokyr, author of *The Lever of Riches* and *A Culture of Growth*, identifies the decisive development not as any one discovery, but as the emergence of the “Republic of Letters”—a network of people who published results, checked one another’s work, and competed for recognition rather than secrecy (Chapter 9). Knowledge open to verification accumulates; knowledge locked inside a guild, monastery, or secret bureau dies with its keepers. Soviet science (Chapter 13) was strong where it was allowed to exchange data and test results—in mathematics, physics, and space—and weak where ideology blocked verification, as in biology under Lysenko. The World Wide Web and open encyclopedias (Chapter 16) may have been the cheapest development-enhancing investments in history—precisely because their creators declined to charge for each copy.

There is an honest counterargument here. If knowledge is free, who will pay to create it? Patents (Chapter 9) arose as a bargain: the inventor reveals the secret in exchange for a temporary monopoly. Many economists believe that pharmaceuticals or mechanical engineering would have developed more slowly without this bargain. Others—Michele Boldrin and David Levine, for example, in *Against Intellectual Monopoly*—argue that patents more often protect incumbents than reward newcomers. History offers no unequivocal answer, but suggests the proper form of the question: protection of knowledge fosters development while it is temporary and leads to disclosure, and becomes rent when it turns permanent.

**The third channel is trust and low costs of exchange.** Economists call everything that must be spent to make a transaction happen a transaction cost: finding a partner, checking quality, reaching agreement, and securing performance. When these costs are high, people trade only with relatives and neighbors and therefore cannot specialize. Coinage (Chapter 4) eliminated the need to weigh metal at every purchase. According to Avner Greif, eleventh-century Maghrebi merchants kept one another honest through correspondence and shared reputation (Jessica Goldberg, Jeremy Edwards, and Sheilagh Ogilvie later showed that they did not dispense with courts and contracts after all). The Hanseatic League, the Champagne fairs, Italian bankers’ bills of exchange, double-entry bookkeeping (Chapter 7)—all were technologies of trust among strangers. The Amsterdam Exchange and the Bank of England (Chapters 8 and 9) extended trust to public debt: creditors came to believe they would not be cheated, and interest rates fell over time. The container (Chapter 16) reduced the cost of handling cargo so sharply that world trade was reorganized.

The reverse side is equally visible in the table. Debasement of coinage in third-century Rome, hyperinflation in Germany in 1923, and pyramid schemes in Russia and Albania in the 1990s destroyed trust itself—and with it people’s ability to exchange. Restoring it proved far more expensive than destroying it.

**The fourth channel is protection against arbitrary expropriation.** A peasant who knows the harvest will be taken will not drain a marsh. A merchant who fears confiscation keeps wealth in gold beneath the floor rather than in a workshop. One of the book’s most persistent patterns is the connection between confidence in tomorrow and willingness to invest. Parliament’s limitation of royal power after 1688, according to the well-known work of Douglass North and Barry Weingast, made the English Crown’s promises credible. Nathan Sussman and Yishay Yafeh qualified their argument: trust did not arrive at once—debt rates fell only after several decades of reliable payments (Chapter 9). Roman law (Chapter 5) protected citizens’ property and survived the empire by a thousand years.

But protection can cover not only what was created, but also what was seized. Property rights in slaves were among the most rigorously protected rights in nineteenth-century America. Enclosures in England secured some people’s rights at the expense of others’ customs. What matters, therefore, is not protection of property as such, but protection from arbitrariness: a rule that is the same for strong and weak, and a ruler subject to it. Chapter 15 showed the result of privatization without such a rule: property in Russia in the 1990s was formally protected, but went to those closest to power.

**The fifth channel is incentives to invent rather than seize.** In a 1990 paper on productive, unproductive, and destructive entrepreneurship, economist William Baumol proposed a simple idea: every society has roughly the same number of enterprising people; what differs is where the rules of the game direct them. In imperial China, the most ambitious young man prepared for the civil-service examination; in medieval Europe, he might become a knight and win wealth by the sword; in eighteenth-century England, he built a spinning mill; on Wall Street at the beginning of the twenty-first century, we might add, he designed complex financial instruments. The talents are the same, but the surplus goes into entirely different baskets.

This is perhaps the most direct explanation of why deadweight and destructive activity are so persistent: for the person choosing them, they are often more profitable than development. Lobbying for a customs tariff can bring a firm more than inventing a new machine. Chapter 17 showed how, when money was cheap, buying existing assets was more profitable than creating new ones. Society develops when the easiest path to wealth runs through creation rather than capture.

**The sixth channel is which elites stand at the sluice gates.** All five mechanisms converge on the question posed at the end of the first section: who decides where the surplus will go? In *Why Nations Fail*, Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson divide institutions into two types. Extractive institutions are designed so that a narrow group takes the income of the majority. Inclusive institutions give a broad range of people the opportunity to participate in the economy and in decision-making. Their central example is Nogales, a city divided by the border between the United States and Mexico: the same climate and largely the same people, but living standards differ severalfold.

The book confirms this framework, with qualifications. First, extractive elites are also capable of development: temple bureaucracies built canals, Soviet rule taught people to read, and South Korea under military rulers (Chapter 14) grew faster than most democracies. The difference is that such development depends on the rulers’ will and stops when the new begins to threaten their power. Second, inclusiveness was never complete: “inclusive” nineteenth-century Britain simultaneously ruled India and Ireland, where the famine of the 1840s claimed around a million lives. Critics of Acemoglu and Robinson—including Jared Diamond and Jeffrey Sachs—add that geography, disease, and resources also set the frame within which institutions are chosen, while Edward Glaeser and his co-authors argue that causation can run the other way: educated and increasingly prosperous societies build better institutions.

With these qualifications, the conclusion remains. Elites that invest do not appear because they are kinder, but because something constrains them from outside: competition among neighboring states, as in fragmented Europe; a parliament that can refuse a tax; voters who can replace a government; a press that can report failure. An elite that nothing constrains sooner or later begins to extract. The six channels are six ways to make the surplus flow toward development on its own, without depending on the virtue of those who control it.

But if these mechanisms have been known for so long, why do societies abandon them again and again? That is the subject of the next section.

## Recurring Traps

If the mechanisms of development resemble channels, the traps resemble dams. They need not be reinvented: similar mechanisms arose in different eras, though their causes and scale varied—the surplus that could have flowed into tools, knowledge, and trust stagnated, seeped into the sand, or burst the dam. The book contains five such traps, and they appeared so often that their first signs are easy to recognize.

**The first trap is rent.** To recall: economists call income received not for what a person created, but for owning something or controlling a passage, rent. Rent is not evil in itself—payment for the use of land or someone else’s invention can be entirely fair. The trap begins when extracting rent becomes more profitable than creating. In 1974, the American economist Anne Krueger introduced the concept of “rent-seeking” (Gordon Tullock had expressed the idea earlier)—spending effort and money not on production, but on the struggle for a right to receive income: a license, quota, privilege, or place at the trough. Her examples came from India and Turkey at the time, where import licenses were so valuable that firms spent enormous effort obtaining them.

But the book has shown that the phenomenon is far older. Roman tax farmers under the Republic (Chapter 5) bought from the state the right to collect taxes from the provinces and squeezed out as much as they could. Offices in ancien-regime France (Chapter 9) were sold and inherited, turning public service into family income. The sixteenth-century Spanish Crown (Chapter 8) received silver from the Americas that required no workshops, and so built none. The oil states of the twentieth century (Chapter 15) repeated the story: income coming from beneath the ground does not require educated, free subjects, and rulers therefore often failed to invest in them. Economists Jeffrey Sachs and Andrew Warner called this the “resource curse”; others, including Stephen Haber and Victor Menaldo, later showed that the curse is a risk, not a rule: much depends on what institutions were like before oil was discovered. Norway, for example, turned its oil into a fund for future generations.

Rent is also dangerous because it takes root. In *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982), Mancur Olson observed that stable societies accumulate “distributional coalitions” over time—alliances that secure privileges for themselves: guilds, cartels, professional lobbies. Each is small and has no interest in expanding the overall pie; it is more profitable to cut itself a larger slice. The longer a society lives without disruption, the denser this network becomes. Olson used this, among other things, to explain why defeated Germany and Japan grew faster after 1945 than victorious Britain: war had swept away their old coalitions. This is an uncomfortable conclusion, and a disputed one—many historians believe reconstruction and catch-up growth mattered more—but the observation that rent surrounds itself with defenders is confirmed in almost every chapter.

**The second trap is monopoly.** It is rent with a permanent address. Monopoly arises when one seller can set the price without fear of being bypassed. Medieval guilds (Chapter 7) began as associations of craftsmen that guaranteed quality and ended as barriers against competitors: they limited the number of masters, prohibited new techniques, opened the way for members’ sons, and closed it to outsiders. The East India companies (Chapter 8) received from their states exclusive rights to trade with Asia, and with them armies and the right to wage war; for the sake of its spice monopoly, the Dutch company conquered the Banda Islands, killing or expelling most of their inhabitants, and cut down clove trees on islands outside its control to sustain the price. The American trusts of the late nineteenth century—John Rockefeller’s Standard Oil controlled around nine tenths of the country’s oil refining by about 1880 (Chapter 11)—provoked the first antitrust laws. Digital platforms (Chapters 16 and 17) posed the same question in a new form: if a network is valuable because everyone is already in it, entry by a newcomer is almost impossible.

In fairness, monopoly does not always impede progress. As noted above, the temporary monopoly of a patent serves as a reward to the inventor. Joseph Schumpeter believed the hope of monopoly profit was the principal engine of innovation, while large firms with research laboratories, such as Bell (Chapter 14), gave the world the transistor, the solar cell, and information theory; Bell itself was a regulated monopoly. The boundary lies in the same place as with rent: a monopoly that can be challenged by a new invention spurs development; a monopoly protected by law, weapons, or habit stops it.

**The third trap is debasement of money.** Money is a promise: a coin or piece of paper is valuable above all because it can buy something tomorrow. A ruler short of funds is always tempted to break that promise quietly—to mix copper into silver or print more paper. It is a tax that requires no one’s consent. Over a little more than two centuries, the Roman denarius and the antoninianus that replaced it (Chapter 5) lost almost all their silver: by the 260s and 270s, only a few percent of precious metal remained in the coin, and the army increasingly demanded payment in kind. Henry VIII carried out the “Great Debasement” in England in the 1540s to pay for wars. Germany in 1923 (Chapter 12), Hungary in 1946, Zimbabwe in 2008, and many countries of the former USSR in the early 1990s (Chapter 15) experienced hyperinflation—prices rising so fast that money ceases to perform its function.

The picture is the same each time. At first, debasement looks like an easy solution: public debts melt away and the treasury is full. Then people stop keeping savings in money and flee into gold, foreign currency, and goods. Large debtors and those closest to the printing press win; pensioners, salaried workers, and depositors—the broad majority—lose. Worst of all, the trust discussed in the third channel is destroyed, and restoring it takes years and requires painful austerity. The opposite, however, is also a trap: excessively tight money—fidelity to the gold standard in the early 1930s (Chapter 12)—helped turn a contraction into the Great Depression. Debasement of money is not any change in its quantity, but a breach of the promise on which exchange rests.

**The fourth trap is war.** The book has said more about it than about anything else, yet it remains the most persistent. War destroys everything development accumulates: people, capital, knowledge, trust. According to Peter Wilson’s estimate, the Thirty Years’ War (Chapter 9) claimed around one fifth of the population of the German lands—older estimates put the figure as high as one third—and as many as half the inhabitants in some areas. The two world wars (Chapter 12) erased a vast share of the wealth Europe had accumulated over the preceding decades. War also produces two other traps: it is financed by debasing money, while afterward the victors obtain rent from the defeated—tribute, indemnities, and colonies.

Why, then, does it return? Partly because it is often profitable for those who begin it—Baumol’s “destructive entrepreneurship.” Partly because the trap is structured as a dilemma: a state that gives up its army risks becoming its neighbor’s prey, and the arms race (Chapters 11 and 14) then becomes deadweight expenditure that no one can stop first. There is also an honest counterargument discussed in Chapters 9 and 12: wars and rivalry advanced public finance, science, medicine, aviation, and computing. Sociologist Charles Tilly put it briefly: war made states, and states made war. But the fact that a side effect can be useful does not make war a rational way of obtaining it. The same technologies, developed in peacetime as the postwar decades showed, cost far less.

**The fifth trap is rejection of openness.** This may be the most insidious of the five because it looks not like expenditure, but like caution. A society that feels threatened closes itself: it prohibits foreign ideas, expels inconvenient people, and curtails trade. The Ming Empire banned private maritime trade as early as the 1370s—the ban was relaxed only in 1567—and ended Zheng He’s maritime expeditions after 1433 (Chapter 6). In the Ottoman Empire, printing in Arabic script for Muslim readers scarcely developed for almost three centuries after Gutenberg and was authorized only in the 1720s, under severe restrictions. Spain expelled the Jews in 1492, while France revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685 and drove out an estimated 150,000 to 200,000 Huguenots—craftsmen, merchants, and financiers who took their skills to Holland, Prussia, and England (Chapter 9). Germany in the 1930s (Chapter 12) expelled scientists, many of whom later worked for its enemies. Soviet genetics (Chapter 13) lost a generation because verification was prohibited.

Each such step looked rational at the moment to those taking it: it strengthened unity and protected faith, order, or power. The trap is that the gain from closure is visible immediately, while the losses emerge only a generation later, when neighbors that remained open pull ahead. The reverse is also true: openness carries risks, and societies that opened too rapidly and without protection—Russia in the early 1990s (Chapter 15) or the Asian countries that suffered a sudden outflow of capital in 1997—paid dearly for it.

**What unites the traps.** Viewed together, the five share one feature: in many examples, the benefits to the decision-makers and the costs to others were distributed unevenly. But this does not describe every case of war, monetary policy, or trade restriction. The tax farmer, monopolist, ruler printing money, general, censor—each acts rationally from his own perspective. This is why the traps recur: the problem is not stupidity, but that the gain is concentrated among a few and arrives at once, while the costs are spread among many and deferred. Hence the section’s central lesson. Appeals to virtue are useless defenses against traps; only arrangements that make capture costlier than creation work—the same six channels from the previous section. Where they weaken, the traps return by themselves, without any evil design.

One final question remains: how fair is this entire framework to history? Where does the book’s thesis work, and where does it stumble? The concluding section of the chapter addresses that question.

What does this table show if we do not attach importance to individual figures, but look instead at orders of magnitude?

First, the absolute expenditure of large industrial societies could be far greater than that of ancient ones. But the selected examples do not form a complete sample for each era, so the table cannot reveal a trend in the share of deadweight expenditure. The share of Soviet military spending in GNP is not the same as the share of labor employed and does not allow the “more than sufficient” portion to be calculated without separate justification.

Second, the selected estimates show the difference in order of magnitude between individual structures and long wars, but their uncertainty varies. Estimates of working lives for the Depression and military expenditure are especially sensitive to the assumptions chosen; they cannot be regarded as the established price of a single decision. Some major expenditures were spread across multiyear programs rather than a single construction project; the table does not assess how frequent such cases were.

Third, in several of the selected comparisons, the developmental project cost less than the military or prestige project. Mills versus the abbey, Bridgewater Canals versus the Seven Years’ War, railways versus the wars with France, sewers versus dreadnoughts, schools versus the White Sea Canal. The comparison is, of course, hypothetical: the alternative was never built, and its return is also an estimate. These are illustrations of opportunity costs, not proof that every substitution would have produced the same result.

And finally. Literal lives appear in a separate column of this table for a reason: Sicily, the White Sea Canal, the V-2, and Iraq show that destructive expenditure takes not only hours, but also the people who could have worked them. This is the difference between deadweight and destructive expenditure on which the book’s entire framework rests: deadweight expenditure burns the surplus; destructive expenditure burns the society that creates it.

## Where the Thesis Stumbles and What Remains

The introduction promised that the book’s thesis would not merely be illustrated, but tested. Seventeen chapters later, it is time to admit that it stumbles in several places. It would be dishonest to hide them, and useful to list them: doing so shows which part of the thesis bears the strain and which should be handled with care.

**The first difficulty is that the categories can be separated only in retrospect.** We confidently called the pyramids deadweight expenditure and irrigation canals developmental. But the pharaoh and his scribes were unlikely to see the distinction so clearly: to them, a pyramid was an investment in the order of the world, not in prestige. We ourselves often discover which category an expenditure belonged to only from its consequences. The space race of the 1960s (Chapter 14) was a contest between two superpowers to demonstrate strength—in our framework, almost pure prestige. But it produced satellite communications, meteorology, and miniaturized electronics. Military contracts produced radar, the jet engine, and the first computers. Cathedrals, which we placed partly in the deadweight category (Chapter 7), fed builders for centuries, trained engineers, and still attract travelers. The framework allowed for “ambiguous cases” from the outset, but so many appeared in the book that it is more honest to admit that the boundary between categories is not a wall but a broad band, whose precise course can be seen only from a distance.

This does not make the distinction meaningless. Even if prestige expenditure has a side benefit, it is reasonable to ask whether the same benefit could have been obtained more cheaply if pursued directly. For radar and the computer, the answer is probably yes, but it is difficult to test: postwar science, too, lived largely on Cold War military funding. This remains a question rather than a conclusion, and the reader should remember that many cells in the summary table are filled with judgment, not calculation.

**The second difficulty is that luxury sometimes propelled development.** As early as the beginning of the eighteenth century, Bernard Mandeville argued in *The Fable of the Bees* that “private vices”—vanity and a passion for luxury—gave work to thousands of artisans. Werner Sombart later linked the birth of capitalism to the demand of courts and the wealthy for silk, sugar, and porcelain. Historian Jan de Vries described the “industrious revolution” of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: Dutch and English families began working more in order to buy tea, textiles, and tableware—in other words, the desire to consume, including for status, drove growth even before the steam engine (Chapters 8 and 9). Many of today’s cheap goods were once expensive toys for the few: clocks, mirrors, automobiles, mobile phones. Status consumption by the elite paid for the first and most expensive examples, which later became mass-market goods.

This does not overturn the thesis, but it qualifies it. Luxury proved developmental when it was followed by falling prices and diffusion; it remained deadweight when elite demand merely channeled the labor of the majority into goods that never became more affordable. Once again, the distinction follows the book’s criteria—whether knowledge grows and whether the benefit reaches the majority—not how frivolous an expenditure appears.

**The third difficulty is that before 1800, the right investments changed little.** In *A Farewell to Alms*, economist Gregory Clark insists that for almost all of history humanity lived in a Malthusian trap: every improvement—a new plow, a better harvest, a fairer ruler—led to population growth rather than higher income per person, and within a generation everything returned to its previous level. If he is right, asking where the surplus of Sumer or Rome went explains little about the living standards of their peasants: had they invested more in canals and less in temples, there would have been more people, but they would have been no better fed. The book answered this point (Chapter 9) by arguing that the developmental investments of those centuries accumulated not income but potential—knowledge, institutions, networks of exchange—which took effect when cheap energy was added. Yet it must be admitted that for most of history the thesis describes what prepared the future takeoff more than what improved the lives of contemporaries.

**The fourth difficulty is luck and geography.** In *The Great Divergence*, Kenneth Pomeranz argued that around 1750 the Yangtze Delta matched England by many measures. His critics—Stephen Broadberry and his co-authors—use new GDP estimates to argue that the divergence began earlier, in the seventeenth century; but they, too, acknowledge the role of coal and colonies. In Pomeranz’s account, the divergence was decided by two things that can hardly be called merit: coal, which lay close to England’s future industrial regions, and America—the colonies that supplied the cotton, sugar, timber, and land Europe itself lacked. Add to this the fact that part of the English capital invested in factories had first been acquired through colonial trade, including the slave trade (Chapters 8 and 10). How large that part was remains disputed: Eric Williams (1944) considered it decisive, while Patrick O’Brien (1982) put it at only a few percent of investment. It follows that one of the chief examples of “surplus invested in development” grew alongside extraction and partly out of it. Here the thesis must admit that developmental and destructive processes can be intertwined in a single chain: for an Englishman in 1850, the Industrial Revolution was development; for a Bengali weaver or an enslaved worker on a Jamaican plantation, it was extraction. The answer to “Did this promote development?” depends on whose life we count.

**The fifth difficulty is states built by war.** We have already cited Charles Tilly’s argument that wars made states. The uncomfortable consequence is that many institutions praised in this book—the tax system, the Bank of England, public debt accountable to Parliament (Chapter 9), and to some extent even universal schooling and the expansion of voting rights—emerged or accelerated in response to the needs of war. Rulers needed soldiers who were literate and loyal, and money that subjects would consent to provide. The destructive, it turns out, repeatedly acted as midwife to the developmental. The book does not claim that war should have been invented for the sake of these institutions—they were successfully built in peacetime as well, as postwar Western Europe and Scandinavia showed—but the connection must be acknowledged.

**The sixth difficulty is who gets to measure benefit.** Finally, the framework itself rests on criteria that are not neutral. Productivity, knowledge, the well-being of the majority, sustainability—these are values many will share, but not everyone. A medieval monk would have regarded a cathedral as the best possible investment of the surplus because he measured it by the salvation of souls, not output per hour of labor. For a hunter-gatherer, the free time that farmers, by many estimates, had less of (the estimates are disputed; Chapters 1 and 2) might have been worth more than any harvest. We have tried to account for the quality of life of the broad majority, but what people consider a good life changes from era to era, and the framework inevitably views the past through twenty-first-century eyes.

**What remains.** After all these qualifications, the thesis becomes more modest, but it does not disappear. At least the following has withstood the test of seventeen chapters.

First, the thesis works better as a question than as a law. It does not predict which country will become rich, but it helps frame questions about the causes of decline or stagnation; it does not replace the testing of other explanations. In every chapter, the question “Where did the surplus go?” brought into view what would otherwise have remained in the shadows: tax farmers behind the Roman provinces, Potosí silver behind Spanish debts, gigantomania behind planning reports, and changes in asset prices behind the wealth statistics of the 2010s—a separate process that cannot be treated as expenditure of the surplus.

Second, the long rise in living standards after 1800 in many countries was accompanied by a combination of these conditions: cheap energy, open knowledge, trust among strangers, protection from arbitrary power, and rules under which creation pays better than capture. These mechanisms are useful for comparing histories of growth, but the book does not prove that each was necessary in every country. Luck and geography determined who arrived first, but not whether the first arrival could hold its lead.

Third, deadweight expenditure disappears in no society, and the ability to detect and stop it depends, among other things, on the accountability of those who allocate resources. Examples from different eras demonstrate such a mechanism, but do not quantify the share of deadweight expenditure or prove that its force was the same in all societies.

And fourth, the central subject of economic history is not only production, but choice. Every era produced more than was required for survival, and every era decided what to do with the remainder. As long as human labor was the main constraint, that choice was narrow: surplus per person was often small; feeding workers belongs to the necessary product under this book’s definition and is not an expenditure of the surplus. As machines, and now software, take over an ever larger share of work, the remainder grows, and the question of who controls it and under what rules ceases to be historical and becomes one of the most practical questions of our time. Which lessons from this book can be carried into the design of such an economy—and which familiar traps it will have to avoid—is the subject of the next chapter.
