# Chapter 19. A Bridge to the Model: An Economy Where the Surplus Goes into Development

## What History Tells an Economy of Automation

In 1930, at the very beginning of the Great Depression, John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay, “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren.” This book began with it, and it is a fitting place to begin the end. Keynes predicted that within a hundred years productivity would rise four- to eightfold, and people’s chief concern would no longer be earning their bread, but deciding how to occupy their leisure; by his estimate, the working week might fall to about fifteen hours. The first half of the prediction came true: income per person in rich countries rose roughly five- to sevenfold—within his range and closer to the upper bound. The second did not. Seventeen chapters have shown several reasons why rising output did not automatically turn into a shorter working week: consumption, income distribution, and working conditions changed. The book does not measure the shares of rent, status expenditure, and disputed forms of work in the total surplus; rising asset prices must in any case be accounted for separately from the flow of goods produced.

Now the question arises again, and more sharply. Machines that learn—what is now called artificial intelligence—are taking on not muscular labor, as the steam engine did, nor routine calculation, as the computers of the 1980s did, but some of the mental work that until recently was considered exclusively human: writing, translating, drafting contracts, finding errors in software, reading medical images. No one knows how many jobs this will affect. Estimates differ by multiples: a widely discussed study by Carl Frey and Michael Osborne (2013) classified almost half of U.S. occupations as being at high risk of automation, while an OECD study that considered individual tasks within occupations rather than whole occupations (Arntz, Gregory, and Zierahn, 2016) put the figure at about one tenth; a later OECD estimate (Nedelkoska and Quintini, 2018; Chapter 17) put it at about one seventh. All these figures are estimates, and all predated today’s language models. The first estimate produced after their arrival (Tyna Eloundou and co-authors, 2023) found something different: language models could affect at least one tenth of the tasks performed by four fifths of U.S. workers, and at least half the tasks performed by roughly one worker in five. To affect, however, is not to replace. Many expect the share of indispensable human labor to shrink; how fast, and whether it will shrink at all, remains an open question.

What can history tell us about this? More than it might seem.

**First: technology frees labor, but does not decide where the gain goes.** This is the central lesson of Chapters 10 and 11. Mechanization reduced the labor required for a pound of yarn by hundreds of times, while across the British economy, according to Robert Allen’s calculations, output per worker rose by almost half in the first decades of the nineteenth century, but real wages by only about one tenth. Workers’ earnings remained nearly stagnant for several decades—roughly from the 1780s to the 1840s (Chapter 10)—while profits and capital’s share rose. Allen called this “Engels’ pause,” after Friedrich Engels, who described the poverty of Manchester workers during those very years. Why the pause ended is explained in different ways. Allen himself regards the main cause as economic: technical progress spread throughout the economy, capital accumulation caught up with it, and demand for labor rose. In *Power and Progress* (2023), Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson emphasize the strength of workers: the Chartist movement, trade unions, the Factory Acts (including the Ten Hours Act of 1847), and the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846; the extension of the franchise in 1867 and mass schooling after 1870 consolidated the gain later. Their broader conclusion is that the fruits of technology reached the majority when the majority had the power to bargain for them, and did not when it lacked that power. Technology determines the size of the surplus, but not its destination.

**Second: labor that has been freed does not disappear, but flows elsewhere—if there is somewhere for it to go.** In 1900, about 40 percent of U.S. workers were employed in agriculture; by the end of the twentieth century, fewer than 2 percent were. This did not lead to famine: people moved into factories, then offices, schools, and hospitals. Economists call this compensation—hands released from one task find new uses because demand for other things rises along with productivity. The mechanism worked for two centuries, and it is usually invoked by those who regard fears of AI as a new Luddism. According to David Autor and co-authors (2024), about 60 percent of U.S. workers in 2018 were employed in occupations that had not existed in 1940. This is a strong argument, and it must be acknowledged. But it has two conditions. The transition takes a generation, and for those caught at its breaking point—the weavers of the 1830s, the miners of the 1980s (Chapter 15), the factory towns of the Rust Belt—it was not compensation, but ruin. Nor is the new use necessarily more valuable than the old: according to David Graeber, some of the labor released in the twentieth century went into “bullshit jobs”—positions that, by the admission of those who held them, would make no difference if they disappeared. A test based on large European surveys (Soffia, Wood, and Burchell) found significantly fewer such people than he believed, but not none (Chapter 17). In the 1980s, Nobel laureate Wassily Leontief warned that what happened to horses after the arrival of the tractor might happen to people: their numbers in the economy simply declined. Most economists regard the analogy as exaggerated—unlike horses, people have a voice, desires, and the ability to learn. But the disagreement itself shows that technology does not predetermine the outcome.

**Third: when labor ceases to be the bottleneck, distribution becomes the bottleneck.** For almost all of history, the main constraint was how much human hands could do. The surplus was small, and the question “Where should it go?” was decided by a narrow circle—priests, kings, lords, and later the owners of capital and officials—while everyone else was occupied with securing subsistence. If machines take on an ever larger share of work, the surplus grows faster than the demand for labor. The mechanism on which distribution in a market economy has rested—a person sells their labor and receives a share of the product in wages—then begins to falter. Those who have nothing but labor lose bargaining power; those who own machines, data, land in the right place, or control over a platform receive a growing share. This is not a prediction about the distant future: a declining share of labor in national income has been recorded in most rich countries since the 1980s (Chapter 17), though its size and causes remain disputed.

**Fourth: answers to this question have already been proposed—and each repeated an old fork in the road.** One answer is to leave everything to the market and hope compensation works as it did before. Another is redistribution through taxes and benefits, as in the welfare state of the golden age (Chapter 14). A third is universal basic income, paid to everyone without a means test. Experiments so far have been partial: in Finland in 2017–2018, two thousand unemployed people received the payment; in Kenya, residents of villages took part in the GiveDirectly program; in the United States, participants joined several municipal and private experiments. The results are mixed: employment barely changed in the Finnish experiment, while recipients’ well-being improved markedly; in Kenya, people did not work less; in the largest U.S. experiment, OpenResearch (2024, one thousand dollars a month for three years), recipients worked slightly less—by a little over an hour a week. A fourth answer is to hand control of the surplus to a state planning authority, and Chapter 13 showed where that path turned into gigantomania, shortages, and stagnation. Each answer solves part of the problem. None fully answers this book’s central question: not only how to divide the surplus, but who decides how it will be invested—in tools, knowledge, and trust, or in prestige, rent, and control—and how.

The seventeen chapters also add up to something like a technical specification for any answer. Those who allocate the surplus must be visible and replaceable—otherwise, as the story from Sumer to corporate boards repeatedly showed, the surplus eventually flows into their prestige and rent. Knowledge must remain open. The costs of exchange must be low, and trust among strangers high. Errors must be detected early and corrected cheaply rather than accumulate, as they did under Gosplan, until they reach catastrophic proportions. Creation must pay better than capture. And finally, the broad majority must have enough power to prevent the fruits of technology from stopping at another “Engels’ pause.”

The rest of this chapter describes one attempt to assemble such an answer—a model of an economy that the author of this book is developing together with its critics. It does not follow from history by necessity: other structures can be built from the same lessons. But it is intended as a direct answer to the question the book has asked of every era, and so it is fairest to test it by the same measures.

## The Model as an Answer to the Lessons of the Book

The model discussed here is called a decentralized public economy. Its starting premise is the same one to which the preceding section—and the book as a whole—has led: automation has reached a level at which the central question is no longer “How do we make people work more?” but “Who decides where the surplus is invested, and how do we keep it from leaking into rent and prestige?” Its main components are described below, each paired with the historical lesson it addresses. This is an account of the design, not proof that it will work; its weaknesses are examined in the next section.

**The goal is not accumulation, but a functioning society and development.** Different systems have used indicators capable of displacing the ultimate goal: the wealth of the temple, the king’s treasury, steel output under the plan, gross product, corporate capitalization. The model refuses to make growth its objective function—that is, the thing the system seeks to maximize at any cost. Its goal is stated more modestly: for society to function and change for the better as understood by those who live in it. This is an answer to the lesson of Chapters 13 and 15: the pursuit of an indicator—tons, gross output, quarterly profit—repeatedly turned the surplus into whatever raised the indicator rather than whatever people needed. Pyramids were built for prestige and worship, vast Soviet projects for tons and gross output, corporate share buybacks for stock prices (their defenders reply that the money is returned to investors and flows into other companies; Chapters 15–17). The motives and consequences in these cases differed; a single indicator cannot measure their aggregate benefit or harm.

**The means of production are held in common, but there is no plan from above.** Land, factories, networks, and machines belong to society and cannot be privately owned. Here the model draws on a line of argument that ran through the book. In the nineteenth century, Henry George argued that no one produced land and that rent from it was income created by society rather than the owner (Chapter 11). Nobel laureate Herbert Simon estimated that “social inheritance”—accumulated knowledge, institutions, and infrastructure—accounted for roughly nine tenths of the income of a rich country. This was an estimate, not a measurement, but it conveys the scale of what no individual created alone. The model, however, deliberately rejects the second step taken by the Soviet project: transferring decisions to a central authority. There is no Gosplan deciding how many kettles to produce. If people stop directing labor and resources toward kettles, production falls; if kettles are scarce, it rises. This answers the central lesson of Chapter 13: common ownership combined with centralized control did not free the surplus, but created a new stratum of allocators accountable to no one.

The rationale has also been clarified. Private ownership of a factory is excluded not because its origins are questionable, but because it entails command over other people’s labor: the owner sets the terms on which others spend their lives. Personal possessions, household tools, and the home one lives in remain personal—so long as they are not used for hiring labor and extracting rent.

**Everyone receives a share—a social dividend.** The economy’s income after common expenditure is distributed among all citizens as a regular payment, while basic needs—housing, food, health care, education—are provided unconditionally. This continues the discussion of “Engels’ pause” and unconditional income from the preceding section, but with an important difference. Basic income in today’s experiments is a benefit paid from taxes on top of a market economy, in which capital ownership and the distribution of income vary by country and group. Here the dividend is not a redistribution of someone else’s income, but a share of the common product. If machines produce more, everyone’s share also rises: the gain from automation is assigned by the design of the system itself rather than becoming lodged with the owners of the machines. And a person who is materially independent from the outset is less susceptible to bribery and pressure—a lesson the book observed both in Roman client networks and in factory towns where the owner controlled the workshop, the store, and the housing.

**Citizens themselves direct the surplus.** The unspent part of the dividend accumulates in a personal account, and people can direct it to projects they consider important: a laboratory, a workshop, a library, a new form of transportation, a theater. This does not make them owners of the project or entitle them to profit. Money is released in tranches—installments tied to completed work and reports—and support can be withdrawn. Long-term undertakings—fundamental science, medicines, energy—can receive commitments for several years ahead. Chapter 8 showed how the Venetian *commenda*—a contract under which one person supplied the money while another sailed with the goods—allowed someone without capital to start an enterprise. Chapter 9 showed how scientific societies and patents linked invention to reward. The model tries to preserve the mechanism of “many small bets instead of one large one,” but separate it from ownership: contributors receive not a share of future rent, but the result for which they contributed. People likewise choose freely where to work: there are no assigned positions; they join projects and organizations on their own (Chapter 17). Staged financing is not an invention of the model: venture funds and scientific grants already work this way, and it allows failure to be detected early, at the second tranche, rather than in the tenth year, as happened with long-running Soviet construction projects.

**Prices show cost, and scarcity is public.** Prices in the model do not serve as an instrument of profit: the cost of each product is published, rising when a resource becomes scarcer or more difficult to extract. If several projects claim the same limited quantity—of titanium, electricity, or land—the system does not conceal the conflict, but shows exactly which projects are competing, on what timetable, and what would be deferred under each option. Citizens make the final choice. This answers the lesson of Chapters 4 and 8 that low exchange costs and predictable rules are preconditions for trust among strangers, and the lesson of Chapter 16: in the digital age, information about resources can be publicly available rather than serving as an intermediary’s advantage.

**Decisions are traceable, and allocators are replaceable.** Every allocation of resources must be auditable through a chain: citizens’ decision → rule in force → source data → calculation → result. There must be no closed bodies that cannot be scrutinized. Enterprises are managed by their workers through an elected manager who can be replaced. This does not abolish the police, intelligence services, or auditing: operational information is not published, but the grounds, powers, and logs of such services must be open to scrutiny. Experts, auditors, and machine analysis prepare information—probabilities, quantities, timetables, historical analogies—but have no right to decide. This is perhaps the central component of the model, and it responds to the book’s most persistent theme. From the temple warehouses of Sumer to the boards of directors of the twenty-first century, the surplus flowed wherever the allocator was invisible and irremovable: into ziggurats and palaces, nomenklatura dachas, and the bonuses of bankers rescued in 2008. Acemoglu and Robinson (Chapters 9 and 18) called societies where power is constrained and accountable inclusive, and the others extractive—that is, organized for extraction. The model tries to make accountability not a concession wrested from an elite by the majority, as in the nineteenth century, but an initial property of the system.

**Development proceeds in many directions at once.** Neither a small group nor a state agenda has a monopoly on what counts as progress. Scientific, cultural, technical, environmental, and practical projects develop in parallel if they find support. This answers the lesson of Chapter 6 about Ming China, where the decision of a single court ended an entire country’s oceanic expeditions, and the lesson of Chapter 9 about Europe, where fragmentation allowed someone expelled in one place to find a patron in another. Competition among ideas remains; only the ability to turn victory in that competition into permanent rent disappears.

**Land is held in common, and rent is excluded.** Housing confers a protected right of residence, but not the right to speculate in land or rent out scarce apartments for income. Every six months, an automated system publishes how land, housing, factories, and infrastructure are being used, and any citizen may propose a vote to transfer an idle resource to someone who needs it more; when such a transfer occurs, the new user pays the relocation costs. New construction begins only if enough prospective users support it—so as not to repeat the Soviet houses and factories built to fulfill the plan. This answers perhaps the book’s most frequent observation: rent—from feudal obligations (Chapter 7) to housing prices in large cities after 2010 (Chapter 17)—was the main channel through which the surplus flowed from those who created it to those who controlled a bottleneck.

Taken together, the model is an attempt to answer the book’s question constructively: to make the surplus flow by default to the majority as a dividend, while decisions about investing it in tools, knowledge, and trust are made openly by many people, with the ability to stop in time. How well this design withstands its own lessons from history is the question of the next section.

## What Failures the Model Must Avoid and What Remains Weak

Any design that promises to direct the surplus into development must first answer an uncomfortable question: how does it differ from earlier attempts that began with the same words? Common ownership, self-government, and citizen participation also appeared in twentieth-century programs—and the book has shown what they became. It is therefore more honest to begin not with the model’s virtues, but with the failures it must avoid repeating, and then say where it still has no answer of its own.

**Failure one: a new allocator in place of the old.** The hardest lesson of Chapter 13 is not that Gosplan made errors in its calculations, but that common ownership quickly acquired a master—the nomenklatura, a stratum of people who controlled the surplus without formally owning it and without being accountable to anyone below. As early as 1957, the Yugoslav politician and former Tito ally Milovan Djilas called this stratum “the new class”; the book was published in the West while he was already in prison, and his sentence was extended by several years because of it. The model’s answer is replaceability, public decision logs, and a ban on bodies whose decisions cannot be scrutinized. Its military is voluntary, professional, and under civilian control. Yet here it immediately encounters its first unresolved question. What happens if the management of an organization—a warehouse, power grid, or audit service—refuses to carry out a citizens’ decision or denies access to inspectors? In a market economy, such a dispute has a court, a bailiff, and the police. The model acknowledges that it, too, needs an enforcement mechanism with a limited mandate, replaceability, and parallel auditing. But any enforcer with the power to compel is the seed of the very apparatus the model seeks to escape. Where is the boundary between an agent that carries out the will of citizens and an apparatus that begins to set its own goals? The question has merely been posed, not resolved.

**Failure two: self-government that closes in on itself.** Yugoslavia (Chapter 13) is the model’s closest historical relative: its enterprises were managed by workers’ councils rather than ministries. The experiment produced successes—rapid growth in the 1950s and 1960s, relative freedom to travel abroad for work—and characteristic maladies. As early as 1958, the American economist Benjamin Ward demonstrated in a simple model what was later partly borne out: if an enterprise divides income among its workers, they have no incentive to admit new people, because every newcomer reduces everyone else’s share. Enterprises clung to their workshops and spent revenue on earnings rather than development; unemployment rose, and by the end of the 1980s inflation was measured in four digits. Yugoslavia’s troubles did, however, have other causes as well—one-party rule, easy credit, national conflicts (Chapter 13)—and studies of real cooperatives, such as the plywood cooperatives of the U.S. Pacific Northwest (John Craig and John Pencavel, 1992), did not find the “perverse” employment response predicted by Ward. The model tries to break this loop: income flows not into an enterprise’s treasury, but into the common dividend, so workers have no reason to guard their workshop against newcomers, and closing an unnecessary factory does not deprive people of their livelihood. This is a substantial difference. But a related risk remains: the group that effectively controls a factory or plot of land may begin to behave like a collective owner. Critics of the model point directly to this: a limited voting community with exclusive control over a resource is ownership by another name—group ownership.

**Failure three: a commons that belongs to no one.** Chapters 2 and 7 discussed common lands—pastures, forests, irrigation canals. For a long time, the commons were thought doomed to ruin: everyone grazes one extra cow, and the meadow dies. This was the argument of ecologist Garrett Hardin in his famous article “The Tragedy of the Commons” (1968). Elinor Ostrom, who received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, showed through numerous examples—from Swiss alpine pastures to Philippine irrigation communities—that this was not so: common resources endured for centuries where boundaries were clear, use was monitored, penalties for violations were graduated, and disputes could be resolved. But Ostrom’s conclusion cuts both ways. It confirms that common ownership is possible without central planning—and at the same time shows that declaring a resource common is not a solution, but a statement of the problem. The model proposes publishing an analysis of the use of land, housing, and factories every six months and giving any citizen the right to initiate a vote on transferring an idle resource. This resembles Ostrom-style monitoring. But everything depends on the details: how long a right of use lasts, what counts as idleness, what threshold of support a transfer requires, and who enforces the decision if the previous user objects. Ostrom’s communities were small, and their rules had been refined over generations. Applying them at the scale of an entire country remains untested.

**Failure four: the majority against the future.** The model gives citizens the final choice. The history of democracies shows that a majority does not always protect the long term. In a 1978 referendum, Californians adopted Proposition 13, sharply limiting property taxes; homeowners benefited, while the state’s schools were constrained for decades—though researchers dispute how much blame belongs to the amendment itself and how much to the preceding court-driven reform of school finance (economist William Fischel argues that the reform itself pushed voters toward the amendment). Switzerland, the country with the most developed system of direct democracy, did not grant women the vote at the national level until 1971—later than almost all of Europe—because men decided the question. The model’s answer is that citizens see the alternatives and calculations, irreversible projects publish risk assessments, and AI assistants help verify claims. But the model deliberately enshrines no eternal list of rights: rules can change as problems arise. This is an honest choice—and also a conscious political risk that the project separates from its practical problems: the majority will be able to repeal yesterday’s guarantees. The procedure for such changes—a higher threshold, a repeat vote a year later, or nothing special—remains open. This connects to a question raised by critics: who formulates the question put to a vote, and who belongs to the electorate in a particular dispute? Neither can itself be decided by a vote, and together they often determine the outcome.

**Failure five: a signal without profit.** Hayek (Chapter 13) argued that a market price compresses the knowledge of millions of people about scarcity and abundance into a single number. The model preserves prices, but as publicly stated cost rather than a source of profit. This answers part of the objection: information about a resource’s scarcity is still transmitted. But profit performs another function in a market economy: it rewards the first person to notice an unmet need and punishes those who cling to something unwanted. In the model, this role is to be played by citizens’ voluntary support for projects and their ability to withdraw it. Whether such a signal would be fast and precise enough for millions of small decisions—which components to order, how much medicine to produce, where to open a workshop—cannot be settled by theoretical debate. The model relies on existing contracts, standards, logistics, and dispatch systems continuing to coordinate production without profit. This is partly true: within a large corporation, most coordination takes place through plans and schedules rather than prices. But the corporation is ultimately tested by the market, while here the only test is citizen support.

Related to this is an objection formulated by the Hungarian economist János Kornai in his study of socialist enterprises: the “soft budget constraint.” When the money is not one’s own and bankruptcy is impossible, a project manager expects funding to continue even when the undertaking is failing: the project will be rescued because stopping something already begun is politically awkward. The market punishes this with insolvency; the model removes that mechanism, and so the objection meets the model’s own criterion of specificity (discussed below). Its answer is tranches, the right to withdraw support, and a public log showing how much the project has already received and what it promised. Only practice can show whether this is enough to overcome the habit of pouring more money into an unfinished project. The method by which the model intends to calculate the full opportunity cost of a decision—that is, everything society gives up by choosing the project—also remains open: without it, publicly stated cost shows the price of a resource, but not the price of the alternatives forgone.

**What remains weak in practice.** Beyond the issues of principle, some questions look minor but cause real people to stumble. An overall surplus of built housing does not guarantee that the right apartment will be in the right city, of the right size, and near a school; the model has not yet determined how to compare such options or who pays moving costs when someone voluntarily relocates to vacant housing in another city. The legal status of a house built on common land is also not entirely clear: is it limited ownership of the structure, or a long-term right of use with personal ownership of improvements? The answer determines what a person can pass on to their children, exchange, or rent out. And finally, there is the transition. None of the systems described in this book arose on an empty site: feudalism grew within the late Roman Empire; capitalism grew within medieval cities and, in the view of some historians (Robert Brenner), above all in the English countryside with its rents and land market. The model describes how an established system might work, but not how a society in which land and factories belong to private owners and pension funds could reach it without the upheavals Chapter 15 described through the example of shock therapy.

It is also worth stating what does not belong on this list. Mistakes by experts, bureaucracy, shortages of particular goods, dependence on imports, and unpleasant work that few people want are not in themselves arguments against the model: all exist in the present economy as well. An objection becomes specific only when it shows that the market economy handles the problem through some mechanism, the model removes that mechanism, and a new cause of failure appears in its place. By this measure, which the project has adopted for itself, most early objections have been answered through clarifications—but this is the project’s own assessment, it has not yet undergone external testing, and Kornai’s soft budget constraint qualifies precisely as a specific objection. The remaining questions reduce to four practical problems—enforcement of decisions and control of the apparatus, the procedure for reconsidering resource use, the spatial allocation of housing, and the status of a personally owned structure—plus the separate conscious risk that the basic rules can be changed. That is not a short list. But it is no longer a list of reasons the model is impossible; it is a list of what must be tested.


## What Can Be Tested Right Now

The book has repeatedly shown how elegant schemes failed not in theory, but at their first encounter with people, weather, and the warehouse. The final question of this chapter is therefore practical: what in the model can be tested without waiting for someone to rebuild an entire country? Quite a lot, as it turns out. Almost every component of the model has living relatives—experiments already under way or completed—and almost every component can be tested on a small scale, through calculations, or in computer models. No such test will prove that the model works as a whole. But each can show where it does not work—and that is the most valuable knowledge of all.

**Test one: how much labor is actually needed.** The model’s starting premise—that automation has made labor less of a bottleneck—is not a matter of faith, but of calculation. It can be tested like an engineering project: take a particular country and calculate not how many people are employed today, but how many person-hours are required to provide every resident with a specified basic standard of living—housing, food, health care, education, transportation—to maintain infrastructure, and to pay for imports with exports. The project from which the model grew has begun such a calculation using data from Sweden: a small, wealthy country with a diverse industrial base that does not live off oil rent. The result may be uncomfortable in either direction. If the necessary hours are far greater than the model’s supporters expect, its foundation is weaker than it appears. If they are far fewer, then a substantial share of today’s labor really does go into what Chapter 17, following Graeber, called bullshit jobs, and the question “Where does the surplus go?” acquires a numerical measure. Importantly, such a calculation can be published in full, with all its assumptions, for anyone to challenge.

**Test two: a dividend from a common resource.** The idea that income from a common inheritance should be divided equally among everyone already operates in one place. Since 1982, every Alaska resident has received an annual payment from the state’s Permanent Fund, into which part of the income from oil extracted on public land is deposited. The amount varies from year to year: from a few hundred dollars in the mid-1980s to $3,300 in 2022 (including a one-time energy rebate); in recent years it has been about $1,500. Alaska’s experience demonstrates both strengths and weaknesses. The payment has enjoyed overwhelming support for decades and is almost impossible to abolish; according to an estimate by Matthew Berman and Randall Reamey (University of Alaska, 2016), it significantly reduces poverty, especially among Indigenous residents of rural areas, while Damon Jones and Ioana Marinescu (2022) found no reduction in employment. But its size is continually drawn into political conflict when the state lacks money for schools and roads. Thus even on a small scale one can already see the dispute between the dividend and common expenditure that the model would have to resolve on a large scale. Together with the Finnish experiment and Kenya’s GiveDirectly program, discussed in the first section, Alaska provides evidence about how people use unconditional money. What these experiments do not reveal is how people would use a share that could be invested in common projects. That is the next step.

**Test three: citizens direct the money.** In 1989, the Brazilian city of Porto Alegre became the first to place part of its municipal budget in the hands of open neighborhood assemblies. Studies, including by the World Bank, found that in subsequent years expenditure on sewers, water supply, and schools rose markedly in poor neighborhoods that had previously received the least. The idea—known as participatory budgeting—was then adopted by thousands of cities around the world, from Paris to New York. The honest picture, however, is mixed: the practice weakened over time in Porto Alegre itself, while in many cities citizens allocate only a few percent of the budget and few people attend the assemblies. Nevertheless, this is a ready-made laboratory. It can test precisely what the model has not resolved: whether funding should continue until canceled or stop until reconfirmed; how often people withdraw support; and whether money flows to conspicuous, “beautiful” projects at the expense of dull pipe repairs.

Similar experiments also exist in the digital realm. Since 2019, open-source software projects have distributed donations through so-called quadratic funding, proposed in 2018 by Vitalik Buterin, Zoë Hitzig, and Glen Weyl. Under this rule, support from many small donors carries more weight than one large contribution. Both its virtue and a characteristic vulnerability are already visible: money goes to projects needed by many people rather than by one wealthy patron, but participants also try to inflate support with fake identities. Any system in which people’s voices are converted into resources will face the same problem, and defenses against fraud will have to be designed from the first day.

**Test four: a house on common land.** One unresolved component of the model—the status of a home built on common land—has long been tested through community land trusts. One of the first, New Communities, was established in Georgia in 1969 by Black farmers active in the civil rights movement. The arrangement is simple: a nonprofit organization owns the land, while residents own their homes and may sell them, but at a price limited by a predetermined formula so that rising land value does not become someone’s rent. Today there are hundreds of such trusts in the United States, as well as trusts in the United Kingdom and other countries. They can be asked exactly the questions the model has yet to answer: what residents consider fair when they move, how often the price formula causes conflict, and how the right is inherited. A related example is Vienna, where the city and housing cooperatives have owned a significant share of the housing stock for decades and charges for that housing are limited. This is not the model, but it is evidence that a large city can function without surrendering housing entirely to the rent market.

**Test five: self-government without closing in on itself.** The risk described by Benjamin Ward—that workers in a self-managed enterprise will not want to admit newcomers—can be studied in operating cooperatives. The best known, the Basque Mondragon Corporation, was founded in 1956 and brings together tens of thousands of workers. Its experience confirms that a large enterprise can be managed by workers and withstand global competition. But it also demonstrates what Ward predicted: at Mondragon’s foreign factories, most employees are hired workers rather than cooperative members. The model claims that a dividend flowing into a common budget rather than an enterprise’s treasury removes this incentive. That claim is testable—first in computer simulations in which many artificial “workers” and “projects” follow simple rules (these are called agent-based models), then in small communities where part of enterprise income flows into a common fund.

**Test six: traceable decisions.** The chain “citizens’ decision → rule → data → calculation → result” can be built now, at the level of a city or organization. Precedents exist. Since 2014, Taiwan has operated the vTaiwan platform, where citizens, officials, and experts have openly discussed such matters as rules for ride-hailing services; Estonia has designed public registries so that people can see which agency accessed their data and when. These experiments show that transparency is technically achievable. They also show its limits: open discussions often remain advisory, while the authorities still make the decision. For the model, the test is not whether data can be opened, but whether people will use them and whether the implementer can be kept within a public mandate.

All these tests share one rule that should be stated plainly. Each must be designed with an advance declaration of what result will count as failure. If citizens given the power to direct money systematically neglect maintenance in favor of eye-catching novelties, that is evidence against the model. If, within a few years, a small community develops a stratum of allocators who cannot be replaced, that is evidence against it. If the required amount of labor proves far greater than expected, that is evidence against it. The book has shown that history does not forgive schemes incapable of admitting their mistakes: Gosplan could not say that the plan was bad, nor could banks on the eve of 2008 say that their risks were unbearable. A model that seeks to answer the lessons of history must begin with the opposite—with a readiness to discover that it is wrong.

Here the book ends its account of history and hands the question to the reader. Throughout history, decisions about the surplus were often made without the participation of many of the people whose labor created it; the degree of such participation varied among societies. For the first time, humanity has both machines capable of producing far more than is needed for survival and means of communication capable of making decisions about the surplus visible to everyone. Whether these capacities will be used—and precisely how—depends not on a law of history, but on what people decide to test and build.
