# Chapter 15. The Turning Point (1973–2000)

## Portrait of the Era

The Netherlands, Sunday, November 4, 1973. Not a single car can be seen on the motorways around Amsterdam and Rotterdam. Families with strollers walk on the empty asphalt, children roller-skate, some people have ridden onto the highway on horseback, while others have spread out picnic blankets directly on the median strip. This is not a holiday but a government order: private automobiles are forbidden from using the roads on Sundays. Similar “car-free Sundays” will soon be introduced in West Germany, Switzerland, and Denmark. The cause lies thousands of kilometers away. A month earlier, on October 6, Egypt and Syria attacked Israel on the Jewish holiday of Yom Kippur, and in response to Western support for Israel, the Arab oil-producing countries declared an embargo—a ban on oil sales to the United States and the Netherlands—and began cutting production month after month. Within a few months, the price of Middle Eastern oil roughly quadrupled, from three dollars a barrel to nearly twelve.

Remember how the previous chapter ended? Cheap oil was one of the pillars of postwar growth. Now that pillar had cracked. In the United States, lines several blocks long formed at filling stations; in some states, drivers were allowed to buy gasoline on odd or even days depending on the final digit of their license plate. President Nixon asked Americans to turn down their heating and not switch on holiday lights at Christmas. In Britain in the winter of 1974, when the miners’ refusal to work overtime and then their strike compounded the oil crisis, Edward Heath’s government introduced the “Three-Day Week”: to conserve coal for power stations, most businesses were allowed to draw electricity only three days a week. Television broadcasts ended at half past ten in the evening.

But the real shock lay not in the lines—they disappeared within a year—but in what happened to economic thought. Postwar economists were confident that they knew how to manage the economy. Their experience said that there was either unemployment or inflation—a continuous rise in prices. When people are laid off, prices do not rise because buyers have little money; when there is enough work for everyone, prices creep upward. A government can therefore choose: a little more inflation for the sake of employment, or a little more unemployment for the sake of stable prices. In the mid-1970s, something happened in the rich countries that this model said should be impossible: prices and unemployment rose at the same time while production stood still. A new word had to be invented for it—“stagflation,” combining “stagnation” and “inflation.” In Britain in 1975, prices rose by almost a quarter in a single year; in the United States in 1980, by more than thirteen percent. Someone who kept savings in a bank watched them melt away, while a worker who received a raise discovered six months later that the new wage bought less than the old one had.

Now let us move several years ahead and to very different places.

Xiaogang village in China’s Anhui Province, December 1978. At night, the heads of eighteen peasant households secretly gather in a mud-brick house. The village is destitute: during the famine years, its residents had gone begging in neighboring counties. The peasants decide to do something that could send them to prison: divide the collective brigade’s land among the households, so that each family will work its own plot, deliver the required quota of grain to the state, and keep the rest. They write a short agreement and place their fingerprints beneath it in red ink, promising that if any of them is imprisoned, the others will raise his children until they turn eighteen. The following year, according to participants’ accounts and local reports, the village harvest multiplied several times over. Today, that sheet of paper is kept in the National Museum of China in Beijing—as a symbol of the beginning of reforms that, according to World Bank estimates, lifted about eight hundred million people out of extreme poverty over the next forty years. The story of Xiaogang later became the official founding myth of the reforms, and its details are known mainly through polished retellings—but every source confirms the shift from brigade to household.

Washington, October 1979. The new chairman of the Federal Reserve—the central bank of the United States—is Paul Volcker, a man more than two meters tall with an ever-present cheap cigar. He announces that from now on the bank will fight inflation at any cost. The rates at which banks lend money to one another soar to nearly twenty percent by the early 1980s. Credit becomes unobtainable. Builders mail Volcker scraps of lumber—a symbol of the houses that no one can build—while farmers surround the Federal Reserve building with tractors. The most severe downturn since the Great Depression begins, and US unemployment rises above ten percent. But inflation does fall—and with it ends the world in which the state promised full employment above all else.

Britain, March 1984. A miners’ strike begins that will last almost a year—the fiercest in postwar British history. It faces the government of Margaret Thatcher, who came to power in 1979 with a program that had seemed marginal only ten years earlier: less government, less power for trade unions, and the sale of state companies to private owners. The strike is defeated; over the following years, most British mines will close, and entire mining towns in Yorkshire and South Wales will become places the young leave behind. Three years earlier in the United States, President Ronald Reagan had cut taxes on high incomes and fired striking air traffic controllers.

Moscow, January 2, 1992. The Soviet Union no longer exists—it collapsed a week earlier. On this day, the Russian government frees prices: for the first time in six decades, shops may sell goods at whatever prices they set themselves. Food appears on shelves that had stood empty throughout the autumn—but over the course of the year, prices rise by roughly twenty-six times, according to official statistics. Savings accumulated over a lifetime in bank passbooks turn into nothing. Pensioners stand outside metro stations, selling cigarettes, canned food, and their own possessions. A few years later, the country’s largest natural-resource companies will be sold for next to nothing, and a handful of people close to power will become billionaires.

New York, the 1980s and 1990s. Graduates of the best universities, who a generation earlier would have become engineers, scientists, or civil servants, increasingly choose Wall Street. The financial sector’s share of American corporate profits grows; new financial instruments—contracts whose price depends on the price of other securities—become an enormous market. Tom Wolfe’s novel *The Bonfire of the Vanities* (1987) makes its protagonist, a New York bond trader, a symbol of the era, while the hero of Oliver Stone’s film *Wall Street*, released the same year, delivers a line that will be quoted for decades: greed is good.

Five scenes—five sides of a single turning point. The postwar order described in the previous chapter rested on three pillars: cheap oil, fixed exchange rates, and the easy copying of American technologies. The first two collapsed in 1971–1973, while the third exhausted itself: Europe and Japan had nearly caught up with the United States, and further growth through copying was no longer possible. Familiar prescriptions stopped working, and over the next quarter century the world rebuilt itself according to new rules. They are known by different names: supporters call them “market reforms” and “economic liberalization,” while critics call them “neoliberalism”—a return, in a new form, to the nineteenth century’s faith in the free market. The word itself has become more an insult than a term, and we will use it cautiously.

The outcomes of these decades still provoke debate, and there is plenty to debate. On the one hand, inflation was defeated, world trade and growth recovered, and hundreds of millions of people in China, India, and Southeast Asia escaped poverty for the first time. According to the World Bank, from the early 1980s to the end of the century, the share of humanity living in extreme poverty fell from more than forty percent to just over a quarter—the exact figures depend on where the poverty line is drawn—and then continued to decline. On the other hand, wage growth for ordinary workers in rich countries slowed and fell markedly behind productivity growth, inequality returned to early-twentieth-century levels, and the countries of the former Soviet Union endured a deep economic contraction whose scale varied. The officially recorded fall in Russian output was comparable to the Great Depression in the United States, although the precision of that comparison is disputed.

For the central question of this book—where did the era’s surplus go?—this period is especially instructive because it shows how the same set of ideas produced entirely different results depending on where and how they were applied. In China, gradual reforms were accompanied by growth in production and investment; the contribution of individual reforms to this growth remains a matter of calculation. Russia’s contraction in the 1990s had several causes: the breakup of the Soviet Union’s economic ties, reductions in investment and military orders, changes in prices, and mistakes in reform. Weak law made it easier for a narrow circle to seize part of the assets. In rich countries, the share of financial intermediation increased, but it also provides useful services, and its turnover is not the same as the share of resources withdrawn from production. Where the boundary between development and extraction lay, who decided where surplus would go, and how visible those decisions were to the people they affected—these are the questions this chapter will address.

## Energy and Technology

The oil shock of 1973 was not merely a political event but a lesson in physics for economists. As we saw in the previous chapter, postwar growth rested on energy becoming cheaper year after year and being used without counting the cost: large American automobiles with eight-cylinder engines consumed twenty liters of gasoline per hundred kilometers, homes were heated without insulation, and factories were designed as though fuel cost almost nothing. When the price of oil quadrupled—and then, in 1979–1980, after the Iranian Revolution and the start of the Iran–Iraq War, more than doubled again—it became clear that the era of nearly free energy was over. The response to this realization defined the technological character of the next quarter century.

The first and cheapest response was conservation. It sounds dull, but by the scale of its consequences conservation was perhaps the largest “new source of energy” of the 1970s and 1980s. In 1975, the US Congress introduced mandatory standards for each manufacturer’s average automobile fuel consumption, known by the English acronym CAFE: within ten years, the average new American passenger car used almost half as much gasoline per kilometer. Japanese firms—Toyota, Honda, and Nissan—were better prepared than anyone: their small, fuel-efficient cars, which Detroit had mocked only recently, flooded the American market, and by 1980 Japan had overtaken the United States in automobile production. Europe and Japan insulated homes, converted industry to more efficient furnaces and motors, and imposed high taxes on fuel. From the mid-1970s onward, energy intensity—the amount of energy used to produce one dollar of output—fell steadily in rich countries: the economy learned to make more while burning less for each unit of output. The International Energy Agency, created by oil-importing countries in 1974 in response to the embargo, later estimated that without this improvement in efficiency, energy consumption in rich countries would have been roughly one and a half times higher by the end of the century.

Here the framework of this book appears in its purest form. Greater efficiency is a classic example of a developmental action: the same work is performed at lower cost, and the resources released can be directed elsewhere. But notice that the impetus came from a shock, not from wise planning. When fuel was cheap, the incentive to conserve it was weaker, and some expenditure in the 1950s and 1960s now looks excessive: uninsulated walls through which heat escaped into the street, and cars that carried two tons of metal for the sake of a single passenger. Once the market received the signal of a high price, it adapted quickly—but the signal arrived twenty years late and cost several years of stagflation.

The second response was to search for oil where extraction had previously seemed too expensive. At three dollars a barrel, venturing into the stormy North Sea made no sense; at thirty dollars, it did. Norway began production from the Ekofisk field in 1971; the first North Sea oil reached the British coast in 1975; and the giant Forties and Brent fields soon came on stream. In 1977, oil from Alaska’s North Slope began flowing through the nearly thirteen-hundred-kilometer Trans-Alaska Pipeline. Production grew in Mexico. Engineers learned to install drilling platforms over water hundreds of meters deep, work in icy seas, and drill directional wells. It was a genuine technological revolution—but for what purpose? Not to obtain fundamentally more energy, but to obtain it in secure places beyond the cartel’s control. Part of the rich countries’ surplus therefore went not into growth but into insurance against political risk. By the criteria of this book, this is a mixed case: greater resilience to shocks purchased at high cost.

The British story is especially instructive. North Sea oil arrived during the Thatcher years, bringing the state tens of billions of pounds in tax revenue during the 1980s. Where did this surplus go? Economists still disagree. Critics point out that much of it went toward tax cuts and benefits for the millions left unemployed after mines and factories closed—in other words, essentially toward current expenditure. The usual comparison is Norway, which established a state fund for oil revenue in 1990—the first contributions were made in 1996—and invests it in shares and bonds around the world; today it is the largest sovereign wealth fund on the planet, designed with many future generations in mind. Defenders of the British approach reply that the money remained in the economy in any case and that lower taxes encouraged entrepreneurship. But the contrast itself makes the point clearly: the same natural wealth can be turned either into long-term capital or into current consumption—and that is a choice, not a law of nature.

This choice appears even more starkly in the Soviet Union. Here the oil shock was not a disaster but a gift: it was in these very years that the giant fields of Western Siberia, above all Samotlor, one of the largest in the world, reached full production. By the early 1980s, Soviet oil production exceeded six hundred million tons a year, the country became the planet’s largest oil producer, and the prices paid for its exports multiplied. In *Collapse of an Empire* (2006), Yegor Gaidar described in detail where the oil dollars went: largely to purchases of grain and food abroad because domestic agriculture could not cope; to imports of consumer goods and equipment; and to maintaining military parity and allies around the world. The modernization of domestic industry that would have made the economy less dependent on raw materials never took place. When Saudi Arabia sharply increased production in 1985–1986 and the price of oil fell by more than half, the Soviet economy lost its chief source of foreign currency—and this, in the view of Gaidar and many other historians, became one of the forces that drove its collapse. There were other causes, and some researchers consider the role of oil prices exaggerated, but the pattern is familiar: surplus obtained not from rising productivity but from a favorable commodity price was consumed, and when the price fell, there was nothing left to rely on.

The third response was nuclear power. In 1974, at the height of the crisis, the French government adopted what became known as the Messmer Plan, named after the prime minister of the day: nuclear power stations would be built in series, to a standard design, several reactors a year. The plan was carried out. In fifteen years France built more than fifty reactors, and by the end of the 1980s nuclear stations supplied about three quarters of the country’s electricity—a larger share than in any other major country. France secured cheap electricity, almost independent of imports, for decades to come. Japan, South Korea, Sweden, and Belgium followed similar paths.

In the United States and most other countries, by contrast, the nuclear boom ran out of steam. Construction costs rose as safety requirements tightened, schedules slipped, lawsuits multiplied, and no standard design was adopted: almost every plant was built as a one-off. In March 1979, the reactor core at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania partially melted down. No one was killed and the release of radiation was small, but public confidence in nuclear power was shaken. Orders for new reactors in the United States had already been falling since the mid-1970s, because credit was expensive and electricity demand was growing more slowly; after the accident, they stopped altogether. More than a hundred ordered reactors were cancelled, many after construction had begun—billions of dollars buried in concrete that never produced a single kilowatt-hour. Then, on April 26, 1986, the fourth reactor at the Chernobyl nuclear power plant in Ukraine exploded. It was the worst accident in the history of nuclear power: a thirty-kilometer exclusion zone, more than a hundred thousand people evacuated in the first weeks and many more later, and hundreds of thousands of “liquidators” sent to deal with the aftermath. The number of deaths caused by the accident over the long term remains disputed, with estimates differing by an order of magnitude—from about four thousand among those most heavily exposed (the UN Chernobyl Forum, 2005; the WHO gave a figure of around nine thousand when the rest of the contaminated areas were included), to sixteen thousand across Europe (International Agency for Research on Cancer, 2006), and thirty to sixty thousand in the TORCH report (2006), commissioned by the Greens in the European Parliament. The direct economic losses suffered by Ukraine and Belarus ran into many billions of dollars and stretched over decades.

Chernobyl is an example of destruction in the most literal sense: land, towns, human health, and confidence in a technology across the world were destroyed. But its causes lay not in nuclear physics but in organization: in design flaws in the reactor that specialists knew about, as detailed in a 1992 IAEA report; in a culture where a superior’s order mattered more than the operating instructions; and in secrecy that prevented lessons from being learned from earlier, smaller incidents. We will return to this when we discuss what makes a system resilient.

While some engineers were drilling beneath the North Sea and building reactors, others—in the valley south of San Francisco that people had begun to call Silicon Valley—were making something that would ultimately change the economy more profoundly than oil. In 1971, Intel released the first commercial microprocessor, the 4004: a piece of silicon the size of a fingernail contained a computing unit that twenty years earlier would have filled an entire room. From there, development proceeded at the pace described by the observation of Intel co-founder Gordon Moore: the number of transistors on a chip doubled roughly every two years. The Apple II, one of the first mass-market personal computers, appeared in 1977; the IBM PC followed in 1981 and the Macintosh in 1984. Word processors and spreadsheets entered the office. VisiCalc (1979), followed by Lotus 1-2-3 (1983), let an accountant recalculate in minutes what had once taken days. Numerically controlled machine tools and industrial robots appeared on factory floors, and Japan led the way: by the end of the 1980s, its factories employed more industrial robots than all other countries combined.

And here economists encountered a surprise. Computers were everywhere—yet productivity growth in rich countries had slowed sharply after 1973. In the United States, output per hour worked, which had grown by about 2.8 percent a year from 1948 to 1973, rose by only about one and a half percent from 1973 to 1995. Robert Solow, who received the Nobel Prize in that same year, 1987, remarked in a book review that the computer age could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics. The observation became known as the “Solow paradox.”

Many explanations were proposed. One was historical: the economist Paul David pointed out that the same thing had happened with electricity. Electric motors appeared in the 1880s, but the sharp rise in factory productivity did not come until the 1920s, when a generation of engineers had grown up who understood that factories themselves had to be redesigned around the new technology, rather than merely putting a motor where the steam engine had stood. At first, companies in the 1980s likewise simply put a computer where a typewriter had been. A second explanation was statistical: the gains from computers were hard to measure—for how does one assign a monetary value to the fact that a document can now be corrected without being retyped? A third was that the new technologies had not yet reached sufficient scale. Each explanation seems to have contained some truth: in the second half of the 1990s, US productivity did indeed accelerate, and most researchers link this to computers and networks. In *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016), the American economist Robert Gordon offered a more pessimistic view: in his account, for all its spectacle, the information revolution affected fewer aspects of life than electricity, the internal combustion engine, running water, and sewerage, and therefore produced a much shorter burst of growth. The debate is not over, and we will return to it in the chapters ahead.

The turning point affected poor countries as well. When China began its reforms, its energy system depended above all on coal, and in the decades ahead coal would fuel the Chinese miracle—with grave consequences for the air in Chinese cities and the planet’s climate. In Asian agriculture, the fruits of the “Green Revolution,” begun in the 1960s, were ripening: high-yield varieties of wheat and rice bred by agronomists such as Norman Borlaug, together with fertilizers and irrigation, allowed India to free itself from dependence on grain imports by the end of the 1970s. This was one of the century’s most development-enhancing events—and at the same time it was laying the foundations of future problems: the depletion of groundwater, soil salinization, and dependence on fertilizers whose production itself requires large amounts of energy.

The technological side of the era can be summed up as follows. The world of 1973–2000 learned to live with expensive energy—mainly through conservation and new oilfields, rather than through a fundamentally new source. Nuclear power could have become such a source, but in most countries it did not, because of its cost, accidents, and fear. And the era’s most important technology, microelectronics, was for a long time barely visible in economic growth; its main economic effects would arrive only in the next period. So where did the surplus go? Some of it was invested in efficiency and knowledge that paid off over decades. Some went toward insurance against oil blackmail. And some—like the Soviet Union’s petrodollars or America’s unfinished reactors—burned away, leaving almost no trace.

## Organization of Labor

August 1981. Thirteen thousand American air traffic controllers—the people who guide aircraft past one another in the sky from their radar screens—go on strike. They demand higher pay, a shorter working week, and earlier retirement: the job is stressful, and a mistake can cost hundreds of lives. Their union, PATCO, is one of the few that supported Ronald Reagan in the election the year before. But federal employees are forbidden by law to strike, and the president gives them forty-eight hours to return to work. Few do. More than eleven thousand people are fired and barred from working as controllers for the federal service, the union is dissolved, and new controllers are trained in haste for several years while military personnel cover some shifts. Many labor historians regard this episode as a turning point: large companies saw that strikers could simply be replaced, and began doing so with increasing frequency.

Three years later, as we saw at the beginning of the chapter, a similar drama unfolded with Britain’s miners. Two strikes, two defeats—and the end of the “Treaty of Detroit” described in the previous chapter. That bargain rested on a simple exchange: workers did not obstruct modernization, and companies gave them a stable share of the growing surplus. As long as the economy was growing by five percent a year, sharing was easy. When growth fell and prices began to climb, both sides started pulling the blanket toward themselves. Unions demanded raises to keep up with inflation, companies raised prices to keep up with wages, and this “wage–price spiral” became one of the causes of stagflation. The British winter of 1978–1979 entered history as the “Winter of Discontent”: refuse collectors, truck drivers, and hospital workers went on strike; bags of rubbish piled up in London’s streets, and in Liverpool, during a gravediggers’ strike, the dead ceased to be buried. Thatcher came to power a few months later.

From then on, change came from three directions at once.

The first was the contraction of industry itself. In 1970, about a quarter of all nonfarm workers in the United States were employed in manufacturing; by 2000, the figure was about one eighth. The same thing was happening in Britain, Germany, and France. In part this was the continuation of a long-standing process: just as peasants had once left the countryside because machines could feed a country with fewer hands, factories were now producing more with fewer workers. In part it was the result of competition: Japanese cars and televisions, followed by Korean steel and Taiwanese electronics, displaced products from the old industrial regions. America’s “Rust Belt”—Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Youngstown, Detroit—lost its steel and automobile plants one after another. But the main problem lay not in the aggregate numbers but in their geography. When a steel mill in Youngstown closed, new jobs appeared somewhere else—in Houston hospitals, Atlanta offices, and Phoenix stores. They were almost inaccessible to a fifty-year-old steelworker: a different occupation, a different city, and wages half as high. The Swedish model described in the previous chapter, in which the state paid for retraining and relocation, was never built in most countries. Labor that had been freed and could have become a resource often turned instead into years lost to benefits, early disability retirement, and, in the worst cases, alcohol and broken families.

The second was the weakening of trade unions. In the United States in 1983, when statistics began to measure union membership using a consistent method, about one worker in five belonged to a union; by 2000 the figure was around thirteen percent, and in the private sector it was less than one in ten. In Britain, union membership fell from more than thirteen million in 1979 to roughly seven and a half million by the end of the century. There were several reasons: new laws restricted sympathy strikes and required secret ballots before industrial action; employment moved from large factories, where unions were strong, to small offices and shops, where they failed to take root; and the spirit of the age itself made collective demands seem like a relic of the past. Supporters of the reforms point out that British unions in the 1970s often defended not just wages but jobs that were plainly redundant—for example, firemen on diesel and electric locomotives, where there was no longer any fire to tend. In the framework of this book, that was deadweight waste, and eliminating it was a step toward development. But along with the redundant rules disappeared the mechanism that had divided the surplus between owners and workers during the “golden age.”

The third was a new philosophy of corporate management. In September 1970, the economist Milton Friedman published an article in *The New York Times Magazine* whose title became a slogan: the social responsibility of business is to increase its profits. A few years later, the economists Michael Jensen and William Meckling (1976) offered a theory explaining why hired executives tended to spend shareholders’ money on their own comfort and prestige, and how to correct this: pay managers in shares so that their interests aligned with those of the owners. Both ideas took aim at a real problem. The corporations of the 1960s had indeed accumulated conglomerates of dozens of unrelated businesses, luxurious headquarters, and corporate jets—classic deadweight waste that no one controlled. But in practice, “maximizing shareholder value” often meant something else: if firing thousands of workers raised the share price, and the chief executive was paid in shares, he would fire them and be rewarded for it. Jack Welch, who took charge of General Electric in 1981, eliminated more than a hundred thousand jobs in his first years and was nicknamed “Neutron Jack” by the press, after the neutron bomb that kills people while leaving buildings intact. He also became the most revered manager of the age.

What ultimately happened to workers’ share of the surplus? The best-known and most disputed chart of the era compares productivity growth in the United States with the growth of compensation for an ordinary worker. Until the early 1970s, the two lines moved together; afterward they diverged: productivity continued to rise, though more slowly, while the hourly compensation of a typical worker, according to calculations by the Economic Policy Institute’s Lawrence Mishel and colleagues, increased very little. Critics, including the economist Martin Feldstein (2008), objected that much of the gap was created by the method of calculation: if compensation included not only wages but also health insurance and pension contributions, which had become much more expensive, and if income and output were adjusted using the same price index, the gap narrowed considerably. Both sides are partly right. Average compensation grew roughly in line with productivity, while the compensation of the typical, “middle” worker lagged behind—because an increasing share of the gains went to those at the top: executives, financiers, and stars. According to estimates by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, the share of income received by the richest one percent of Americans grew by more than half between 1980 and 2000, and roughly doubled when capital gains were included. The economists Gerald Auten and David Splinter obtain a much smaller increase after recalculating post-tax, post-transfer income and adjusting for changes in tax rules. The dispute over its magnitude is not settled, but both sides recognize the direction of change. In continental Europe and Japan, the shift was much weaker—which itself tells us that the cause lay not only in technologies shared by all, but also in the rules by which each country divided the proceeds.

While the old bargain was breaking down in the West, Japan’s own bargain continued to work. In 1984, General Motors and Toyota opened the NUMMI joint venture at a plant in Fremont, California, that GM had closed two years earlier as one of its worst: absenteeism, drinking on the job, sabotage, defective products. Toyota agreed to take back largely the same workers, with the same union, but introduced its own system: teamwork, the right of anyone to stop the line, training, and proposals for improvement from below. Within a couple of years, the plant’s quality had risen almost to the Japanese level. The same shop floor, the same people—a different organization. The case became a textbook example of the fact that laziness and carelessness are not innate traits of workers: they are created by a system in which a person’s opinion counts for nothing.

In those same years, Sweden tried to go further. At Volvo’s Kalmar plant, opened in 1974, the continuous assembly line was abandoned: cars were assembled by teams that allocated tasks among themselves. At Uddevalla in 1989, the experiment went further still—small groups assembled almost an entire car. The Uddevalla plant closed in 1993, during a downturn; the dispute over whether it was less efficient or merely fell victim to cutbacks remains unresolved. Bolder still was the “Meidner Plan,” named for the same trade-union economist who had devised the solidaristic wage policy. In 1976, he proposed that every year large companies transfer part of their profits, in the form of newly issued shares, to funds managed by workers’ representatives; over several decades, these funds would become the controlling owners of Swedish industry. Business owners saw this as expropriation by stealth and in 1983 brought as many as seventy-five thousand people, by varying estimates, onto the streets of Stockholm. The version that was adopted was severely curtailed, and the funds were abolished in 1991. The question Meidner posed—whether the right to a share of the surplus could rest not only on ownership of capital but also on participation in labor—remained open.

In the East, the question arose in an even more paradoxical form. In the summer of 1980, workers at Gdańsk’s Lenin Shipyard went on strike, and within weeks an independent trade union, Solidarity, led by the electrician Lech Wałęsa, had emerged in Poland. By the following year it had, according to estimates, around ten million members—more than a quarter of the country’s population. In a state that called itself a workers’ state, workers needed an organization to defend themselves against that state. Formally, the factories belonged to the people, but decisions about what to produce, how much to pay, and where to invest were made far from the shop floor, and workers had no influence over them. Chapter 13 showed how this destroyed incentives; Solidarity showed that ordinary people understood it perfectly well themselves.

In China, reform began with the same question—who controls the fruits of one’s labor?—and the answer transformed the lives of hundreds of millions of people. By the early 1980s, the Xiaogang peasants’ agreement with which this chapter began had become a nationwide “household responsibility system”: the land remained collectively owned, but each household received the right to use it, delivered a quota to the state, and disposed of the rest. The hands released from the communes flowed into “township and village enterprises”—small factories owned by local governments or rural collectives. By the mid-1990s, official figures put their workforce at more than a hundred million people. Then the largest movement of people in history began. In 1980, Shenzhen—then a small border town opposite Hong Kong—became the first and best known of four special economic zones where foreign companies could build factories on preferential terms. Millions of young peasants, very often women, traveled to the coastal assembly plants, lived eight to twelve to a dormitory room, and worked ten- to twelve-hour days. Under the *hukou* household-registration system, they remained residents of their villages, with no right to urban schools for their children, urban health care, or a city pension. By the end of the century, estimates put the number of such migrants close to one hundred million. They received a smaller share of what they created than urban residents, but incomparably more than they could have earned in their home villages—and the money they sent home built new houses in the inland provinces.

At the end of the 1990s, China also broke the second pillar of the old order—the “iron rice bowl,” or lifetime employment at state-owned enterprises. Loss-making factories were closed or merged; according to varying estimates, between twenty-five and forty million people lost their jobs from 1995 to 2002, mostly in the old industrial cities of the northeast, such as Shenyang. This was China’s own “Rust Belt”—larger in scale than America’s.

In Russia, the transformation of the organization of labor looked different, and far quieter than might have been expected. There were almost no mass layoffs: official unemployment remained surprisingly low during the first years of reform. Instead, enterprises stopped paying wages. Months-long wage arrears became commonplace; workers were sent on unpaid leave, put on shorter working weeks, or paid in their own factory’s products—dishes, tires, crystal—which they then sold along the roadsides. The economists Vladimir Gimpelson and Rostislav Kapeliushnikov called this the “Russian labor-market model”: employment is preserved, while the price of labor adjusts. By various calculations, real wages fell by a third or more during the 1990s. Enterprises paid one another in goods rather than money: by 1998, according to estimates by Clifford Gaddy and Barry Ickes in “Russia’s Virtual Economy,” barter accounted for about half of industrial sales. Millions of people were formally employed in jobs that produced almost nothing, while surviving on vegetable plots, “shuttle” trading, and odd jobs. Scientists, engineers, and teachers left for the markets or went abroad. Knowledge accumulated over decades—the principal form of capital discussed as far back as the first chapter—drained away in silence.

Let us bring the three stories together. In the United States and several other rich countries, the share of workers belonging to trade unions fell; the distribution of income gains varied across countries and periods. In China, the household responsibility system gave peasants greater rights to the fruits of their labor and made a substantial contribution to rising harvests, alongside changes in procurement prices and the use of fertilizers. In Russia, many workers lost their former guarantees, while some assets passed to a narrow circle of owners; this does not describe the entire outcome of the transition. The common thread is this: what matters is not only how much labor creates, but whether workers have a voice when decisions are made about what to do with what has been created.


## Exchange and Money

On the evening of Sunday, August 15, 1971, President Nixon addressed the American people on television. Among other measures, he announced that the United States would “temporarily” stop converting dollars into gold at the request of foreign central banks. The temporary measure proved permanent. Attempts to maintain fixed exchange rates lasted little more than a year, and from the spring of 1973 the world’s major currencies began to float: their prices relative to one another were now set daily by the market. For the first time in history—from the Lydian coin of Chapter 4 to the gold standard of Chapter 11—not one of the world’s major currencies was tied to any metal. Money had finally become what it had always been in essence: a promise backed by trust in the state that issues it and in the rules by which that state operates.

This changed everything. Under Bretton Woods, described in the previous chapter, a government that printed too much money eventually ran into a wall of gold. Now the wall was gone, and the question of “how much money to issue” became a purely human decision. The first years showed how easily that decision could be made badly: throughout the 1970s, the governments of rich countries repeatedly chose slightly more money in exchange for slightly more employment—and got stagflation. Hence the influence of monetarism, Milton Friedman’s school of thought, which held that inflation was “always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon”: if prices are rising, more money has been issued than the economy needs, and the remedy is not price controls but restraint of the money supply. The Volcker shock with which this chapter began was an application of that idea in practice, although Volcker himself was not a literal monetarist. By the middle of the 1980s, meanwhile, British attempts to manage the economy by setting strict targets for money-supply growth had been quietly abandoned: the relationship between the quantity of money and prices proved far less reliable than the textbook suggested.

More important than any particular theory was the institutional conclusion: advocates of central-bank independence feared that short-term political incentives would intensify inflation. West Germany’s Bundesbank, renowned for its intransigence, served as the model. In 1989, New Zealand became the first country to give its central bank one overriding statutory goal—to keep inflation within a specified range—and by the end of the century, independent central banks that “targeted inflation” had become the norm. This was a major institutional invention, comparable to the Bank of England in Chapter 9: it restored predictability to money. But it also had a downside, to which we will return. One of the most important decisions about the distribution of the surplus—how expensive credit should be, and who should benefit, creditors or debtors—was removed from direct democratic control and entrusted to a narrow circle of specialists.

Floating exchange rates also gave rise to an entirely new market. If currency prices changed every day, money could be made from those changes—or protection could be bought against them. Companies that earned revenue in marks but paid their bills in dollars bought contracts fixing the exchange rate for a future date. Banks traded these contracts among themselves, then invented contracts on contracts. Such instruments are called derivatives: their price is “derived” from the price of something else—a currency, a bond, an interest rate. An options exchange opened in Chicago in 1973—options are contracts granting the right to buy or sell something at a price agreed in advance—and that same year Fischer Black and Myron Scholes published a formula for valuing them. Scholes and Robert Merton received the Nobel Prize for this work in 1997; Black did not live to see it. According to the Bank for International Settlements, by 1998 about $1.5 trillion changed hands on the foreign-exchange market every day, while annual world trade in goods amounted to roughly $5.5 trillion. In other words, in four days the currency market turned over more money than all of world trade did in a year. The overwhelming majority of these transactions were connected neither to the purchase of goods nor to the construction of factories: money was buying other money.

Where did the surplus pumped into exporting countries by the oil shocks go? Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, and the Emirates received more dollars than they could spend and deposited them in large Western banks. The banks needed to lend the money to someone, and in the 1970s they found willing borrowers in Latin America, Eastern Europe, and Africa. The arrangement was called “petrodollar recycling.” Citibank chairman Walter Wriston reassured skeptics with an argument that became famous: countries do not go bankrupt. Brazil, Mexico, and Argentina borrowed at floating interest rates and built dams, roads, and steelworks—or sometimes merely covered holes in their budgets while local elites moved capital to accounts in Miami and Zurich. As long as interest rates were low and dollar inflation was high, the debt seemed almost free.

The Volcker shock turned it into a noose. Interest rates on floating-rate loans multiplied, the dollar appreciated, and the prices of the raw materials these countries sold fell. In August 1982, Mexico’s finance minister, Jesús Silva Herzog, flew to Washington and announced that his country could not service its debts. Mexico was followed by Brazil, Argentina, and dozens of other countries. The largest US banks were at risk of collapse: their loans to Latin America exceeded their own capital. To prevent this, the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the governments of rich countries devised a scheme: debts would not be written off but “restructured”—stretched out and refinanced—while debtors would in return be required to impose austerity, cut subsidies, open their markets, and sell state-owned companies. The economist John Williamson (1989) later distilled these prescriptions into ten points and called them the “Washington Consensus.”

For Latin America, the 1980s became a “lost decade.” According to estimates by the UN Economic Commission for Latin America and the Caribbean (ECLAC), income per person in the region was lower at the end of the decade than at its beginning, and for many years more money left the region to service debt than arrived in new loans and investment. In the most literal sense, the surplus flowed from south to north: from Brazilian workers and Mexican peasants to holders of debt securities in New York and London. Partial relief came only in 1989 with the “Brady Plan,” when banks agreed to exchange old loans for new bonds with reduced principal. Readers of Chapter 3 will remember the Mesopotamian kings who periodically proclaimed debt forgiveness, understanding that otherwise debtors would become slaves and the army would be left without free peasants. The late twentieth century solved the same problem more slowly and painfully: debt was formally treated as inviolable, while the cost of its unpayability was borne mainly by those who had neither borrowed nor spent it.

At the same time, money freed from gold and from the old restrictions learned to fly across borders. Britain abolished exchange controls in 1979, other rich countries followed in the 1980s, and many developing countries did so in the 1990s. Supporters argued that free capital movement would direct the savings of rich countries to where they could earn the highest returns—to young economies short of factories. To some extent, this is what happened. But along with long-term investment came “hot money”—short-term loans and purchases of securities that could leave as quickly as they arrived.

The Asian crisis showed what this looked like. On July 2, 1997, after exhausting its reserves, Thailand stopped defending the exchange rate of its currency, the baht. Panic spread to Indonesia, Malaysia, and South Korea. The Indonesian rupiah lost about four fifths of its value in a matter of months, food prices soared, riots erupted, and in May 1998 the regime of Suharto, who had ruled for more than thirty years, fell. Korea, until recently the model “tiger” of the previous chapter, received in December 1997 the largest rescue package ever assembled up to that point under IMF leadership—about $57 billion. Millions of Koreans donated wedding rings and family gold at collection points to help the country pay its debts. The factories, ports, and knowledge of these countries did not disappear in a single winter—trust disappeared, and with it the money without which the factories stood idle.

The wave reached Russia. Unable to collect taxes, the state covered its budget deficits in the mid-1990s by borrowing through short-term bonds known as GKOs. To persuade investors to buy them, it promised ever-higher returns; by the summer of 1998, yields on some issues exceeded one hundred percent a year, and new borrowing was in effect being used to repay old debts. On August 17, 1998, the government announced that it would stop paying some of these obligations and would no longer defend the exchange rate. Within weeks, the ruble had lost about two thirds of its value, falling from a little over six to more than twenty rubles to the dollar. A month later, the US hedge fund LTCM—a private investment fund for wealthy investors—nearly collapsed; its founders included the same Merton and Scholes. Its bets rested on flawless models, but the models did not allow for the possibility that a major power might simply stop paying. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York brought together fourteen large banks, which invested about $3.5 billion in the fund so that its failure would not drag down the rest. This was a new lesson: the financial system had become so interconnected that the failure of a single bet threatened everyone, and rescuing it became a collective concern.

Against this background, the countries that went against the current stood out. China opened up trade but kept capital movements under lock and key: foreigners could build factories, but they could not pull billions out of Chinese securities in a single day. Domestically, it introduced “dual-track” pricing: planned quotas were fulfilled at state prices, while output above the quota was sold at market prices, so that the market grew alongside the plan rather than on its ruins—a mechanism described in detail by the economist Barry Naughton. In 1994, China unified the two exchange rates of the yuan into one, significantly devaluing its currency, and the Asian crisis barely touched it. In 1998, contrary to IMF advice, Malaysia imposed restrictions on capital outflows—and, in the view of many economists, emerged from the crisis no worse than its neighbors, though that conclusion remains disputed. Europe responded to exchange-rate instability in the opposite way: the Maastricht Treaty of 1992 provided for a common currency, and on January 1, 1999, eleven countries introduced the euro for noncash transactions. It was backed by no metal—only by a treaty and trust in a common central bank.

To sum up, the turning point in money came down to three changes. After the abandonment of gold convertibility, the exchange rate and purchasing power of money depended on monetary policy, the state of the economy, and trust in institutions. The turnover of financial markets increased, but transaction volumes cannot be counted as surplus: a single asset can be resold many times. Foreign debt denominated in another currency became a source of severe crises for some countries; how large this channel was among all international flows has not been calculated here. Who set the rules governing these flows, and in whose interests, is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

In 1982, the American economist Mancur Olson published *The Rise and Decline of Nations*. At first glance, its main argument sounds paradoxical: prolonged peace and stability can harm an economy. The longer a society lives without upheaval, the more time organized groups have to emerge—industry associations, professional guilds, trade unions, lobbyists—each seeking a piece of the common pie for its members: a privilege, a tariff, a ban on competitors, a guaranteed position. Each piece is small on its own, and fighting it is worthwhile for no one. Taken together, however, they make the economy sluggish, like a ship encrusted with barnacles. Olson used this to explain why Germany and Japan, defeated in war and swept clean of the old groups, grew faster than Britain, where those groups survived. A little earlier, in 1974, the economist Anne Krueger had introduced a term—Gordon Tullock had described the idea shortly before—that we have already used in this book: “rent-seeking,” the expenditure of effort and money not on creating wealth but on obtaining the right to take wealth already created.

These ideas fit their moment. By the end of the 1970s, many people felt that the postwar state had become just such a barnacle-covered ship. Nationalized British companies received subsidies year after year; Londoners waited months for a telephone connection; airline tickets in the United States were expensive because a federal agency approved routes and fares. The Nobel Committee also marked the intellectual turn: in 1974, the economics prize went to Friedrich Hayek—the same man whose debate with the planners we examined in Chapter 13—and in 1976 to Milton Friedman. Ideas regarded as marginal for thirty years had moved to the center.

The turn began neither with Thatcher nor with Reagan. American airline deregulation was enacted in 1978 by the Democrat Jimmy Carter; trucking and railroads followed. Fares fell, and millions of people for whom flying had once been a luxury took to the air—although some of the old airlines went bankrupt and their workers lost many of the gains they had won. But with the arrival of Thatcher in 1979 and Reagan in 1981, change became a program. Reagan cut the top income-tax rate from seventy percent to fifty percent in 1981 and then to twenty-eight percent in 1986. His supporters promised that the tax cuts would pay for themselves through rapid growth; they did not, and US government debt roughly tripled during the 1980s—largely because military spending was rising at the same time, as we will discuss in the section on deadweight waste.

Britain became a laboratory of privatization—the transfer of state-owned companies to private owners. British Telecom was floated on the stock exchange in 1984, and its shares were bought by around two million people, many of whom had never owned a security before. British Gas was sold in 1986 under the advertising slogan “Tell Sid”—an imaginary neighbor who was to be invited to take a stake. Airlines, steel, water, electricity, and railways followed. Thatcher spoke of a “property-owning democracy”: everyone should have a stake in the economy. Its most widespread element was the “Right to Buy,” established by a 1980 law: tenants of council housing could buy their homes at a large discount. More than a million families became homeowners during the 1980s alone.

The results are disputed, and fairness requires showing both sides. Many former state-owned companies became markedly more efficient: telephones were installed in days rather than months, and British Airways went from loss-making to profitable. On the other hand, many of the shares bought by the “Sids” were quickly resold. The number of individual shareholders tripled, but the share of equities held by private individuals continued the decline that had begun in the 1960s: twenty years later it was half its pre-privatization level, while funds and foreign investors controlled most of the market. Water companies—natural monopolies with no competitors—took on debt, paid generous dividends, and argued with the regulator for decades over investment in pipes. Local authorities were largely forbidden from spending the proceeds from council-house sales on new construction, and by the end of the century there was noticeably less affordable housing for people on low incomes. In other words, privatization generally promoted development where it created competition; where a monopoly merely passed from public to private hands, it often became a right to collect rents. And the proceeds from the sale of public property, like the oil revenues discussed above, went largely toward the treasury’s current expenditure.

The most characteristic institutional event of the era was the “Big Bang” of October 27, 1986—the overnight deregulation of the London Stock Exchange: fixed commissions were abolished, foreign banks were allowed to buy British brokerage firms, and trading moved from the exchange floor to screens. The City of London once again became a global financial center. But the decision illustrates how the very question of “who decides” had changed. The rules by which trillions were distributed were now increasingly written within the narrow circles of finance ministries, central banks, and international organizations, while voters learned about them after the fact.

The same rules were exported to poor countries through the debt crisis. The IMF and World Bank, discussed in earlier chapters, attached conditions to their loans: cut the budget, abolish subsidies for bread and fuel, sell state-owned companies, open markets. The word “conditions” is the key. Decisions that changed the lives of millions of people in Mexico, Ghana, or Zambia were made in negotiations between ministers and fund officials; the citizens of those countries learned of them when bread and transport became more expensive. Sometimes the conditions treated a real disease—ruined public finances and the printing press. Sometimes, as the fund’s own staff later acknowledged, the same prescription was applied to very different patients. In *Globalization and Its Discontents* (2002), the Nobel laureate Joseph Stiglitz, formerly chief economist of the World Bank, accused the IMF of putting the interests of creditors above those of debtor countries. The fund’s defenders replied that it arrived only after the fire was already burning, and that without its money the collapse would have been deeper.

Chile conducted the earliest and most brutal experiment of this kind. On September 11, 1973, the military under General Augusto Pinochet overthrew the government of Salvador Allende; with it ended Project Cybersyn, described in Chapter 13. The junta’s economic program was written by the “Chicago Boys”—Chilean economists trained by Friedman and his colleagues: privatization, trade liberalization, and, in 1981, the replacement of state pensions with private funded accounts. Over time, Chile became one of Latin America’s richest countries, and supporters credit the reforms. Critics, including the economist Ricardo Ffrench-Davis, point out that rapid growth began only after 1985, when the original program was substantially revised, and continued under democracy after 1990. But in 1982–1983 the country suffered a collapse in which output fell by more than a tenth, and the government had to take control of much of the banking system. All of this took place under a dictatorship, without free trade unions or elections, and with thousands killed or disappeared. The Chilean case is awkward for both sides of the argument: market reforms ultimately produced growth, but they were imposed by force rather than chosen.

China’s reform policy was entirely different. In December 1978, a plenum of the Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party adopted a course of “reform and opening up,” and Deng Xiaoping, who had repeatedly been removed from office under Mao, became the country’s de facto leader. There was no master plan for reform. Its emblem was the saying “cross the river by feeling the stones”—usually associated with Deng, although it appears to have been used first by his ally Chen Yun. The mechanism worked like this: an innovation was first permitted in a single village, county, or special zone; if it worked, it was extended to a province and then to the country; if it failed, it was quietly wound down. The German political scientist Sebastian Heilmann called this “experimentation under hierarchy.” The household responsibility system, special economic zones, and dual-track prices all followed this path from small experiment to general rule.

A second mechanism was also at work: local governments received the right to retain part of the taxes they collected, while the careers of local officials came to depend on economic growth in the territory under their authority. Using data, the economists Hongbin Li and Li-An Zhou (2005) showed that provincial leaders who achieved higher growth were promoted more often. Provinces competed for investors like the medieval Italian cities of Chapter 7. This generated astonishing energy—and new diseases: the pursuit of targets at any cost, local protectionism, pollution omitted from the reports, and corruption wherever an official controlled land and could allocate it to a developer in return for a share. All this took place without independent courts or a free press, and in June 1989, when students and workers gathered in Tiananmen Square to demand political change, the protest was suppressed by the army. China’s path showed that market incentives and a gradual transition tested through experience could produce unprecedented growth even without democracy—but the bill for the absence of feedback from below would come due in the chapters ahead.

Russia took the opposite path: rapidly, all at once, according to a single plan. The reformers’ argument—advanced by Yegor Gaidar, Anatoly Chubais, and their Western advisers, such as Jeffrey Sachs—was less economic than political: the window of opportunity would close and the old nomenklatura would return, so change had to be made irreversible while there was still a chance. The acceleration was not accidental, but neither were its consequences predetermined by a single conspiracy: it was a deliberate political choice made amid inflation, a weak state, and a struggle for control of enterprises. In October 1992, every citizen was issued a privatization check—a voucher with a face value of ten thousand rubles that could be exchanged for shares in enterprises. The intention was to give the entire people a stake in the country’s property. In practice, the voucher did not guarantee a fixed share in any particular enterprise; it was a bearer document that could be sold. With incomes collapsing and no intelligible share market, many people sold it to buyers for a small amount, handed it to voucher investment funds, or exchanged it for shares in their own factory. Control therefore concentrated in the hands of enterprise directors, funds, and intermediaries, while most citizens received neither control over an asset nor a durable income—only cash once or shares that were difficult to realize.

Then, in 1995, a state short of budget funds—and of money for the 1996 political campaign—conducted the so-called loans-for-shares auctions: several large banks lent the government money against collateral consisting of blocks of shares in the largest companies—Norilsk Nickel, Yukos, Sibneft, and others. The state failed to repay the loans—and, in the view of many researchers, never intended to—and the shares passed to the lenders. The auctions were often organized by the very banks that took part in them, competitors were excluded, and, according to the journalists Chrystia Freeland in *Sale of the Century* (2000) and David Hoffman in *The Oligarchs* (2002), the prices were many times lower than the values the market assigned to these companies only a few years later. The direct beneficiaries were bankers and businessmen who had access to credit, state-owned blocks, and political connections; managers and owners also benefited indirectly when the companies’ assets rose sharply after 1999. Defenders of the reforms, such as the economists Andrei Shleifer and Daniel Treisman in *Without a Map* (2000), replied that, however questionable the transactions, the new owners invested money in the companies and increased production, whereas under state ownership the companies would have continued to disintegrate. Data from Sergei Guriev and Andrei Rachinsky confirm that by the early 2000s the new owners’ companies operated more efficiently than the rest. There is some truth in this: at the turn of the century, oil production did indeed begin to rise. But the improved performance of the companies does not erase the fact that price and control were distributed in favor of a narrow circle. The main difference from China lay not in speed but in institutions. The market was introduced in a country with no independent courts, no functioning tax service, no enforceable bankruptcy law, and no press capable of following transactions over time. In October 1993, the conflict between the president and parliament was resolved by tank fire against the building of the Supreme Soviet. When rules are written and rewritten on the fly by a narrow circle of people, the winner is not the one who produces best but the one sitting closest to the pen.

Poland showed that speed was not the only issue. Its “Balcerowicz Plan,” launched on January 1, 1990, was no less of a shock than Russia’s reforms, but after enduring a severe contraction the country began growing as early as 1992 and became the region’s most successful economy. It had several advantages: private farming that had survived socialism, a living memory of the market, and the prospect of joining the European Community with its ready-made rules and courts. And there was one detail worth remembering: in 1991, Poland’s Western government creditors wrote off roughly half of the debt it owed them. Debt forgiveness, familiar to us from Mesopotamia, proved to be a working instrument at the end of the twentieth century as well.

Supranational rules were being constructed at the same time. The Maastricht Treaty, signed a year earlier, took effect in 1993 and transformed the European Community into the European Union. The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) followed in 1994, and on January 1, 1995, the GATT discussed in the previous chapter gave way to the World Trade Organization (WTO), with its own mechanism for resolving trade disputes whose decisions member states undertook to obey. The rules of exchange became more global and predictable—and even more remote from the people they affected.

Against this background, a book published in 1990 and long left in the shadows is especially interesting. In *Governing the Commons*, the American political scientist Elinor Ostrom studied many cases in which people jointly used resources that belonged to no individual alone—alpine pastures in Switzerland, irrigation canals in Spain and the Philippines, fisheries in Turkey. The prevailing theory of the time, known as the “tragedy of the commons,” held that such resources were doomed to plunder and could be saved only by the state or by private ownership. Ostrom showed that there was a third way: communities that made their own rules, could see who had taken how much, could punish violators, and could change the rules with the participation of the users themselves had preserved their resources for centuries. She received the Nobel Prize only in 2009, but the question she posed bears directly on the central theme of this book: who controls the surplus, and how visible is it who has taken how much?

To sum up, the era of the turning point reassembled power over the surplus. From the hands of states accustomed to controlling it through nationalized industries, production targets, and fixed exchange rates, it passed partly to markets and private owners and partly to independent central banks and international organizations. Where the transition proceeded gradually, was tested through experience, and was built on functioning rules, it unleashed production. Where rules were written quickly by a narrow circle and no one could verify their enforcement, the surplus went to those sitting closest to the table. The degree to which workers and citizens participated in such decisions varied by country and institution; these examples establish no single direction common to every version of the transition.

## What Fostered Development

After the story of strikes, debt traps, and loans-for-shares auctions, it may seem that the era of the turning point did nothing but destroy. That is not so. It was during these very decades that humanity made several of the cheapest and most rewarding investments in its history. Almost none of them made the front pages the way Volcker or Thatcher did, but if we ask which part of the surplus of 1973–2000 yielded the greatest return, the answer is surprising.

Let us begin with the largest mechanism—the right to control the fruits of one’s own labor. We have already seen what happened in Xiaogang. On the scale of the country, it looked like this: between 1978 and 1984, according to estimates by the economist Justin Yifu Lin, China’s grain output rose by about a third, and he attributed roughly half of the increase in agricultural output specifically to the shift to the household responsibility system; higher procurement prices and fertilizers accounted for the rest. The land, the people, and the tools remained the same. Only one thing changed: the peasant knew that an extra sack of rice would go to his family rather than disappear into the team’s common pot. According to World Bank estimates, almost nine out of ten Chinese lived below the extreme-poverty line in the early 1980s; by the early 2000s, the figure was about one third. None of Mao’s campaigns, for all their slogans, achieved anything comparable.

But how this was done matters. China’s reforms fostered development not because “the market is always right,” but because each innovation was first tested on a small scale and spread only when it produced a visible result. A mistake in one village or one special zone was cheap; a mistake imposed on the entire country at once, as during the Great Leap Forward, cost tens of millions of lives. The ability to make small mistakes and learn from them is one of the most underrated development-enhancing qualities of any system.

India underwent a similar turn, only later and in a different way. In the summer of 1991, by various calculations, the country had enough foreign-exchange reserves for only two or three weeks of imports, and the government sent dozens of tons of gold from the Reserve Bank’s holdings to London as collateral for an emergency loan. For a country where gold is a family treasure, this was a humiliation. The new finance minister, the economist Manmohan Singh, announced in his budget speech of July 24, 1991, the dismantling of the “License Raj”—the system under which opening a factory, expanding output, or buying a machine abroad required official permission, and sometimes dozens of permits. For decades, entrepreneurs had spent their energy not on production but on obtaining papers—a pure case of rent-seeking in Krueger’s sense. After most licenses were abolished, growth accelerated, and the programmers of Bangalore and Hyderabad were soon working for the whole world. In fairness, the economists Dani Rodrik and Arvind Subramanian (2004) showed that the acceleration had begun in the 1980s, when the authorities became friendlier toward existing businesses, so 1991 was more a continuation than a beginning. But the general conclusion remains unchanged: when barriers protecting someone else’s rent are removed, the energy of those who want to produce is released.

The second mechanism was predictable money. Victory over inflation came at a high price, and we will return to it in the next section. But the result itself fostered development. When prices rise by ten or twenty percent a year, people expend effort protecting themselves from depreciation: they stockpile goods, flee from money into real estate and gold, and hesitate to make long-term investments. When inflation falls to two or three percent, they can plan years ahead, take out a thirty-year mortgage, and save for retirement. In the language of our framework, the costs of trust fall. Money once again became what the florin and guilder had been at their best: a common ruler with which the future could be measured.

The third mechanism was freely shared knowledge, and here the era perhaps gave its most generous gift to the generations that followed. On January 1, 1983, ARPANET, a network created with funding from the US Department of Defense to connect university computers, switched to a common set of rules for transmitting data—the TCP/IP protocols. These rules belonged to no one and could be used by anyone, and precisely for that reason more and more networks began to connect to them: the internet was born. In March 1989, the British physicist Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, the European particle-physics laboratory near Geneva, proposed a way to link documents on different computers—the World Wide Web. In April 1993, CERN announced that anyone could use the Web software free of charge. Had a fee been charged for it, today’s internet would most likely resemble a collection of separate paid networks. Around the same time, in 1991, the Finnish student Linus Torvalds posted online the kernel of an operating system that came to be called Linux, and thousands of volunteers around the world began improving it without being paid. Within ten years, it had become the foundation for a vast share of the world’s servers.

This is worth remembering. The era’s most development-enhancing technologies were often born in government laboratories and universities, shared as a common good, and only then became the foundations on which private companies grew. This does not negate the role of the market—without Intel, Microsoft, and Apple, the personal computer would not have reached every home—but it shows that knowledge given to everyone can yield more than knowledge locked behind a fence.

The fourth mechanism was public goods that no individual could have purchased alone. In October 1977, Ali Maow Maalin, a hospital cook in Somalia, contracted smallpox—the last person in the world to catch it naturally. He recovered. In May 1980, the World Health Assembly declared smallpox eradicated. A disease that, according to medical historians, had killed hundreds of millions of people in the twentieth century alone was gone. The entire campaign, conducted in intensified form by the World Health Organization (WHO) from 1967 onward, cost, by its own estimate, about $300 million, roughly a third of it contributed by international donors. The savings from vaccinations and quarantines alone, once vaccination ended, more than repaid that sum every year. If we are looking for an example of surplus invested with the greatest return in history, it is hard to find a better one.

Beside it stands a much humbler story: a solution of salt and sugar in clean water. In the 1960s and 1970s, doctors in Bangladesh and India showed that if given in time, this solution could save a patient with cholera or severe diarrhea from dying of dehydration—without intravenous drips or hospitals, and for pennies. In the 1980s, the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF), under James Grant, launched its “child survival revolution”: immunization, oral rehydration—the medical name for this treatment by drinking—breastfeeding, and monitoring children’s growth. According to estimates by the UN Inter-agency Group, global mortality among children under five fell from roughly 145 per thousand in 1970, based on UN reconstructions, to less than eighty by the end of the century. Millions of children born in poor countries during these years grew up and themselves became workers, parents, and inventors.

And in 1985, scientists from the British Antarctic Survey published evidence in *Nature* of a “hole” in the ozone layer above Antarctica—a seasonal thinning of the atmospheric layer that protects life from harsh ultraviolet radiation. Ozone is indeed constantly formed and destroyed by ultraviolet light, and the Antarctic hole varies with the polar night, the return of sunlight, and the breakdown of the cold polar vortex. But that does not disprove the role of the gases: chlorine and bromine released from long-lived chlorofluorocarbons trigger catalytic ozone destruction on polar clouds, and spring sunlight accelerates the process. The hole’s disappearance after warming and the mixing of air is therefore not evidence that CFCs were irrelevant; it is part of the same seasonal mechanism. Just two years later, in September 1987, the Montreal Protocol was signed, providing for the gradual phaseout of these substances. Industry, which initially resisted, found substitutes—but this was not a disinterested gift: DuPont and ICI were among the first to commercialize HFC-134a, and patents and early control of production gave them a commercial advantage; patent protection for the refrigerant ended in key jurisdictions by 2005. In time, every country in the world ratified the protocol. The ozone hole was not expected to disappear immediately: because CFCs persist in the atmosphere, recovery takes decades. This is a rare example of humanity recognizing a shared threat, agreeing to visible and verifiable rules, and carrying them out, while also creating a new market for chemical companies.

The fifth mechanism was access to credit—but not credit as a ready-made way out of poverty. In 1976, the Bangladeshi economist Muhammad Yunus lent twenty-seven dollars from his own pocket to forty-two women in the village of Jobra who wove bamboo stools; until then, they had obtained raw materials from middlemen at extortionate interest rates. From this experiment grew Grameen Bank (1983), which made small loans to poor people, above all women, without collateral but with the guarantee of a group of neighbors. Microcredit became fashionable, and Yunus received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006. But the commercial model often shifted the risk onto the borrower: once fees, compulsory savings, and short maturities were included, effective annual rates exceeded one hundred percent in some documented programs, while the profits went to microfinance institutions and their investors. When economists conducted rigorous randomized trials in six countries in the 2000s and 2010s—the findings were brought together in 2015 by Abhijit Banerjee, Dean Karlan, and Jonathan Zinman—they found that microcredit sometimes expanded a business or helped households smooth cash shortages, but on average it did not reduce poverty or produce transformative income growth. In some places, such as the Indian state of Andhra Pradesh in 2010, it generated debt crises. The development-enhancing discovery was therefore not the loan itself: durable poverty reduction requires affordable credit, protection against debt spirals, and a viable source of income—not merely access to money.

Finally, the sixth mechanism was competition where monopoly had existed before. On January 1, 1984, AT&T, which had controlled almost all telephone service in the United States for decades, was broken up by order of an American court. Long-distance calls became sharply cheaper, and new companies and technologies—from modems to cellular service—grew in the space that opened up. Airline deregulation, discussed above, made flying accessible to millions. In all these cases, the gains came not from a change of owner but from the fact that consumers gained a choice and producers gained an incentive to improve rather than defend what they already controlled.

Taken together, these stories reveal a common pattern. Development was fostered by whatever widened the circle of people able to produce and decide: the peasant with a right to his own harvest, the entrepreneur free of a licensing barrier, the programmer with open code, the woman with a small loan. It was fostered by small trials with visible results that could be repeated or reversed. It was fostered by common rules agreed openly and verifiable by everyone—from internet protocols to the Montreal Protocol. Some of these investments cost relatively little, but comparing their total return with other expenditures requires calculation. Where that other spending went—into destruction and deadweight waste—is the subject of the next two sections.

## What Destroyed

In the summer of 1994, demographers examining the latest Russian statistics initially thought there was an error in the data. Male life expectancy in Russia—the average number of years a newborn boy would live if mortality remained at that year’s rates—had been around sixty-four and a half years in 1987. By 1994, it had fallen to roughly fifty-seven and a half. Seven years lost in seven years: in peacetime, without war or epidemic, no industrial country in the twentieth century had experienced such a collapse. Those dying were above all men in the prime of life, between forty and sixty—from heart attacks and strokes, poisoning by adulterated alcohol, murder, suicide, and accidents. At the same time, the birth rate fell sharply, and the two lines on the graphs—deaths and births—crossed. The image became known as the “Russian Cross.”

This is the right scene with which to begin the section on destruction, because it shows the central lesson of the era of the turning point: the most valuable capital an economy can destroy is not factories or money, but people.

The causes of the mortality crisis of the 1990s are still debated. The demographers Vladimir Shkolnikov, France Meslé, and their colleagues showed that alcohol played a central role: mortality rose and fell almost in step with consumption, while Gorbachev’s anti-alcohol campaign of 1985–1988, which temporarily reduced deaths, merely delayed the blow. Other researchers, including the economists Elizabeth Brainerd and Giovanni Andrea Cornia, placed greater emphasis on the stress caused by job loss, the evaporation of savings, the collapse of the familiar world, and the breakdown of health care. Most likely both are true: alcohol was the means by which stress killed. In 2009, the epidemiologists David Stuckler, Lawrence King, and Martin McKee linked the rise in mortality to the speed of mass privatization; the economists John Earle and Scott Gehlbach replied that the relationship disappeared under more careful comparison. The dispute shows how difficult it is to separate the effects of reform from those of the wider collapse. Estimates differ as to how many lives this decline cost above the normal level, but the toll runs into the millions. Ukraine, Belarus, Kazakhstan, and the Baltic states experienced similar, though less severe, declines. Poland, the Czech Republic, and Hungary, where the transition involved a smaller collapse and more functional institutions, knew no such catastrophe.

The scale of the economic decline was also unprecedented. According to official statistics, by 1998 Russia’s gross domestic product—the value of everything produced in the country in a year—was about forty percent below its 1990 level. The statistics of those years captured the shadow economy poorly, and the true contraction was probably smaller. But even after adjustments, it was comparable to the Great Depression in the United States described in Chapter 12, and lasted almost twice as long. What exactly was being destroyed matters too. It was not only obsolete factories making products no one wanted that closed; closing them could in itself have been a step toward development. Scientific institutes, design bureaus, and laboratories capable of making complex things also fell apart. According to Russian science statistics, the number of researchers in the country fell by more than half during the 1990s: some went into trade, others left for the United States, Germany, or Israel. Knowledge built up over generations dispersed in a few years—and proved far harder to rebuild than to demolish.

The second form of destruction, inherited by this era from all those before it, was war. After the end of the Cold War, many hoped for a “peace dividend,” but the 1990s began with the breakup of Yugoslavia. As recently as the 1980s, the country had seemed one of the most prosperous in the socialist world: its citizens traveled freely to work in Western Europe, and tourists from across the continent came to its resorts. From 1991 to 1995, wars raged in Croatia and Bosnia and Herzegovina. Research conducted for the International Criminal Tribunal for the former Yugoslavia found that in Bosnia alone around one hundred thousand people were killed and more than two million became refugees. Sarajevo spent almost four years under siege; residents fetched water under sniper fire. In November 1993, artillery destroyed Mostar’s Old Bridge, which had stood for more than four hundred years. The Yugoslav economy, bound together by thousands of chains among the republics, disintegrated along with the country; by the end of the war, according to World Bank estimates, output in Bosnia had fallen to roughly a quarter or a fifth of its prewar level.

An even greater number of lives were lost in a war less often remembered in Europe. From 1980 to 1988, Iraq and Iran fought over border territories and regional power: a positional slaughter of trenches, human-wave assaults by poorly armed volunteers, chemical weapons, and attacks on cities and oil tankers. Estimates of the death toll range from several hundred thousand to a million. Two oil-producing countries that had received an enormous surplus from the price shocks of the 1970s burned it up in eight years of war that ended almost where it had begun. Then, in Rwanda in 1994, between five hundred thousand and one million people were killed in one hundred days, according to varying estimates—the UN gives a figure of around eight hundred thousand—most of them Tutsi. The genocide was prepared openly, through a radio station that called for killing, and the outside world, watching it happen, did almost nothing.

The third form of destruction was the debasement of money—an old disease familiar from third-century Rome and Germany in 1923. The end of gold convertibility opened new opportunities for it. In Bolivia in 1985, prices rose, by varying estimates, more than a hundredfold in a year; in Peru in 1990, more than seventyfold. In Argentina and Brazil, the hyperinflations of the late 1980s devalued wages faster than people could spend them. Ukraine experienced a more than hundredfold rise in prices in 1993. Hyperinflation destroys more than savings. It destroys society’s very ability to calculate and make agreements: prices and wages rapidly lose predictability, and long-term lending and construction become difficult. The distribution of gains and losses depends on who holds assets, debts, and income in different currencies. This is extraction in its purest form, carried out without any vote.

A related calamity was the destruction of trust through pyramid schemes. In societies encountering the market for the first time and unfamiliar with its rules, promises of “returns” in the hundreds of percent found millions of trusting victims. In Russia in 1994, Sergei Mavrodi’s company MMM, whose television commercials everyone knew, collapsed; estimates put the number of investors at several million. Albania’s case was more terrible still. By 1996, a large share of the population of this poor country had invested savings—often money from the sale of apartments and livestock, or remittances from relatives abroad—in pyramid “funds.” According to IMF estimates, at their peak the funds’ nominal liabilities amounted to roughly half of the country’s annual GDP. Early in 1997 the pyramids collapsed, people took to the streets, army arsenals were looted, and for several months the country descended into chaos in which, by varying estimates, around two thousand people were killed. A pyramid scheme is the perfect example of extraction disguised as exchange: money is simply moved from some people’s pockets into those of others, while the illusion of wealth exists only as long as new investors continue to arrive.

There was also slow destruction, without gunfire. The debt crisis of the 1980s, discussed in the section on money, struck not only at incomes but also at what those incomes were supposed to create in the future. In several countries of Africa and Latin America, budget cuts under “structural adjustment” programs were accompanied by fees for schooling and hospital services and by reductions in teachers’ and doctors’ pay. How much these programs, rather than the crisis itself and poor governance, were responsible for the decline of schools and hospitals remains disputed: defenders of the IMF point out that the money was unavailable in any event. But the outcome for sub-Saharan Africa is known: according to Angus Maddison’s estimates, income per person in the region was lower in 2000 than in 1980. Twenty years had been lost. The HIV/AIDS epidemic compounded this failure. By the end of the century, in some southern African countries—Botswana, Zimbabwe, Zambia—one fifth or more of adults were infected, and life expectancy fell by ten to fifteen years or more, erasing the gains of previous decades. The disease killed above all young adults—teachers, nurses, workers, parents—the very people in whom society had already invested years of education.

Finally, there was the destruction of nature—the form of capital that remains invisible in the accounts until it is gone. The Aral Sea, once the world’s fourth-largest lake by area, began to shrink in the 1960s, when the rivers that fed it, the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, were diverted to irrigate the cotton fields of Central Asia. By the end of the 1990s, it had lost more than half its area and broken into separate bodies of water; fishing villages lay dozens of kilometers from the shore, while the wind carried salt and pesticide residues from the exposed seabed. The planned economy traded a living sea for cotton—and none of those who paid for the exchange took part in the decision. In December 1984, a cloud of the poisonous gas methyl isocyanate escaped from a factory owned by the American company Union Carbide in Bhopal, India. Officially, around four thousand people died in the first days; independent doctors and human-rights advocates estimate fifteen to twenty thousand deaths when later fatalities are included, and the state authorities recognized more than half a million people as victims. Investigations found that the plant’s safety systems had been disabled or were not working. Together with Chernobyl, discussed above, Bhopal revealed the same pattern under two entirely different systems, planned and market-based: disaster ripens where the benefits of economizing on safety go to one group while the risks fall on another that has no voice.

What unites these very different stories? Almost nowhere was destruction the result of a shortage of resources or technology. Russia’s collapse occurred in a country with oil, gas, an educated population, and strong science. Albania’s pyramids grew not out of poverty as such but from the absence of rules and oversight. The Aral Sea and Bhopal were destroyed by decisions made far from the people who had to pay for them. The common denominator was a broken feedback loop: those who decided where to direct the surplus and where to economize did not bear the consequences of their decisions, while those who did bear them could not influence the decision and often did not even know about it. Where that link survived—in the countries of Central Europe, with elections, a free press, and the prospect of common European rules—the transition was painful too, but it did not lead to mass death or civil war.

One further observation is needed to complete the picture. The destruction of this era most often struck those who had the least say in the decisions: pensioners whose savings were devalued, children in schools without teachers, mining towns, residents of the Aral Sea region. The gains from the same processes—from loans-for-shares auctions, from betting against a falling currency, from economizing on safety—went to a narrow circle. The surplus did not vanish without a trace: it changed owners. But some of it truly burned away—in wars, in hyperinflation, in years of life lost and knowledge that no one can restore. And some of it, as the next section will show, was spent peacefully and legally enough, but just as fruitlessly.

## What Was Deadweight Waste

October 1988, New York. The board of RJR Nabisco, the tobacco and food giant that made Winston cigarettes and Oreo cookies, is considering bids to buy the entire company. Two groups are competing: a team led by the company’s own chief executive, Ross Johnson, and Kohlberg Kravis Roberts (KKR), a firm specializing in leveraged buyouts—purchasing companies with borrowed money that is then repaid from the acquired company’s own earnings. The bids rise week after week, and KKR ultimately wins at a price of around $25 billion—the largest deal of its kind in history at the time. The journalists Bryan Burrough and John Helyar described the battle in *Barbarians at the Gate* (1990), and it reads like a novel: private jets, dinners costing thousands of dollars, pride, and wounded feelings. The factories, tobacco plantations, bakeries, and workers remained the same. The deal did not produce a single new cookie. But the company acquired an enormous debt, which it spent years repaying by selling divisions and cutting jobs, while the bankers, lawyers, and consultants on both sides divided hundreds of millions of dollars in fees, according to estimates at the time.

This is deadweight waste in the sense used by this book: enormous human effort and ability invested in transferring rights to existing wealth from one set of hands to another, without creating any additional wealth in the process.

In fairness, let us hear the defense. The economist Michael Jensen, the same man discussed in the section on the organization of labor, argued that the takeover wave of the 1980s treated a real disease: bloated conglomerates that spent profits on prestige acquisitions and unnecessary layers of management. Debt, in his view, imposed discipline: a company that had to pay interest could not afford corporate jets. Some takeovers did make companies more efficient, and using plant-census data, the economists Frank Lichtenberg and Donald Siegel showed that the productivity of acquired plants often increased after the deals. But another story unfolded alongside this one. Michael Milken of Drexel Burnham Lambert built the market for “junk bonds”—high-yield, high-risk debt used to finance takeovers. In 1990 he pleaded guilty to violations of securities laws, and the bank itself went bankrupt. Where restructuring ended and gambling with other people’s money began was often impossible for anyone to say, including the participants.

More graphic still was the American savings-and-loan crisis. Savings and loan associations were small banks that for decades had taken deposits and issued fixed-rate mortgages. When interest rates soared under Volcker, they began losing money, and in the early 1980s they were “liberated”: they were allowed to invest in almost anything, while retaining government deposit insurance. The result was a perfect machine for deadweight waste: if a risky bet paid off, the owners kept the profit; if it failed, the government covered the loss. Depositors’ money flowed into empty shopping centers and office buildings in Texas, resort condominiums, and dubious loans to friends. Roughly a thousand such institutions closed between 1986 and 1995, and according to estimates by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, the direct bill for American taxpayers was around $125 billion. In the framework of this book, this was both deadweight waste—concrete poured not for use but for the sake of a deal—and extraction: private gains, public losses.

Stepping back from individual scandals, the most uncomfortable question for the era is this: how much does the financial system itself cost, and did it become cheaper? In 1984, the Nobel laureate James Tobin delivered a lecture, “On the Efficiency of the Financial System,” in which he expressed concern that society was directing ever more resources—and above all ever more talented young people—into financial activities remote from production, even though their private returns were far higher than their social returns. Thirty years later, the economist Thomas Philippon (2015) tested this against more than a century of data. According to calculations by Robin Greenwood and David Scharfstein, finance’s share of the American economy rose from less than three percent in 1950 to five percent in 1980 and more than eight percent by the middle of the 2000s. Yet the cost of its central service—moving a dollar from saver to borrower—barely changed over a century, remaining around two cents per dollar. This is not a verdict against finance as such: the economist Ross Levine, synthesizing dozens of studies, showed that on average countries with more developed financial systems grow faster. But Jean-Louis Arcand, Enrico Berkes, and Ugo Panizza found that this benefit ends when private-sector credit exceeds roughly a year’s output; beyond that point, additional finance tends to slow growth. Computers, networks, and new instruments, which should have made intermediation cheaper, did not do so: in this sector, the gains from technology went not to customers but to the intermediaries themselves. This does not mean that finance is useless—without credit and insurance, the modern economy would not function for a day—but a substantial part of its growth in this era was growth in the trading of rights to the surplus, not growth in the surplus itself.

The same logic can be seen in compensation at the top. According to calculations by the Economic Policy Institute, in 1965 the chief executive of a large American company earned about twenty times as much as a typical worker; by 2000, the ratio was several hundred to one. In part, this reflected the genuinely increased scale of companies. But stock options now made up the largest share of compensation, and a company’s share price could be raised not only by a new product but also by layoffs, share buybacks, or a well-timed earnings announcement. Effort spent making a company look better rather than work better is deadweight waste too.

The era’s largest bubble, however, grew not in America but in Japan. In the second half of the 1980s, Japanese banks, flush with cheap money, competed to finance purchases of land and shares, and the prices of both rose as though they had no limit. A popular calculation at the time held that the land beneath the Imperial Palace in central Tokyo was worth more than all the real estate in California. On December 29, 1989, Tokyo’s Nikkei index reached nearly thirty-nine thousand points; three years later it was worth less than half as much. Land prices in the largest cities continued falling for another decade and a half. Banks were left with a mountain of bad debts that they refused to write off for years, keeping hopeless “zombie” companies afloat, and the country entered a “lost decade” of slow growth. The bubble left other traces as well: concrete embankments, bridges, and roads in sparsely populated prefectures, built in the 1990s as stimulus measures and mocked by Japanese critics as signs of a “construction state.”

Deadweight waste, however, was not an invention of the market. It flourished wherever a single person made the decisions. In 1984, on Nicolae Ceaușescu’s orders, construction began in Bucharest on the House of the People, one of the largest administrative buildings in the world. A large part of the city’s historic center, including churches and residential districts, was demolished to make way for it and the boulevard leading to it, and tens of thousands of residents were relocated. At the same time, Ceaușescu decided to repay the country’s foreign debts ahead of schedule, and throughout the second half of the 1980s Romania exported food while its citizens received bread by ration card and sat through winters in apartments where the heat and electricity were cut off. The debt was repaid in the spring of 1989; in December of the same year the regime fell and the dictator was executed. In Côte d’Ivoire, President Félix Houphouët-Boigny built the Basilica of Our Lady of Peace in his hometown of Yamoussoukro, by some measures larger than St. Peter’s Basilica in Rome. It was consecrated in 1990, when the country was deep in a debt crisis. These were direct descendants of the palaces and tombs of Chapters 3 and 5—with the difference that the pharaohs did not have to negotiate with the IMF at the same time.

But the largest item of deadweight expenditure in this era, as in all those before it, remained armaments. The Cold War went through its final escalation in the 1980s. Under Reagan, the United States increased military spending to roughly six percent of gross domestic product; in March 1983, the president announced the Strategic Defense Initiative, a system for intercepting missiles in space that newspapers immediately nicknamed “Star Wars.” Tens of billions of dollars were spent on it, but a working shield never appeared. The Soviet Union spent, by varying estimates, between fifteen and more than twenty percent of its output on defense—the precise figure remains unknown: the official defense budget understated spending by several times, while a substantial share was hidden in the accounts of civilian ministries. The response to the American space shuttle was the Energia–Buran system. On November 15, 1988, *Buran* made its only flight—uncrewed and under automatic control—and landed flawlessly. It never flew again; the program was closed in 1993. The same category includes the thousands of tanks and missiles produced by both sides and later cut up under disarmament treaties: metal and the labor of engineers and workers turned into objects whose best possible fate was never to be used.

Here, as in previous chapters, there are ambiguous cases. The American military built the GPS satellite-navigation system, but in 1983, after a Soviet interceptor shot down a South Korean passenger airliner that had strayed off course, Reagan announced that the system would be opened to civilian use. Civilians gained full accuracy in 2000, and today every smartphone uses it. The Baikal–Amur Mainline—a gigantic construction project of the 1970s and 1980s that drew hundreds of thousands of young people from across the Soviet Union—long remained a railway with very little traffic and was not placed in permanent operation until 2003; in the twenty-first century, trains carrying coal and ore to Pacific ports did begin to use it. A project to divert part of the flow of the northern rivers to Central Asia, on whose design years had been spent, was halted by a Politburo decision in 1986 after protests by scientists and writers—a rare case in which a wasteful and probably destructive expenditure was stopped before most of the money had been buried in it. The Anglo-French supersonic Concorde, which carried passengers from 1976 onward, was a marvel of engineering and at the same time a prestige project that never recouped the governments’ investment: only fourteen aircraft entered scheduled service, and few people could afford the tickets.

What do the RJR Nabisco deal, the House of the People, and *Buran* have in common? At first glance, nothing: private greed, personal megalomania, superpower rivalry. But they were structured in the same way. In all three cases, decisions about where to direct a vast share of the surplus were made by people who did not bear its full cost: bankers received their fees whatever the outcome of the deal; the dictator did not stand in bread lines; the military departments of both superpowers were scarcely accountable to those who paid. And in all three cases, the expenditure was poorly visible to those whose resources financed it: a depositor at a savings and loan did not know where his money had been invested, a Romanian family did not vote for the palace, and a Soviet taxpayer did not even know how much the military cost. Deadweight waste flourishes where expenditure is hidden and the benefits go to a narrow circle—and for all its celebration of markets and competition, the era of the turning point provided many new examples.


## Lesson of the Era

Let us return to Xiaogang. The eighteen peasants who put their fingerprints beneath a secret agreement had read neither Hayek nor Friedman. They were solving a simple problem: how to ensure that the person who grew an extra sack of grain would receive it. Fourteen years later, the government in Moscow appeared to be solving the same problem—to put the economy into the hands of people who would have a stake in it—and used almost the same words. The results were opposite. The central lesson of the era of the turning point lies in this contrast. Let us condense what we have seen into five propositions.

**First: the same rules bear different fruit depending on what supports them.** “Market,” “private property,” and “free prices” are not magic words but tools, and they work only together with what makes them fair: courts that settle disputes according to law rather than connections; a state able to collect taxes and enforce a contract; transparent rules for transferring property. In China, market incentives were introduced on top of a functioning, if harsh, administration, and the surplus flowed into factories, roads, and ports. In Russia, privatization outran the law, and the surplus went to those closest to power at the moment of division. Poland, the Czech Republic, and Hungary also paid dearly for the transition, but they had elections, a free press, and the prospect of common European rules—and they suffered no collapse comparable to Russia’s. Defenders of rapid reform object, and their argument cannot be dismissed: in 1992, the Russian government had neither foreign-exchange reserves, time, nor an obedient administrative apparatus, and a slow path might have ended in hyperinflation and empty shelves. The debate over whether Russia had a better option in those years remains unresolved. But the conclusion that institutions must be built before assets are distributed, not afterward, is now shared even by many of those who carried out the reforms at the time.

**Second: the most valuable quality of a system is the ability to make small mistakes.** China’s reforms proceeded through trials: one village, one province, one special zone. What succeeded was extended; what failed was quietly wound down. “Shock therapy,” by contrast, was an experiment conducted on an entire country at once, with no control group and no possibility of reversal. The same principle appears in very different parts of this chapter. The Montreal Protocol worked because it introduced bans gradually and verified their results. The northern-river diversion project could be stopped while it was still a project; the Aral Sea could not be saved because the water had begun to be diverted long before anyone calculated the consequences. The larger and more irreversible the decision, the higher the cost of omitting a trial step. This lesson reaches back to Chapter 13: during the Great Leap Forward, the suppression of bad news intensified the famine; in the Soviet Union, the famine of 1932–1933 arose amid poor harvests, while procurements, inadequate relief, and the disruptions of the transition to collective farms made the disaster worse. These different events cannot be reduced to a single cause.

**Third: the cost of a turning point falls on those with the least voice.** Defeating inflation is a development-enhancing achievement: predictable money lowers the costs of trust and makes long-term planning and investment possible. But the price was paid by Yorkshire mining towns, the steel cities of America’s Rust Belt, African teachers and nurses, and Russian pensioners whose savings vanished. The gains—from betting against a falling currency, from loans-for-shares auctions, from successful takeovers—went to a few. In the section on destruction, we called this a broken feedback loop: one group makes the decision, another bears the consequences. It explains Bhopal and the Aral Sea, savings and loan associations and the House of the People. The era showed that this rupture belongs to no single system: it arose under planning and markets, dictatorship and democracy. It was weakest where those who would be harmed could see the decision in advance and influence it—through elections, a court, a trade union, or a free press.

**Fourth: the sector that distributes the surplus can grow at the expense of the sector that produces it.** Finance is necessary: without credit, insurance, and exchanges, the modern economy would not function for a day, and in Chapters 8 and 9 we saw how strongly Dutch and English financial innovations fostered development. But during these decades, much of the growth of intermediation became growth in the trading of rights to income that already existed. Thomas Philippon’s calculations show that the service of “moving a dollar from saver to borrower” did not become cheaper over a hundred years, even as finance’s share of the economy nearly tripled. Alongside this stands a disputed but important fact: in the United States from the late 1970s onward, the compensation of ordinary workers grew noticeably more slowly than productivity. The size of the gap depends on how it is calculated: economists at the Economic Policy Institute consider it enormous, while Martin Feldstein, who included benefits and adjusted for rising prices differently, found it much smaller. Under some methods of calculation, the share of the gains received by a typical US worker was smaller than before. This does not allow us to estimate what share of the entire surplus went to the financial sector or to executives.

**Fifth: the era’s most rewarding investments were the cheapest and most open.** Eradicating smallpox cost several hundred million dollars. Internet protocols and the World Wide Web were given to the world free of charge. A solution of salt and sugar costs pennies. Compare these with *Buran*, which made one flight; “Star Wars,” which produced no shield; and thousands of tanks cut up for scrap. Knowledge given to everyone and common rules whose observance anyone can verify produced more than any of the prestige programs. Notice, too, who created them: government laboratories, universities, international organizations, and volunteers—on a foundation from which private companies then grew. This is an argument neither for the state nor for the market. It is an argument that the most development-enhancing part of the surplus often goes into things that no one can wholly appropriate.

Now for the answer to the question running through this book. Where did the surplus of the era of the turning point go? The picture is more variegated than in any previous chapter. In China, and later in India and several Southeast Asian countries, investment in factories, roads, and energy increased, and the incomes of many people rose. The share of the total surplus represented by these uses has not been measured here. It was here that the largest reduction of extreme poverty in history began. In the rich countries of the West, finance’s share of the economy and the incomes of those at the top increased in a number of cases, while productivity growth slowed after 1973. This comparison does not show that money used in financial transactions was taken from productive investment: transaction turnover and changes in asset prices are not the same as expenditure of real resources. In the former Soviet Union, much of the surplus accumulated over generations in factories, science, and resource industries was not newly created but transferred into the hands of a few, while part of it burned away in the collapse and in the years of life that millions of people did not live. Oil revenues provide a vivid comparison. From the mid-1990s onward, Norway saved them in a common fund belonging to all citizens and future generations, while in Russia the resource rents of the 1990s went to a narrow circle of owners who acquired the oilfields in the loans-for-shares auctions. The same oil—and two different answers to the question of whose surplus it was. Military spending fell after the end of the Cold War; the shares of the other expenditures discussed here in the total surplus have not been established.

The limits of these conclusions must also be stated. China’s success was achieved under one-party rule, at the cost of strict controls on people’s movement, exhausting factory labor, and severe pollution of rivers and air; it cannot be treated as a model without qualification. Inequality within many countries increased, but for the first time in two centuries the gap between poor and rich countries as a whole began to narrow—largely because of China and India—and that too was an outcome of the era, not merely one of its failures. Finally, many of the troubles of the 1990s—from Russia’s collapse to Africa’s lost decades—grew out of debts, wars, and decisions inherited from earlier eras. Attributing them solely to the reforms of the turning point would be as one-sided as crediting those reforms with nothing but successes.

How many people escaped poverty, how incomes and life expectancy changed in China, Russia, and the rich countries, and how much finance’s share grew—all this appears in the section with the figures.

## The Numbers

The figures for the era of the turning point are more reliable than those in any previous chapter, but they are also more fiercely disputed. In the view of many researchers, China’s statistics of the 1980s and 1990s overstated growth, while Russia’s statistics of the 1990s, by contrast, failed to capture the shadow economy and barter. The “extreme poverty” line is a conventional threshold drawn by the World Bank in dollars adjusted for purchasing power; a different line would produce different numbers. Almost everything below is therefore an estimate, and the source is named in each case.

**Oil and prices.** According to Daniel Yergin’s calculations, in the three months from October 1973 to January 1974 the price of a barrel of Middle Eastern oil rose from about three dollars to just under twelve. After the Iranian Revolution it approached forty dollars in 1980. In 1986, when Saudi Arabia opened the taps, it fell below fifteen dollars and at times to around ten; as Yegor Gaidar showed, it was this collapse that deprived the Soviet Union of the foreign currency needed to buy grain. According to official statistics, inflation was around twenty-four percent in Britain in 1975 and about thirteen and a half percent in the United States in 1980. By the end of the 1990s, it remained within two or three percent in most rich countries. The price of victory appears in another series: unemployment in the United States exceeded ten percent in 1982, while in Britain in the mid-1980s more than three million people were officially unemployed.

**Growth slowed.** According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, labor productivity in the American nonfarm business sector grew by about 2.8 percent a year from 1948 to 1973, but by only about one and a half percent from 1973 to 1995. The difference looks small, but at the first rate output per hour doubles in a quarter century; at the second, it takes almost half a century. Western Europe and Japan experienced a similar slowdown: the “golden age,” with its annual growth of four to eight percent, never returned.

**Who received the gains.** According to calculations by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, the richest one percent of Americans received around eight percent of income, excluding capital gains, during the 1970s; by 2000 the share had risen to roughly sixteen or seventeen percent, returning to the level of the 1920s. According to the Economic Policy Institute, the chief executive of a large American company earned about twenty times as much as a typical worker in 1965 and several hundred times as much in 2000; the precise multiple depends on how stock options are counted. According to calculations by Greenwood and Scharfstein, finance’s share of the American economy rose from less than three percent in 1950 to five percent in 1980 and more than eight percent by the middle of the 2000s. The Bank for International Settlements estimated foreign-exchange turnover in 1998 at around $1.5 trillion a day: in less than a week, more money passed through the currency market than was paid for a full year’s worth of world trade in goods.

**The Great Divergence in reverse.** China’s officially reported economic growth from 1978 to 2000 was around nine to ten percent a year. Even skeptics who recalculated the data arrived at more than seven percent—an unprecedented rate for a country of that size. In Angus Maddison’s tables, income per person in China rose from roughly one thousand “1990 international dollars” in 1978 to more than three thousand by 2000. According to World Bank estimates, the share of Chinese people living below the extreme-poverty line fell from almost nine tenths in the early 1980s to roughly one third in the early 2000s, while the share of all humanity living below the World Bank’s threshold of $1.90 a day fell from about forty-three percent in 1981 to twenty-eight percent in 2000. In absolute numbers, this meant hundreds of millions of people freed from hunger for the first time in their families’ history. After the reforms of 1991, India grew by around six percent a year. Alongside this stands the opposite example: according to Maddison, income per person in sub-Saharan Africa was lower in 2000 than in 1980. Latin America’s external debt exceeded $300 billion by 1982, according to World Bank estimates, while the “lost decade” meant that by the end of the 1980s income per person in the region was lower than at the beginning.

**The price of transition.** According to Goskomstat, consumer prices in Russia rose roughly twenty-sixfold in 1992, while by 1998 output had fallen about forty percent from its 1990 level—a deeper decline than the United States suffered during the Great Depression. According to Rosstat and the Human Mortality Database, Russian male life expectancy fell from around sixty-five years in 1987 to fifty-seven and a half in 1994. Russia’s population, having reached almost 149 million in the early 1990s, had fallen to around 146 million by 2000. By comparison, Poland, which also underwent shock therapy, had, according to IMF and EBRD estimates, returned to its pre-reform output level by the middle of the 1990s and exceeded it by a quarter to a third by 2000.

**Life overall.** According to UN estimates, the world’s population grew from almost four billion in 1973 to more than six billion in 2000, while average life expectancy rose from roughly fifty-eight to sixty years in 1973 to sixty-six and a half in 2000. According to the UN Inter-agency Group, mortality among children under five was around ninety-three per thousand live births in 1990 and about seventy-six in 2000. Every percentage point here represents hundreds of thousands of children a year. But according to UNAIDS, by the end of the century AIDS had cut ten years or more from average life expectancy in the countries of southern Africa.

**Weapons.** According to estimates by the Federation of American Scientists, the number of nuclear warheads in the world peaked at around seventy thousand in the middle of the 1980s and had fallen by roughly half by the turn of the century. According to official historical tables, US military spending rose to around six percent of gross domestic product in the mid-1980s and fell to about three percent by 2000. The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimates that world military spending fell by roughly a third from the late 1980s to the mid-1990s. This reduction in military expenditure is often called the “peace dividend.” It cannot be used directly to infer the share of deadweight waste in the total global surplus: some defense spending may have served a deterrent function.

The chapter’s conclusion in numbers is this: inflation was defeated, but growth in the rich countries was half as fast as during the “golden age,” and its fruits flowed increasingly toward the top; finance’s share of the US economy grew, though that alone tells us nothing about its net benefit or harm. Hundreds of millions of people in China, India, and Southeast Asia escaped destitution—the largest development-enhancing shift in the entire history covered by this book. Russia experienced a severe contraction and rising mortality; several countries in sub-Saharan Africa also suffered decline, though their trajectories differed. For the first time, the arms race went into reverse. Where the surplus it freed would go—to new technologies, to supply chains encircling the world, or to new bubbles—is the subject of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; an error by a factor of one and a half to two is possible. The unit of measure is one hour of work by a typical worker in the country and period concerned; a working life is seventy thousand hours; for reference, a sack of wheat weighs fifty kilograms. For comparison, I chose two expenditures that the era regarded as opposites: one produced by the market, the other by planning.

### US Savings and Loan Associations, 1986–1995

**Input data.** According to calculations by Timothy Curry and Lynn Shibut of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (2000), the cleanup cost about $153 billion, of which roughly $124 billion fell on taxpayers; the remainder was borne by the institutions themselves through insurance premiums. Around 1990, an hour of work by a typical American worker cost ten to twelve dollars (Bureau of Labor Statistics, rounded).

**Hours and lives.** The taxpayers’ share alone amounts to about eleven billion hours, or roughly 170,000 working lives. A fair range using the full cost is 150,000 to 220,000. For reference, at the wheat price of the time—around seven dollars per sack—the cost was equivalent to seventeen to nineteen billion sacks, or roughly one and a half global wheat harvests.

**Alternative.** According to the National Human Genome Research Institute, the Human Genome Project cost the US budget about $3 billion. The money spent on empty Texas offices could have funded forty such projects—or, by a rough calculation, around fifteen years of work by all the US National Institutes of Health (requires verification).

### Energia–Buran, 1976–1993

**Input data.** Estimates in the literature put the cost of the Energia–Buran program at around fourteen to twenty billion rubles, but their composition and comparability have not been verified here. According to the statistical yearbook *The National Economy of the USSR*, the average monthly wage of workers and employees in 1985 was about 190 rubles.

**Hours and lives.** Converting the program’s unverified cost into hours using the average wage would create an illusion of precision. Moreover, wages do not reflect the full cost of materials, equipment, and related work. I therefore give no figure here for “working lives” or sacks of grain.

**Alternative.** The program made one uncrewed flight. Comparing its cost with the number of apartments requires verified estimates for both the program and housing construction; without them, it is impossible to say how much housing could have been built instead.

**Conclusion.** The US savings-and-loan crisis and the Energia–Buran program belonged to different systems and different types of expenditure. The common question is how an expensive project can be tested in advance and who bears the cost of error. The figures given here do not establish that their costs were numerically equal.
