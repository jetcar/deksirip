# Chapter 12. Catastrophe, 1914–1945

## Portrait of the Era

London, summer 1914. Five years later, John Maynard Keynes would describe this morning in *The Economic Consequences of the Peace* (1919). The passage became famous because the world that had ended before the author’s eyes still stood intact within it. A Londoner, Keynes wrote, could lie in bed sipping his morning tea, order any goods from anywhere in the world by telephone, and expect them to be delivered to his door. He could invest his savings in natural resources and new enterprises in any corner of the earth. If he wished, he could board a train to any port and travel to any country without a passport and with almost no formalities. And, Keynes added, the most remarkable thing was that he regarded this state of affairs as normal, permanent, and capable only of improvement. This was the very world we left at the end of the previous chapter: telegraphs, steamships, the gold standard, fifty-five million migrants, and a single price for wheat in Chicago and Liverpool.

On June 28, 1914, in Sarajevo, the nineteen-year-old Bosnian Serb Gavrilo Princip shoots Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne. Within five weeks, a chain of ultimatums and mobilizations draws every great power in Europe into war. In August, reservists leave for the front with flowers in their rifle barrels; the common belief is that it will all be over by Christmas. Bankers and economists think so too—and they have an argument: modern states simply cannot pay for a war on this scale for long; the money will run out within a few months. That argument will prove wrong in the most terrible way. The money does not run out. States learn to print it, borrow from their own citizens and from future generations, and mobilize factories, grain, and people on a scale no pharaoh could have imagined.

Northeastern France, 1916. At Verdun, from February to December, the German and French armies grind each other down across a few dozen square kilometers; their combined dead and wounded number about seven hundred thousand by commonly cited estimates. On July 1, at the Somme, the British Army loses around fifty-seven thousand men in a single day, almost twenty thousand of them killed—the worst day in its history. For months, the front advances no more than a few kilometers. Everything created by the Second Industrial Revolution is at work here: steel becomes artillery and barbed wire; Haber–Bosch chemistry becomes explosives and poison gas; railways bring as many as a hundred trainloads a day to the front; assembly lines turn out shells by the million. For the first time, the industrial surplus of an entire century is directed not toward construction, but toward destruction—and proves capable of destroying with the same productivity it once brought to building.

Berlin, autumn 1923. The war ended five years ago, but prices in Germany now rise not by the month, but by the hour. Workers are paid twice a day, and their wives wait at the factory gates so they can run straight to the shops before the money loses its value by evening. A loaf of bread costs hundreds of billions of marks; in November, one dollar buys around 4.2 trillion marks. Banknotes are carried in laundry baskets and wheelbarrows, children build houses out of bundles of money, and grandmothers who spent their lives saving for old age in bonds and savings accounts discover that a lifetime’s savings will not buy a tram ticket. It is not only capital that is destroyed, but trust in the very idea that honest work and thrift have meaning. Ten years later, the embittered and impoverished middle class will become one of the pillars of Hitler’s party.

New York, October 1929. After a decade-long boom in which office clerks and shoeshine boys bought shares on credit, Wall Street meets “Black Thursday” on October 24 and “Black Tuesday” on October 29. This is only the beginning: by the summer of 1932, the Dow Jones Industrial Average will have fallen almost nine-tenths from its 1929 peak. In the four years from 1930 through 1933, about nine thousand American banks will close, taking depositors’ savings with them. In 1933, roughly one American in four will be out of work; in Germany, about six million people.

And now for the strangest scene in this chapter—strange because it contains neither war nor crop failure. In 1932–1933, people queue for free soup in Chicago and New York, while Midwestern farmers burn corn in their stoves because it is cheaper than coal. In Brazil, millions of sacks of coffee are burned or dumped into the sea to support the price. In the summer of 1933, seeking to raise farmers’ incomes, the American government buys and slaughters about six million piglets. People go hungry beside food being destroyed; factories stand idle even though the machinery is intact, workers know how to work, and they need what they could produce. The surplus exists—in physical goods and in skills—but the mechanism meant to connect it with human needs has broken down. It is this scene that will force Keynes to reconsider what the economists of his generation believed.

Finally, 1945. Europe from the English Channel to the Volga lies in ruins; total deaths in the Second World War are estimated at seventy to eighty-five million, more than half of them civilians. Auschwitz, the siege of Leningrad, Nanjing, the bombing of Hamburg, Dresden, and Tokyo, and finally Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August. Yet in those same years, under the same pressure, things are born that will define the next half-century of peaceful life: the mass production of penicillin, radar, the jet engine, the first electronic computers, and atomic energy.

The thirty-one years from Sarajevo to Hiroshima give this book its cleanest experiment. Previous chapters asked where the era’s surplus went, and the answer was usually mixed: some into canals and schools, some into palaces and wars. Here, the proportions are reversed to the limit. Two world wars and the Great Depression showed that an industrial economy could produce more than ever before and direct what it produced toward destruction—or produce nothing at all while people and machines sat idle beside one another. This chapter is about how that happened, what it cost, and why the ashes of 1945 gave rise not only to ruins, but also to the institutions that would support the fastest growth in history.

## Energy and Technology

In technological terms, 1914 did not begin a new era. The war was fought with things already invented: Bessemer and Siemens steel, smokeless powder, the Maxim gun, the railway, the telegraph, and the internal-combustion engine. What was new was the scale on which the industrial machine was for the first time switched entirely to a single task. Earlier wars had been limited by how much grain, how many horses, and how much gunpowder the treasury could gather. The constraint now became the capacity of entire nations’ factories, mines, and chemical works.

The shell shortage was the first sign. General staffs had prepared for a short war of maneuver and stockpiled what proved to be only a few weeks’ supply. In the spring of 1915, Britain was shaken by the “shell crisis”: newspapers accused the government of leaving soldiers at the front with nothing to fire. The answer was a new Ministry of Munitions under David Lloyd George, which took control of thousands of private enterprises, standardized production, and brought hundreds of thousands of women into the factories. By 1916, the numbers ran into the millions: in the week before the Somme offensive, British artillery fired more than a million and a half shells at the German trenches. A substantial share, however, failed to explode or cut the wire—mass production had outstripped quality control.

The war’s most revealing story was chemical. Explosives require nitrogen in the form of nitrates, and before 1914 their principal source was Chilean saltpeter carried across the ocean. The British fleet cut Germany off from this raw material in the first months of the war. By prewar logic, the German army should have run out of gunpowder by 1915. It was saved by the Haber–Bosch process discussed in the previous chapter: BASF’s Oppau plant, built in 1913 to manufacture fertilizer, was converted to make feedstock for explosives, and the giant Leuna works was completed in 1917. The same reaction that fixes atmospheric nitrogen would later feed billions of people, but then prolonged the war by several years. Fritz Haber himself directed another project: on April 22, 1915, near Ypres, German troops released a cloud of chlorine from cylinders toward French positions. It was the first large-scale gas attack. During the war, both sides used chlorine, phosgene, and mustard gas; widely accepted estimates put the number killed by gas at about ninety thousand, with many more maimed. In 1919, Haber received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the synthesis of ammonia—a decision already considered controversial by his contemporaries.

The war also accelerated the transition from horse and coal to engine and oil. In September 1914, during the Battle of the Marne, the French command sent several hundred Paris taxis to the front with reinforcements—an episode more symbolic than decisive, but an apt symbol. In September 1916, British tanks went into battle for the first time at the Somme. The airplane, used almost solely for reconnaissance in 1914, had become both fighter and bomber by 1918, and the belligerents were producing them by the tens of thousands. Even before the war, from 1911 to 1914, Winston Churchill, as First Lord of the Admiralty, converted the British fleet from coal to oil, while the government bought a controlling stake in the Anglo-Persian Oil Company. After victory, the British minister Lord Curzon said that the Allies had floated to it “on a wave of oil.” Oil was transformed from fuel for lamps and automobiles into a strategic resource over which states would struggle for the rest of the century.

Between the wars, the same technologies returned to civilian life—an important counterweight to the era’s grim picture. American cities were almost entirely electrified during the 1920s; washing machines and vacuum cleaners began entering homes, followed by refrigerators in the 1930s. Pittsburgh radio station KDKA began regular broadcasting in 1920, and a decade later, according to the 1930 census, roughly two American homes in five had a radio; by the end of the 1930s, most did. About fifteen million Ford Model Ts had been produced by 1927. In *A Great Leap Forward* (2011), economic historian Alexander Field reaches a surprising conclusion. He measures what is called total factor productivity—the part of growth in output that cannot be explained simply by adding labor and machines and is generally associated with new knowledge and better organization. By that measure, the 1930s, the decade of the Great Depression, were the most technologically productive of the twentieth century for the United States. Factories stood idle and millions of people had no work, yet laboratories and engineering offices continued to operate; roads, dams, and electrical grids were built; and when demand returned, the economy could produce far more than before. Field’s estimates are disputed, but the idea itself matters: a surplus of knowledge can accumulate even when a surplus of goods finds no use.

The technology of autarky—the policy of self-sufficiency, in which a country tries not to depend on imports—looked very different. Nazi Germany prepared for war with little oil of its own and almost no natural rubber. The answer was synthetic substitutes: gasoline from coal using the Bergius and Fischer–Tropsch processes, and the Buna synthetic rubber made by IG Farben. This was genuine engineering, but economically it was an expensive detour: synthetic gasoline cost several times as much as imported fuel. During the war, such plants supplied most German aviation fuel by most estimates—and consequently became the principal target of Allied air power in 1944. Near Auschwitz, IG Farben built a Buna plant at Monowitz with the labor of camp prisoners, many of whom died during construction. Here, the technological surplus did not merely go to war; it was fused with slave labor.

The Second World War carried industrial mobilization to its limit. From 1940 through 1945, the United States built about three hundred thousand military aircraft by generally accepted counts. Liberty ships were assembled from standardized sections welded in advance; about 2,700 were built, and the record-setting *Robert E. Peary* was launched in November 1942 less than five days after its keel was laid. By 1944, Ford’s Willow Run plant was turning out roughly one B-24 heavy bomber every hour. In the summer and autumn of 1941, the Soviet Union evacuated about fifteen hundred large enterprises to the Urals, the Volga region, and Siberia according to official figures—dismantling them, loading them onto trains, and sometimes restarting them in the open air—and by 1942 it was producing several times more tanks than Germany. Yet the German army, symbol of the motorized blitzkrieg, moved chiefly on foot and by horse: more than six hundred thousand horses entered the Soviet Union in June 1941, according to widely cited estimates. Even in war, the engine had not yet displaced the horse.

And finally, there was science transformed into a weapon and at the same time into a foundation for the peaceful future. In Birmingham in 1940, John Randall and Harry Boot created the cavity magnetron that made compact radar possible; that autumn, the British Tizard Mission brought it to the United States, where a major research center grew around it. Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming as early as 1928, remained a laboratory curiosity for a decade until Howard Florey and Ernst Chain’s team at Oxford proved its effectiveness and American pharmaceutical companies learned to cultivate mold industrially in enormous vats. By the Normandy landings in June 1944, the drug was being produced in millions of doses. The Heinkel He 178 jet aircraft first flew in August 1939, while Frank Whittle was developing his own engine in Britain. Wernher von Braun’s V-2 was the first machine to reach space; it was assembled by prisoners in the underground Mittelwerk factory, and historians estimate that more people died producing it than were killed by its strikes. Colossus, an electronic machine for breaking German ciphers, began operating at Bletchley Park in 1944; in the United States, ENIAC, built to calculate ballistic tables, was completed in 1945. At its peak, the Manhattan Project employed more than a hundred thousand people and cost about two billion dollars at the time; its results were Hiroshima and Nagasaki—and the atomic energy of the decades that followed.

Here the book comes to one of its hardest questions. If the war gave the world antibiotics, radar, jet aircraft, rockets, computers, and the atom, was it, despite everything, “developmental”? The honest answer is mixed and requires three qualifications. First, almost everything listed above rested on prewar science—Fleming, Whittle, the discovery of nuclear fission in 1938–1939. The war did not create this knowledge; it supplied money and extreme urgency. Second, no one knows how many discoveries never happened because scientists were killed, emigrated, or worked on explosives instead of fertilizers. Field himself shows that for the American civilian economy, the war years were more an interruption in productivity growth than a leap forward. Third, there is the price. Even if every one of these inventions were a pure gain, it would have to be weighed against tens of millions of lives and the ruined cities of half the world.

The answer to the book’s recurring question is therefore sharper in this chapter than anywhere else. The industrial age’s energy and technological surplus was immense—greater than that of any society before it. The greater part was deployed as a weapon. That penicillin and the computer emerged from this meat grinder does not demonstrate the benefits of war; it demonstrates the magnitude of the surplus: even the by-products of its destruction proved capable of changing the world.

## Organization of Labor

If the previous chapter told how the worker became an appendage to the assembly line, this one tells how the state became employer, dispatcher, and overseer all at once. Over thirty years, labor in the industrial countries passed through three entirely different regimes: total mobilization, mass idleness, and mobilization again, this time more complete still. Each regime answers the question of where the surplus goes in its own way, because the greatest surplus in any economy consists not of grain or coal, but of people’s working hours.

Let us begin with the army, because it was the first to take those hours. During the First World War, the belligerent countries put some sixty to seventy million people under arms by generally accepted counts. France and Germany mobilized a substantial share of all men of military age. They were not only soldiers, but miners, machinists, peasants, and engineers—the very people needed behind the lines if the army was to keep firing. By the end of 1914, it had already become clear that some skilled workers in armaments factories would have to be recalled from the front. For the first time, war confronted the state with a question the market had once decided: where were millions of people needed most—in the trench, at the machine, or in the field?

The answers varied. In Germany, the Auxiliary Service Law was adopted in December 1916 as part of the Hindenburg Program to increase war production sharply: all men between seventeen and sixty who had not been called into the army could be assigned to work in the armaments industry. In return—and this detail matters—trade unions received official recognition for the first time and seats on commissions that adjudicated workplace disputes. Britain relied at first on volunteers, but introduced universal conscription in 1916, while the state used a system of “reserved occupations” to decide who entered the army and who remained in the factory. Everywhere, the working day lengthened, strikes were restricted or forbidden, and wages were partly regulated from above.

The great change was the arrival of women. By the end of the war, some nine hundred thousand to one million women were working in British munitions factories by widely cited estimates. They were called “munitionettes.” They filled shells with explosives, and trinitrotoluene turned the skin of many yellow, earning them the nickname “canaries”; hundreds died from poisoning and explosions. Women drove trams and worked in post offices, offices, and on farms. The same happened in France and Germany. The significance of this shift is disputed. After 1918, returning soldiers displaced most women from the factories, and many historians emphasize that the war did not bring a direct revolution in the labor market. But in 1918, British women over thirty, subject to a property qualification, gained the vote; German women did so in November of the same year and voted for the first time in January 1919. Contemporaries linked both changes to women’s wartime labor. For at least a few years, it had become clear that half the adult population, whose labor the economy had used chiefly in the home, could produce exactly what men did in a factory.

Then peace came—and labor began to stand idle. By 1921, British veterans faced unemployment above ten percent among insured workers, and by that measure it remained in double digits for almost the entire interwar period; recalculations by historians put the rate for the whole labor force lower in the 1920s, at around seven to eight percent. The Great Depression turned a chronic illness into an epidemic. In the United States in 1933, about one-quarter of the labor force—roughly twelve to thirteen million people—was unemployed by the standard estimate; in Germany, more than six million unemployed were officially registered at the beginning of 1932. This does not mean that everyone else lived securely: millions worked shortened weeks for reduced pay, with no confidence in tomorrow.

Here it is worth pausing over an idea that will run through the entire chapter. In this book’s framework, “wasteful spending” means resources consumed without creating anything. Unemployment is a special and perhaps the cruelest kind: here, the resource is not even consumed; it simply disappears. An hour of labor that never happened cannot be stored for later like grain in a barn. Twelve million Americans sitting without work for several years represent tens of billions of human-hours in which houses could have been built, children taught, and roads repaired. Sociologists Marie Jahoda, Paul Lazarsfeld, and Hans Zeisel described another cost after studying the Austrian village of Marienthal in 1931–1932, where the only factory had closed. The unemployed did not use their free time to learn or make things. Men walked more slowly in the street, stopped wearing watches, and borrowed fewer books from the library, even though membership was free. The researchers called Marienthal a “tired community.” A surplus of free time without purpose or hope proved not to be leisure, but disintegration.

The response was a new kind of public works. Beginning in 1933, Franklin Roosevelt’s administration launched a range of programs under the New Deal. During its nine years, the Civilian Conservation Corps enrolled about three million young men in its camps according to official figures: they planted forests, laid trails in national parks, built flood-control structures, and sent most of their earnings home to their families. The Works Progress Administration (WPA), established in 1935, gave jobs to roughly eight and a half million people over eight years. Its workers built or repaired hundreds of thousands of kilometers of roads, tens of thousands of bridges, and thousands of schools and hospitals; under the same program, artists painted murals in post offices and writers compiled state guidebooks. Critics then and now have argued that some of this work was invented merely to justify paying people and that the WPA was less efficient than private contractors. That is a fair criticism, and we shall return to it in the section on wasteful spending. But compared with the alternative—idleness that destroyed both skills and human beings—even inefficient work left behind bridges, schools, and people who had not forgotten how to work.

In Germany, the solution looked similar on the surface but was fundamentally different. After 1933, the Nazi regime also built roads—the famous autobahns—and enrolled young people in the Reich Labor Service, where they spent six months draining marshes and digging ditches for token pay. Unemployment fell quickly, becoming one of Hitler’s chief propaganda assets. But economic historian Adam Tooze shows in *The Wages of Destruction* (2006) that the true engine of employment was not autobahn construction, but rearmament: by the late 1930s, a substantial share of industry was working for the military. At the same time, the regime destroyed independent trade unions—on May 2, 1933, storm troopers occupied their offices and workers were forced into the party-controlled German Labor Front. Real wages remained nearly frozen, and the right to change jobs was gradually restricted. Germany returned people to the factories, but not to produce what they themselves needed: it returned them to prepare for war.

The Second World War carried labor mobilization to its limit, and the differences among countries became stark. In the United States, the number of employed women rose by about six million; “Rosie the Riveter” became the era’s poster icon. Unemployment, which the New Deal had failed to defeat, fell below two percent by 1944. Beginning in late 1941, Britain introduced labor and military conscription for unmarried women. In the Soviet Union, women, adolescents, and the elderly replaced men who had gone to the front in evacuated factories and collective-farm fields; the working day stretched to eleven or twelve hours, vacations were abolished, and from December 1941 unauthorized departure from a war plant was punished as desertion. Many of these people lived half-starved in barracks and dugouts.

Nazi Germany, by contrast, long hesitated to send German women into factories on a mass scale, since this contradicted its own ideology about women’s place in the home. Instead, it took a path that threw the economy back thousands of years—to slave labor. According to historian Ulrich Herbert’s figures, also used by Tooze, about seven and a half million foreign civilian workers and prisoners of war were working in the Reich by the summer of 1944—roughly one worker in four. Most of them—Poles and *Ostarbeiter* from Ukraine, Belarus, and Russia—had been deported by force, wore badges, lived behind barbed wire, and received rations calculated to keep them from dying immediately. Below even them stood concentration-camp prisoners rented out to industrial corporations; for many, labor itself was a means of killing—the phrase “extermination through labor” appears in documents of the period. Japan created a similar system, forcibly sending hundreds of thousands of Koreans and Chinese to mines and construction sites.

Thus, over three decades, the organization of labor came full circle. The industrial economy of the nineteenth century had rested on free hiring: a worker, however poor and powerless, could leave his employer. The age of catastrophe showed that this principle was less secure than it had seemed. The state learned to direct millions of people wherever it wished—to a trench, a factory, or a camp. It could also abandon them to the market when the market stopped hiring them. Only in one episode of the era—in public works, for all their shortcomings—did the state try to direct idle labor toward something that would remain for people afterward. This is the short answer to the chapter’s question as it applies to labor: human-hours can be sent into development, destruction, or emptiness, and Europe and America experienced all three at once between 1914 and 1945.

## Exchange and Money

At the end of the previous chapter, money seemed the most dependable thing in the world. The pound sterling, mark, franc, and dollar were merely different names for fixed weights of gold; a banknote could be exchanged for metal at any bank, and exchange rates remained unchanged for decades. This system—the gold standard—imposed discipline: a state could not print more money than its gold reserves allowed. Thirty years of catastrophe left nothing of that discipline, and the history of money from 1914 to 1945 is the story of how exchange built on trust breaks down when trust is spent on war.

War first abolished convertibility. In August 1914, Germany, France, Russia, and Austria-Hungary stopped exchanging banknotes for gold; Britain formally preserved convertibility but in practice made it nearly impossible. The reason was simple: war cost more than any taxes could cover. Governments followed three paths at once. They borrowed from their own citizens through war bonds sold by posters and in schools and churches; in the United States, they were called Liberty Bonds. They borrowed from one another—first and foremost from Britain, and later from America, which in four years was transformed from a major debtor into the world’s principal creditor. And they printed money. The proportions differed: historians’ calculations—for example, those of Niall Ferguson in *The Pity of War* (1998)—suggest that Britain covered about a quarter of its wartime expenditure through taxes, while Germany covered considerably less, expecting the defeated powers to pay its debts after victory. By 1918, prices had doubled, tripled, or risen still further in every belligerent country. This was quiet confiscation: a generation’s surplus of savings went into shells, and the bill would be paid by those holding bonds and cash.

Peace brought no reckoning; it merely rewrote the debts. The Treaty of Versailles of 1919 required Germany to compensate the victors for their losses; in 1921, the so-called London Schedule of Payments set the sum at 132 billion gold marks. Keynes, who had served on the British delegation to the peace conference, resigned and wrote *The Economic Consequences of the Peace*, arguing that the reparations were impossible to pay and would ruin Europe together with Germany. The book was enormously successful and shaped perceptions of Versailles for decades. But honesty requires us to say that many modern historians, Sally Marks among them, dispute Keynes’s case. They point out that much of the headline sum was intended for public consumption and was never expected to be paid, while the amount Germany was actually required to transfer represented a manageable share of its income. In their view, Germany could pay, but did not want to. The debate remains unresolved, and it matters: the answer determines whether the catastrophe that followed was inevitable or chosen.

In any event, the money began moving in a strange circle. Germany had to pay France and Britain; France and Britain had to repay their wartime debts to America. America refused to forgive its allies’ debts, but after the Dawes Plan was adopted in 1924, American bankers readily lent to Germany. American dollars thus arrived in Berlin, traveled as reparations to Paris and London, and then returned to New York as payment on war debts. As long as the flow of credit continued, the wheel turned. The circuit created no new wealth: it merely moved obligations born of the war from one pocket to another—financial busywork in its purest form.

German hyperinflation, whose scene we witnessed at the beginning of the chapter, grew from the same soil. Germany emerged from the war with an enormous domestic debt, and the government of the young Weimar Republic, weak and surrounded by enemies on both left and right, dared impose neither heavy taxes nor strict austerity—either could provoke revolt. It continued covering the deficit with the Reichsbank’s printing press. When French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr, the country’s principal coal region, in January 1923 in response to delayed reparation deliveries, the government called on workers to offer “passive resistance” and paid the strikers with printed money. Inflation became an avalanche. It is important to understand who benefited. Debtors—including major industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes, who bought companies on credit—repaid their loans in devalued marks. The state itself shed its domestic war debt. The losers were those who had saved: bondholders, depositors, pensioners, and people on fixed salaries. Hyperinflation became an immense redistribution from the thrifty to debtors—and from trust to cynicism.

It was stopped with surprising speed. In November 1923, a temporary currency, the Rentenmark, was introduced, notionally backed by mortgages on land and industrial property, and exchanged for the old money at one to a trillion. The backing mattered less than the promise: issuance of the new money was tightly limited, and people believed. Prices stabilized within weeks. This episode would be repeated many times in history: inflation is cured not by a new piece of paper, but by restored trust that it will not be printed without limit. But the middle class’s savings could no longer be restored.

The second half of the 1920s was spent trying to restore the prewar world. Countries returned to gold one after another, and the chief symbol was Britain’s decision in 1925 to restore the pound at its prewar parity. Winston Churchill was Chancellor of the Exchequer; Keynes answered with the pamphlet *The Economic Consequences of Mr. Churchill*, arguing that an overvalued pound would make British coal and textiles too expensive abroad and would require wages to fall. That is what happened: in 1926, an attempt to cut miners’ pay led to the General Strike. The restored gold standard proved more fragile than the old one: gold was distributed unevenly, with most of it accumulated in the United States and France, while central banks no longer cooperated as they had before the war.

When the Great Depression struck, gold was transformed from anchor into fetters. Economist Barry Eichengreen gave his 1992 book that very title: *Golden Fetters*. The mechanism worked as follows: to preserve convertibility during an outflow of gold, a central bank had to raise interest rates and contract credit—that is, strangle an economy already in decline. In the United States, the money supply shrank by about one-third from 1929 to 1933. In *A Monetary History of the United States* (1963), Milton Friedman and Anna Schwartz argued that the Federal Reserve’s failure to act, allowing waves of bank failures to destroy depositors’ money, turned an ordinary downturn into the Great Depression. Ben Bernanke later added another link: failed banks took their knowledge of borrowers with them, and credit did not return even to reliable farmers and manufacturers. Exchange, like blood, ceased to flow—not because goods did not exist, but because no one trusted that they would be paid.

Eichengreen and Jeffrey Sachs demonstrated a simple pattern: the earlier a country abandoned gold, the earlier it began to recover. Britain left in September 1931 and recovered relatively gently; the United States effectively abandoned convertibility under Roosevelt in 1933, and growth began that same year; France and the other countries of the “gold bloc” held out until 1935–1936 and remained depressed the longest. It was a bitter lesson for a generation raised to believe in gold: the rule that had created trust before the war had become its destroyer under new conditions.

Trade was collapsing at the same time. In June 1930, the United States Congress passed the Smoot–Hawley Tariff, raising duties on thousands of goods in an attempt to protect American farmers and manufacturers. Other countries answered with tariffs, quotas, and prohibitions of their own. Economists still dispute how much the tariff itself deepened the crisis: many believe that trade declined chiefly because incomes fell, not because of tariffs. But the direction was common to all: each country tried to save its economy at its neighbor’s expense, and all lost together. In *The World in Depression* (1973), Charles Kindleberger represented the process with a famous spiral: month by month, the value of world trade contracted toward the center, falling by about two-thirds between January 1929 and early 1933. Kindleberger saw the principal cause in the absence of a country willing to act as stabilizer: Britain could no longer do so, while the United States was not yet willing. The world market built over a century disintegrated into blocs—the sterling area, the dollar area, Japan’s “Co-Prosperity Sphere,” and Germany’s system of bilateral clearing.

Nazi Germany showed what money became in the hands of a state preparing for war. Reichsbank president Hjalmar Schacht devised the so-called Mefo bills—promissory notes issued by a shell company and used to pay for armaments without showing the expenditure in the budget. Currency controls prohibited money from leaving the country, while trade with Germany’s neighbors was shifted to clearing arrangements—effectively barter, in which German machinery was exchanged for Romanian oil or Hungarian grain without freely traded currency. Exchange became an instrument of policy, and money a means of concealing where the surplus was really going.

The Second World War carried this process to completion. Prices were frozen and goods rationed in every belligerent country; money ceased to be the sole passport to goods—one also needed a coupon. People accumulated savings they could not spend, while states borrowed those savings through war bonds. The American Lend-Lease program, adopted in March 1941, transferred about fifty billion contemporary dollars’ worth of weapons, equipment, food, and raw materials to the Allies—and deliberately avoided the trap of the previous war: no one wanted to recreate the knot of wartime debts from the 1920s. Finally, in July 1944, before the war had even ended, representatives of forty-four countries gathered in the resort town of Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to build a monetary order that would avoid the mistakes of the interwar years. Keynes was one of its principal architects. What came of it is the subject of Chapter Fourteen.

The conclusion for this book’s question is clear. Money and exchange are infrastructure of trust, just like roads and canals. From 1914 to 1945, that infrastructure was undermined three times over: by printing money for war, by a circulation of debts that created nothing but new obligations, and by clinging to rules that no longer worked. Each time, the surplus—savings, credit, trading relationships—failed to flow where it was needed and instead burned away. And each time, the majority paid, not those who made the decisions.


## Power and Institutions

In 1914, Europe was ruled by four old empires—the German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, and Ottoman. By 1922, not one remained. Emperors and sultans were gone, and in the space of a few years roughly a dozen new states arose in their place: Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, Finland, the Baltic republics, and Austria and Hungary as separate countries. The map of Central Europe came to resemble a patchwork quilt, with every patch acquiring its own customs service, currency, and tariffs. Where Vienna had traded with Prague and Budapest without borders inside a single empire before the war, barriers now stood. Economists of the day complained that factories had ended up in one country, raw materials in another, and customers in a third. The institutional collapse did not begin with the economy, but the economy paid for it at once.

The era’s principal institutional shift, however, occurred not on the map but within states. Before 1914, the typical European—and especially American—government was small by today’s standards: it maintained an army and navy, courts, police, a postal service, and sometimes railways and schools, spending on all of them, according to economists Vito Tanzi and Ludger Schuknecht, an average of roughly one-eighth of national income, and less than one-tenth in the United States. The war changed that forever. The state began to procure and allocate goods, set prices and wages, and decide who would work in a factory and who would go to the trenches. After 1918, spending receded, but not to its former level. In *Crisis and Leviathan* (1987), economic historian Robert Higgs called this the “ratchet effect”: in every crisis, state power rises sharply, then retreats only partway once the crisis is over, because new agencies, habits, and taxes remain—as do people who have learned how to use them. Higgs wrote of this with alarm; his opponents see the same process as an accumulation of useful tools. But almost everyone acknowledges the ratchet itself.

It is most readily seen in taxation. Before the war, income tax in most countries was the concern of a wealthy few. The United States introduced a federal income tax only in 1913, yet by 1918 the top rate had risen to seventy-seven percent. During the Second World War, the American government turned it into a mass tax: according to the Internal Revenue Service, the number of taxpayers grew from roughly four million in 1939 to more than forty million in 1945. In 1943, tax withholding from wages was introduced—the employer remitted the tax to the treasury before the worker received the money. This seemingly technical measure became one of the war’s most enduring legacies: it gave the state a steady, reliable stream of revenue that would later support both the armies of the Cold War and pensions and hospitals. The question decisive for this book—where that stream would go—remained open.

Now let us consider the paths taken by industrial countries after the upheavals of 1914–1933. All were responding to the same calamity: the market had ceased to connect people with work and the surplus with needs. But their answers were opposites.

The first path was dictatorship, subordinating the economy to war. Soviet Russia and its planned system will be discussed in detail in the next chapter; here it is enough to say that the Revolution of 1917 was born of war no less than of Marx’s ideas. Benito Mussolini ruled Italy from 1922. On paper, his “corporate state” united employers and workers in sectoral “corporations” under party supervision; in practice, it stripped workers of the right to strike and of independent trade unions. When Italy’s largest banks began to totter during the Depression, the regime created the Institute for Industrial Reconstruction (IRI) in 1933, taking over their holdings in industrial companies. By the end of the decade, according to widely cited estimates, the Italian state controlled roughly four-fifths of shipbuilding, three-quarters of pig-iron production, and almost half of steel production. In Germany, the Reichstag passed the Enabling Act in March 1933, allowing Hitler’s government to enact laws without parliament. Private property formally remained and industrial combines earned profits, but decisions about what to produce were increasingly made by state agencies—from 1936, above all by the Office of the Four Year Plan under Hermann Göring, charged with preparing the economy for war within four years. The courts ceased to protect those whom the regime declared enemies; Jewish property was seized under the guise of “Aryanization”—the forced transfer of enterprises to “Aryan” owners, often for next to nothing. The institution of property on which the entire industrial economy rested became a privilege of the loyal. For the framework of this book, what matters is that this was not only a crime against people but also the destruction of a capital of trust accumulated over centuries: if the right to property depends on ancestry and a party membership card, no one truly possesses it.

The second path was reform of the market economy while preserving democracy. Its foremost example was the American New Deal. During the first years of Roosevelt’s presidency, institutions emerged that in many cases still operate today. The Banking Act of 1933—often called the Glass–Steagall Act—separated ordinary banks that accepted deposits from investment banks that speculated on the stock market, and created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation: if a bank failed, the state would reimburse deposits up to a specified amount. The effect was striking. Previously, American banks had failed by the thousand, but after 1934 banking panics all but disappeared for a long time—depositors no longer had a reason to race to withdraw their money first. The Securities and Exchange Commission was created in 1934 and required companies to publish honest accounts. The Wagner Act of 1935 guaranteed workers the right to form unions and bargain collectively. That same year, the Social Security Act introduced federal old-age pensions and unemployment insurance. None of these measures was revolutionary on its own—Europe had done much of it earlier, beginning with Bismarck’s Germany in the 1880s. Together, however, they changed the compact among state, market, and citizen: the state assumed the role of insurer against disasters that individuals were powerless to withstand alone.

The Scandinavian countries followed a similar path, though with their own distinctive features. In Sweden, the Social Democrats, who came to power in 1932, began spending on public works and benefits. Then, in 1938, at the resort of Saltsjöbaden, the employers’ confederation and the trade-union federation signed an agreement governing wage negotiations without state intervention and without ruinous strikes. This was an institution that lowered the costs of conflict—one of the five criteria in our framework. The compact between capital and labor, concluded not at gunpoint but across a negotiating table, would underpin the “Swedish model” for half a century.

There was a third option as well: attempting to preserve the old rules at any cost. France and the other countries of the “gold bloc” clung to gold and fiscal austerity until the mid-1930s; the result was a protracted slump, political instability, and, in the view of many historians, a weakened country confronting the year 1940.

A new theory grew out of these practical responses. In 1936, Keynes published *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money*. Stripped of equations, its central idea was this. Classical economics regarded unemployment as temporary: if workers accepted lower wages, employers would hire them again, while falling interest rates would automatically turn savings into investment. Keynes objected that this does not happen in a deep slump. If everyone cuts spending at once—workers because they fear losing their jobs, firms because they see no customers, banks because they fear loans will not be repaid—total demand, the aggregate desire and ability to buy, falls, and the economy can remain stuck for years with factories and people standing idle. Lower wages merely reduce purchasing power. The one actor able to spend when everyone else is afraid can help the economy escape this trap: the state, by building, hiring, and paying benefits, even with borrowed money. Keynes remarked ironically that, in the extreme, the treasury could bury bottles filled with banknotes in abandoned mines and let private companies dig them up—even that would be better than doing nothing, though building houses would of course be wiser. This joke is often cited to justify any spending at all, although its meaning is the reverse: pointless work is better than complete idleness, but purposeful work is better than pointless work. For this book, the distinction is fundamental. Keynes explained how to return an idle surplus to circulation; he did not claim that every form of circulation was equally good.

Critics appeared at once. Friedrich Hayek, who had been debating Keynes since the early 1930s, warned in *The Road to Serfdom* (1944) that a state which began planning the economy for worthy ends would be compelled to extend its control ever further until it also subjugated political freedom; the examples of Germany and the Soviet Union stood before everyone’s eyes. In that same year, Karl Polanyi argued the opposite in *The Great Transformation*: it was the attempt to build a society wholly subject to a self-regulating market that had destroyed social ties and driven Europe toward fascism. Both pointed to a real danger, and their dispute, as we shall see, did not end in the twenty-first century. Yet by 1945, almost every Western government, including conservative ones, emerged from the war convinced that the state bore responsibility for employment and that the mass unemployment of the 1930s must never be allowed to return. In Britain, the debate began even under the bombs: William Beveridge’s 1942 report, which proposed a unified system of social insurance “from cradle to grave,” sold hundreds of thousands of copies.

Finally, there were international institutions. After 1918, the world tried to build them—and failed. The League of Nations, founded in 1920, had neither an army nor money, while the United States, whose president Woodrow Wilson had been its leading advocate, never joined: the Senate refused to ratify the peace treaty. Nor was there any body capable of restraining countries from currency wars and tariff spirals. As we have seen, Kindleberger regarded the absence of a stabilizer as the principal reason the Depression became so deep. The architects of the postwar world learned this lesson. While the war was still under way, Bretton Woods produced agreement to create the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank; in 1945, the Charter of the United Nations was signed in San Francisco. These were attempts to create what had been so sorely lacking between the wars: common rules and an arbiter.

The section’s conclusion for the question of this book is this. The age of catastrophe showed that institutions are not the scenery of an economy but its load-bearing structure. When empires, courts, and currencies collapsed, the surplus of whole peoples was consumed by war or lost to unemployment. Yet whenever workable rules could be built amid the crisis—deposit insurance, collective bargaining, pensions, an international monetary order—they reduced the cost of fear and mistrust for decades to come. At the same time, the very same instruments—taxes withheld from every paycheck, a powerful state capable of mobilizing the economy—served both Roosevelt and Hitler. What proved decisive was not the strength of the state, but who constrained it, how they did so, and where it directed the surplus it collected.

## What Fostered Development

Writing a section about development in a chapter on two world wars and the Great Depression feels almost awkward. But this book’s framework demands honesty in both directions: if we looked for slavery as well as aqueducts in the Roman era, then here, amid trenches and soup lines, we must find what continued to grow. Such things did exist—and, importantly, almost all of them grew not because of the catastrophes but in spite of them, in the narrow channels through which the surplus still managed to flow.

The first channel was knowledge, and not only military knowledge. The 1920s and 1930s were the period in which the industrial research laboratory, born in Germany and in Edison’s workshops in the previous chapter, became a standard part of a large corporation. The famous Bell Laboratories were established within the American telephone monopoly in 1925; there in the 1920s, physicist Walter Shewhart devised statistical quality control—the deceptively simple idea of using a chart to see whether a process had strayed beyond its normal range of variation, rather than inspecting every finished product. At the DuPont chemical company’s laboratory, Wallace Carothers’s team produced nylon in 1935, the first fully synthetic fiber to reach the mass market. The passenger-carrying Douglas DC-3 began flying in 1936 and made air transport profitable without postal subsidies for the first time. This was precisely what Alexander Field, discussed above, meant when he called the Depression-ridden 1930s the century’s most productive decade: knowledge and organizational techniques accumulated even while goods could find no buyers.

The second channel was medicine, in which the era perhaps delivered its purest gain for the majority of people. In Toronto in 1921–1922, Frederick Banting and Charles Best isolated insulin, turning type 1 diabetes from a death sentence into a disease one could live with. In 1932, at Bayer’s laboratory, then part of the IG Farben combine, Gerhard Domagk discovered that the red dye Prontosil cured streptococcal infection in mice. This gave rise to the sulfonamides—the first drugs to kill bacteria inside the body on a mass scale, before penicillin. In 1937, Max Theiler developed a vaccine against yellow fever. Add the chlorination of water, infant milk stations, and school hygiene, all of which spread during these same decades, and it becomes clear why life expectancy continued to rise between the wars in most Western countries, despite everything. It is an important reminder: a period average can conceal tens of millions killed and millions of children saved at the same time.

The third channel was education. During these years, the United States experienced what economic historians Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz called the “high school movement.” In *The Race between Education and Technology* (2008), they show that fewer than one in ten young Americans earned a high school diploma in 1910, but about half did so by 1940. Local communities built schools with their own tax revenues, often in small Midwestern towns, and even the Depression did not halt the expansion: for a teenager who had nowhere to work anyway, it was easier to remain at a desk. Goldin and Katz regard this as one of the chief reasons America became the world’s most productive country by the middle of the century: its workers could read blueprints, calculate, and learn new things. By the standards of our framework, this is a classic investment in development—slow, dull, invisible in the year of construction, and decisive a generation later.

The fourth channel was bringing what was already known to those who did not yet have it. In 1935, the United States created the Rural Electrification Administration. Until then, private companies had found it unprofitable to run wires to scattered farms and, according to the standard figures, only about one American farm in ten had electricity. The administration offered cheap loans to farmer-owned cooperatives, which built the lines themselves; by the early 1950s, almost every farm had electricity. For a farmer’s wife, this meant the end of washing by hand and carrying water in buckets; for the farmer, an electric pump and refrigeration for milk. The Tennessee Valley Authority (TVA), created in 1933, played a similar role: dams in one of the country’s poorest regions generated electricity, protected against floods, and made the river navigable. The TVA remains controversial—private utilities called it unfair state competition, while economists differ in their assessments of its long-term effects—but cheap energy in a poor region is, in substance, closer to Mesopotamia’s irrigation canals than to its pyramids.

A quiet transformation in agriculture belongs in the same category. During the 1930s, American farmers began switching en masse to hybrid corn—seed produced by crossing specially bred lines and yielding substantially larger harvests. In a well-known 1957 study, economist Zvi Griliches traced how rapidly it spread: in Iowa, its share rose from almost nothing at the beginning of the 1930s to an overwhelming majority of plantings by the end of the decade. A farmer suffering from low prices still bought the new seed because it paid for itself. This is a useful reminder that even an ailing market continued to perform its developmental work—spreading better methods.

The fifth channel was the ability to organize and measure. War demanded the coordination of millions of people and tons of cargo, and whole disciplines grew from that need. In Britain, and then in the United States, small groups of scientists began practicing “operations research”—the mathematical analysis of how to position radar, how to form naval convoys, and at what depth to detonate depth charges. At American shipyards building Liberty ships, labor input per vessel fell by more than half from late 1941 to late 1944, according to calculations by economist Allan Searle (1945). This became one of the classic examples of the so-called learning curve: the more often a team does the same thing, the better it becomes at doing it. Economist Peter Thompson later showed (2001) that some of the gains came from new machinery and simplified tasks rather than experience alone—but even with this qualification, the example remained a classic. The Training Within Industry program taught foremen simple methods of instruction and job improvement; after the war, American specialists brought these methods to Japan, where they became one of the roots of the future Toyota Production System.

The same category includes an invention without which this entire book would have been impossible: measuring the economy as a whole. In 1934, commissioned by the United States Senate, Simon Kuznets presented the first official estimates of American national income. For the first time, it was possible to see how far the country’s output had fallen during the Depression rather than guessing from individual industries. In Britain in 1941, Richard Stone and James Meade, working with Keynes’s support, compiled the first national accounts in order to understand how many resources could be devoted to war without ruining the home front. Gross domestic product—the measure used ever since to quantify everything—grew out of this work. Kuznets himself, however, warned that the welfare of a nation could scarcely be inferred from a single national-income figure. That caveat bears directly on our question: in national accounts, an artillery shell and a school desk look identical—as value produced. We must distinguish between them ourselves.

The sixth channel was institutions that lower the price of fear. We discussed deposit insurance, pensions, unemployment benefits, collective bargaining, and the Saltsjöbaden Agreement in detail in the previous section and will not repeat ourselves here. We need only add what matters most for our framework: by the criterion of resilience, all of them fostered development. They did not make a single machine run faster, but they reduced the likelihood that the next crisis would once again leave machines and people idle for years. After 1945, the price of trust—the fifth of our criteria—was lower in the West than ever before.

Finally, catastrophe had one bitter side effect: the migration of knowledge. After 1933, thousands of scientists, engineers, doctors, and architects fled Germany, Austria, and fascist Italy, and later occupied Europe: Albert Einstein, Enrico Fermi, Leo Szilard, Hans Bethe, and many others. For Germany, this was a self-inflicted destruction—to be discussed in the next section. For the United States and Britain, which took them in, it was one of the greatest influxes of human capital in history: knowledge and skill embodied in people. Economists Petra Moser, Alessandra Voena, and Fabian Waldinger studied American patents and showed in a 2014 paper that, in the fields of chemistry where émigrés from Germany worked, the number of inventions in the United States rose appreciably after their arrival. Knowledge did not disappear; it moved to where it was not persecuted.

Put all of this together and ask the chapter’s question—where did the era’s surplus go?—and the answer is lopsided. By comparison with everything produced, the share devoted to development was small: schools, laboratories, rural power lines, and national accounts cost incomparably less than shells and battleships. Yet this was the share that proved most durable. Trenches were filled in and tanks melted down, while diplomas, insulin, hybrid seed, and the habit of measuring national income remained and became the foundation of postwar growth. This is perhaps the principal pattern of the entire book, visible here with particular clarity: destruction is swift and expensive; development is slow and comparatively cheap; and investment in development continues to pay dividends long after the destruction has been forgotten.

## What Destroyed

This section contains more material than its counterpart in any other chapter of the book, so we must analyze rather than merely enumerate. The destruction of 1914–1945 advanced along all four lines of our framework at once: people died, physical capital burned, knowledge disappeared, and trust was torn apart. It is important to see not only the scale of each loss but also how quickly each could be restored—the answer will prove surprising.

Let us begin with people, because no other resource can replace that loss. By generally accepted estimates, the First World War killed roughly nine to ten million military personnel and several million civilians. France lost about 1.4 million soldiers—a substantial share of all its young men; almost every French village still has a memorial bearing a long list of names. Immediately after the war, in 1918–1920, an influenza pandemic known as the “Spanish flu” swept the world; estimates of its death toll vary widely, from seventeen million to fifty million and more. The war did not create the virus, but overcrowded barracks, troopships, and hospitals helped it spread around the planet. Then came the Russian Civil War and the Volga famine of 1921–1922, which, by common estimates, claimed several million lives. Finally came the Second World War, with its seventy to eighty-five million dead, of whom the Soviet Union lost approximately twenty-seven million according to the official estimate. The Holocaust—the murder of some six million European Jews—stands apart even in this catalogue: it was killing undertaken not for conquest and not in battle, but for annihilation itself.

It is difficult to speak of the economic dimension of these deaths, but the framework of this book requires us to name it directly: with those people vanished years of education, crafts, families that were never formed, and teachers who never taught anyone. Demographers could still see these wars in the age pyramids of France, Germany, and the Soviet Union half a century later—as deep notches, like the marks of a blow.

Second came physical capital. In 1918, while retreating from northern France, the German army flooded and dynamited the coal mines of Nord–Pas-de-Calais, one of the country’s principal industrial regions; restoring them took several years. In the Second World War, destruction became systematic. Both sides on the Eastern Front employed scorched-earth tactics: retreating Soviet forces evacuated or blew up factories, while retreating German forces destroyed anything that might be useful to those advancing behind them. According to official Soviet figures, 1,710 towns and more than seventy thousand villages and hamlets were destroyed in the occupied territories. Allied bombing reduced the centers of almost every major German city to ruins, while American incendiary raids in 1945 burned out much of Tokyo and dozens of other Japanese cities.

Here the first surprise appears. Physical capital, which seems the most substantial, was restored faster than anything else. Despite the impression created by the ruins, German industry had lost a relatively smaller share of its machinery and equipment than of its housing by 1945. According to calculations by Rolf Krengel and Werner Abelshauser, the industrial capital stock in 1945 actually exceeded its 1936 level: factories often stood on the outskirts, while bombs fell on old city centers. Within fifteen years, Germany and Japan would become exemplars of the “economic miracle” discussed in Chapter Fourteen. Buildings and machines can be rebuilt in a few years—provided that the people who know how to build them remain, along with rules under which investment can be made without fear.

Third came knowledge, and here recovery followed a very different course. In April 1933, the Nazi “Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service” made Jewish ancestry and political disloyalty grounds for dismissal from government employment; because German universities were state institutions, the law struck them directly. Mathematician Emmy Noether lost the right to teach at Göttingen and left for the United States; physicist Max Born was removed from his post and moved to Britain; Albert Einstein, who was abroad, decided not to return; chemist Fritz Haber resigned when ordered to dismiss employees of Jewish descent and left Germany. In the institutes of the Kaiser Wilhelm Society alone, 104 scientists were dismissed; four of those expelled later died in concentration camps. In May 1933, students burned books in public squares. Economist Fabian Waldinger, who studied the fate of German universities after these dismissals, showed that the quality of graduate training declined markedly in departments that lost prominent professors and did not recover for a long time.

In the Soviet Union, knowledge was lost through a different legal and political mechanism. Economists Nikolai Kondratiev and Alexander Chayanov were arrested in 1930 in the case of the so-called Labor Peasant Party. They were charged not with their scholarly views as such, but with membership in a counterrevolutionary organization, sabotage, anti-Soviet agitation, and preparations to overthrow the government. In 1932, the OGPU Collegium sentenced them to imprisonment; during the Great Terror, they were tried again and executed—Chayanov in 1937 and Kondratiev in 1938. The verdicts were later overturned and both scholars rehabilitated; the Labor Peasant Party case was closed for lack of evidence that a crime had occurred. Their economic views diverged from official policy and were publicly vilified even before their arrests, but the phrase “shot for a theory” would be as much an oversimplification as treating the charges against them as proven.

These stories do not make Nazism and Soviet socialism one and the same. In Nazi Germany, a person’s ancestry was explicitly written into law as grounds for expulsion; in the Soviet case, persecution was framed as a criminal charge of counterrevolution and sabotage. For the question posed by this book, the similarity is narrower and more concrete: when a scholar’s ability to work—and very life—depends on political acceptability, a country loses its accumulated knowledge. The differences in ideology, law, and methods of repression must not be erased.

Fourth came people turned into a resource. By the summer of 1944, according to figures cited by historian Adam Tooze in *The Wages of Destruction* (2006), some seven and a half million foreign workers—most of them forcibly deported from Poland and the Soviet Union—and prisoners of war were working in the German economy; concentration-camp inmates came in addition. This was a return to an economy of slavery in Europe’s most industrialized country, and it confirms the lesson of the chapter on Rome: forced labor is cheap for the master, but unproductive and sustainable only through constant violence. German plans for the occupied East—the so-called Hunger Plan, prepared in 1941 under State Secretary Herbert Backe—explicitly assumed that millions of Soviet city dwellers would die if grain were diverted to the Reich and the army. The Siege of Leningrad, which claimed between six hundred thousand and more than a million civilian lives by varying estimates, formed part of the same logic.

Famine in general became one of the era’s principal weapons—and it was not always deliberate. In British Bengal in 1943, between two and three million people died by varying estimates. Economist Amartya Sen, who witnessed the famine as a child, showed in *Poverty and Famines* (1981) that the harvest that year was not catastrophically smaller than usual. People were killed not so much by a shortage of rice as by wartime inflation, grain purchases for the army and the cities, and bans on shipments between provinces, which deprived the poor of the ability to buy it. Sen’s conclusion became one of the most important in twentieth-century economics: famine is more often a failure of distribution than a failure of production. His estimates have been challenged—other researchers, including historian Mark Tauger, give greater weight to crop losses after the cyclone of 1942 and to a fungal disease of rice, while demographers Tim Dyson and Arup Maharatna put the death toll lower, at roughly two million—but most historians today accept the central mechanism.

Fifth came trust. German hyperinflation erased the savings of the middle class; in the United States, according to the standard figures, the Depression closed roughly nine thousand banks between 1930 and 1933, taking the savings of millions of depositors with them. We have already discussed how these events unfolded, as well as Marienthal, where unemployment destroyed both the habit of purposeful activity and any sense of a future. Here let us add another dimension: trust between countries. In the summer of 1931, after the collapse of the Viennese bank Creditanstalt, President Hoover announced a one-year moratorium on reparations and war-debt payments. It was followed by “standstill agreements” under which Germany ceased repaying short-term foreign loans. The Lausanne Conference effectively cancelled reparations in 1932, and by 1934 almost every European debtor except Finland had stopped paying its war debts to the United States. In response, the United States Congress passed the Johnson Act of 1934, prohibiting new loans to governments that had defaulted on old ones. Each of these decisions could be justified in isolation—the debts truly were unsustainable—but together they meant that the international credit on which the first globalization had rested ceased to exist. Countries shifted to currency controls and clearing: exchanging goods through intergovernmental accounts without freely traded currency, while private capital stopped crossing borders. Trust that had taken a century to build—from London bills of exchange to the gold standard—was destroyed in three years, and rebuilding it would require the new institutions of Bretton Woods.

Finally came destruction of the land. In those same 1930s, the southern Great Plains of the United States were struck by the “Dust Bowl,” a series of immense dust storms. It had been prepared by the boom of the preceding decades: high wheat prices during the First World War and the arrival of tractors made it possible to plow millions of acres of prairie grass that had held the soil in place. When drought came, the wind lifted that soil into the air; clouds of dust reached the Atlantic coast, and hundreds of thousands of people abandoned their farms. This destruction resembles the salinization of Mesopotamian fields in Chapter Three: a short-term surplus was extracted at the expense of long-term capital that no one had counted.

Now for some candid qualifications. Some scholars also see a “cleansing” side to catastrophe. In *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982), economist Mancur Olson argued that stable societies gradually accumulate interest groups—cartels, lobbies, privileged associations—that impede growth, and that war and occupation broke these groups in Germany and Japan, thereby helping the miracles that followed. Like Thomas Piketty, historian Walter Scheidel shows in *The Great Leveler* (2017) that it was precisely the world wars and the Depression, by destroying great fortunes, that made the middle of the twentieth century the most equal period in the history of industrial societies. These are serious observations, and the facts are difficult to dispute. But their lesson is not that destruction is beneficial. It is that entrenched rents and inequality can become so durable that societies failed to dismantle them without upheaval—which means the central question is whether a way can be found that is not paid for with tens of millions of lives.

To sum up the section, the losses form a hierarchy. What is easiest to see—buildings, bridges, factories—is restored most quickly. Knowledge and skills return more slowly: exiled scholars did not come back, and ruined schools and academic departments took a generation to rebuild. Trust and institutions recover slowest of all: the memory of savings burned away, of property seized on account of ancestry, of famine engineered by calculation, endured for decades. This is the chapter’s principal answer to the question of where the surplus went: the most valuable thing destroyed appeared in no set of statistics.

## What Was Waste

After the section on destruction, it may seem that no room remains in this chapter for waste: what could be “idle” against a background of trenches and famine? But recall the distinguishing feature in the book’s framework: waste occurs when resources are expended without raising productivity or well-being, even though nothing is destroyed directly. The years 1914–1945 provided perhaps the most vivid examples of such spending in all of history—and, at the same time, the most uncomfortable question for our framework: what if waste can be better than nothing?

Let us begin with the most obvious category: arms races. An early-twentieth-century battleship was to its nation what a pyramid was to a pharaoh—a symbol of power that cost an immense sum and produced almost nothing. The Anglo-German naval race discussed in the previous chapter ended with the dreadnoughts of the two fleets truly confronting one another only once during the entire First World War, at the Battle of Jutland in 1916, after which the German fleet spent almost all the rest of the war in harbor. Curiously, after the war governments themselves recognized the futility of this expenditure. At the Washington Conference of 1921–1922, the United States, Britain, Japan, France, and Italy agreed to limit battleship tonnage and suspend construction for ten years. It was a rare moment in history when rivals deliberately halted a wasteful race—and it did not last long: Japan withdrew from the treaty system in the mid-1930s. Its answer was the *Yamato* and the *Musashi*, the largest battleships ever built. Both were sunk by American carrier aircraft—the *Musashi* in 1944, the *Yamato* in April 1945—without ever playing the role for which a country poor in steel and oil had invested resources in them. The age of the battleship ended before they were finished.

The Maginot Line met a similar fate. France built this chain of fortifications along its border with Germany from roughly 1929 to 1938. Its logic is easy to understand: a country that had lost a million and a half soldiers in the previous war wanted to protect people with concrete. Yet in 1940, German tanks passed to the north through the Ardennes, and Europe’s most expensive defensive system played almost no part in the decisive battles. A qualification is needed here: military historians debate whether the line was useless—it did channel the attack toward the area where French command expected it, but the attack was poorly met there. As an investment of the surplus, however, it illustrates a typical trap: money is spent preparing for the last war.

The most astonishing example was Germany’s V-2 rocket. Technically, it was an extraordinary achievement: the first ballistic missile and the direct ancestor of space launch vehicles. Economically, it was almost a pure loss. In *The Rocket and the Reich* (1995), historian Michael Neufeld estimates that the program cost roughly two billion Reichsmarks—about one-quarter of the Manhattan Project’s cost in dollar terms, but a comparable burden for the far smaller German economy. Yet while the atomic bomb became part of the denouement of the Pacific war, the several thousand rockets fired at London and Antwerp had almost no military significance. Worse still, they were assembled by prisoners in the underground Mittelbau-Dora camp, and historians estimate that more people died producing them than were killed by the rockets themselves. Here waste and destruction merged into one. The knowledge accumulated at Peenemünde, however, did not disappear: after the war, the United States and the Soviet Union carried it away along with the engineers, and it became a foundation of their space programs. Even this example, then, is not entirely pure: one state’s waste became a developmental investment for others.

The second category was the prestige construction of dictatorships. Hitler and his architect Albert Speer planned to transform Berlin into “Germania,” a world capital with a domed Great Hall intended to hold some 180,000 people. Construction barely began, but in 1941 an enormous concrete cylinder was cast in Berlin as a test structure to determine whether the sandy ground could support the weight of the future triumphal arch. Weighing more than twelve thousand tons, the cylinder still stands: demolishing it proved too difficult. In Moscow in 1931, the Cathedral of Christ the Savior was blown up to make way for the Palace of the Soviets—a building taller than New York’s Empire State Building, topped by a colossal statue of Lenin. The foundation was laid, but during the war its steel structures were dismantled for defense needs, and in 1960 the open pit became the Moskva swimming pool. Both stories directly continue the theme of the pyramids from Chapter Three, but without their excuses: the pyramids, at least, were completed, and a school of organization grew around them.

In fairness, that very New York skyscraper can be placed beside them. The Empire State Building opened in May 1931. Conceived during the speculative building boom of the 1920s, it was completed in the depths of the Depression. Much of its floor space remained empty for years, and New Yorkers nicknamed it the “Empty State Building.” This was the private, market version of waste: the surplus went not toward expanding production, but into a bet on endlessly rising real-estate prices. The building did eventually fill up and pay for itself—which distinguishes it from the concrete cylinder in Berlin.

The third category was the destruction of one’s own harvest—perhaps the era’s bitterest spectacle. In 1933, as part of the New Deal, the American government bought and slaughtered roughly six million piglets to raise collapsed prices, and paid farmers to plow under some of the cotton they had already planted. This was happening at the very time that people stood in soup lines in the cities. Brazil, the world’s largest coffee producer, began burning its coffee and dumping it into the sea in the early 1930s—tens of millions of sacks over more than a decade, by common estimates. The logic of these measures is understandable: a farmer who sells produce for less than it costs goes bankrupt, and then both the farmer and the future harvest disappear. From the standpoint of our question, however, this was waste in its purest form: labor, land, and seed had been expended, but the food never reached people. The root of the problem was not a surfeit of food, but that people lacked the money to buy it—the same failure of distribution as in the Bengal famine, only with the sign reversed.

The fourth category was rent created by prohibition and protection. From 1920 to 1933, Prohibition banned the production and sale of alcohol in the United States. People drank less, but did not stop: the trade went underground, and money that had once gone to legitimate brewers—and to the treasury as excise taxes—flowed instead to gangs such as Al Capone’s Chicago organization. The state, meanwhile, spent resources fighting them. The result was classic rent: income arising not from the production of anything new, but from artificial scarcity and a willingness to risk prison. In Europe during the same period, rent-seeking took other forms: cartels, which were legal in Germany and became part of the state system after 1933; tariffs secured by industry lobbies; and “autarkic” projects such as German synthetic gasoline made from coal and the exploitation of low-grade iron ores, producing at great expense what could have been bought more cheaply. Germany did, however, have an argument: in wartime, there might be no one from whom to buy. That is precisely why such projects are difficult to separate from military preparation—and therefore from the destruction they were preparing.

And finally, the era’s largest and most invisible waste: idleness. In 1932–1933, roughly one-quarter of the American labor force was unemployed; in Germany, some six million people were out of work. Factories stood still, machines rusted, and people who knew how to work and wanted to work sat at home. No one was wasting anything—and that was the waste. It is a loss invisible in photographs: no ruins, no battleships, only goods and services that could have been produced but were not.

Here arises the uncomfortable question posed by John Maynard Keynes. In *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), he half-jokingly remarked that if the treasury buried banknotes in old coal mines and allowed private firms to dig them up, unemployment would disappear and society’s real income would probably increase. He explicitly compared the building of pyramids and even wars: from the standpoint of employment, they might be better than inactivity. Keynes added, of course, that building houses and roads would be far more sensible—but his point was serious: when resources lie idle, even waste can set a developmental chain in motion, because the digger’s wages become the baker’s revenue. Keynes’s critics, then and now, object that such spending has a price: debt that must be repaid, and a state habit of spending that does not disappear when the idleness ends. The dispute remains unresolved, and we shall return to it in Chapter Fifteen.

In the America of the 1930s, this debate unfolded in real time. It was then that the word “boondoggle” entered common usage—the term critics applied to pointless work invented to occupy the unemployed. The Works Progress Administration (WPA), created in 1935, did sometimes pay for questionable projects. But it also built or repaired hundreds of thousands of kilometers of roads and thousands of schools, bridges, and hospitals, many of which remain in service. The boundary between waste and development ran not through the question “Did the state spend?” but through the question “What remained after the spending?”

The harshest example of this dispute is the war itself. What finally eliminated unemployment in America was not public works but military orders from 1940 to 1943. Output surged, and millions of people who had been idle found work. But what surged was the production of tanks and bombers, which were then burned up or melted down. Does this mean the war “cured” the Depression? From the standpoint of employment, yes. From the standpoint of our question, no: the surplus at last drawn from idle hands went into the fire. The postwar governments’ lesson would be different: if the state could mobilize idle resources for war, it could do so for roads, schools, and housing as well. How successfully it did so is the subject of Chapter Fourteen.

The section’s conclusion can be put this way. Waste appeared in two guises during this era. One was familiar from earlier chapters: battleships, fortifications, palaces, rents—the surplus spent on prestige and illusory security without adding to anyone’s productivity. The other was new and perhaps the era’s most important discovery: idleness, when the surplus is not spent at all because no one is able to buy it. For centuries, people had fought the first by limiting power and encouraging competition. Until the 1930s, they did not even have concepts with which to fight the second—and one of the chief legacies of the catastrophe is that those concepts emerged.

## Lesson of the Era

Only nine years separate Keynes’s Londoner, ordering goods from around the world by telephone, from the Berlin housewife running to the shop with a sack of banknotes. Only a few hundred kilometers separate the American farmer burning corn in his stove from the soup line in the neighboring city. And between them and the ruins of 1945 lie thirty-one years during which humanity knew and could do more than ever before, yet lived worse than it might have. This chapter is the book’s purest experiment in what happens when the surplus goes to the wrong place. Let us reduce it to five propositions.

**First: the catastrophe was determined not by technology, but by the rules under which people chose to live after the war.** The worlds of 1918 and 1945 emerged from war equally devastated. But after the first war, the victors demanded reparations that, as Keynes warned, could not be paid, and debts that no one intended to forgive. Money moved in a circle from New York to Berlin, from there to Paris and London, and back to New York, until the circle broke in 1931. No power assumed the role of stabilizer—the one that keeps markets open and lends in a crisis—and, in Kindleberger’s view, this was precisely why an ordinary downturn became the Great Depression. After the second war, many of the same people did almost everything in reverse: Bretton Woods, the IMF, the World Bank, the United Nations, social insurance based on the Beveridge Report. This is the best proof that the economy is not a force of nature: the same ruins can be turned into twenty years of hostility or twenty years of growth, and the difference is determined by agreements, not reserves of coal.

**Second: money is trust, and it can be destroyed from either direction.** Germany in 1923 showed what happens when the state prints money to pay bills it cannot meet in any other way: within a few months, the savings of an entire middle class turned into paper, and with them the belief that honest work and thrift would be rewarded. But America and Europe in 1929–1933 demonstrated the opposite disease: fidelity to gold at any cost, with central banks contracting the money supply in the midst of collapse merely to preserve convertibility. In *Golden Fetters*, Barry Eichengreen showed that the countries which left gold first—Britain in 1931 and the United States in 1933—also emerged from the Depression first. The lesson is not that “printing” is good or bad, but that money has a purpose—to let people exchange and plan—and any monetary order that places its own purity above that purpose will sooner or later destroy the very thing for which it exists.

**Third: the surplus can disappear without ever being spent.** Every previous chapter asked where resources went—into canals or palaces. The Depression added a question no one had asked before: what if the resources exist, people are ready to work, factories remain intact, and yet output still falls by a quarter—and industrial production by almost half—because no one dares to buy? Idleness proved to be the era’s greatest waste, larger than all the battleships and the Maginot Line combined. Keynes gave it a name: insufficient demand—and proposed a remedy: when private buyers freeze, the state must spend. In fairness, the argument did not end there. Hayek and his followers objected that government spending treated the symptom while entrenching the errors of the boom and accumulating debt; that the New Deal in some respects prolonged the recovery; that it was war, not wise policy, that finally eliminated unemployment in America. But even Keynes’s critics acknowledged the essential point: leaving millions of people idle when their labor is needed is not neutral inaction but a loss that society pays for every day.

**Fourth: by closing themselves off from the world, countries all became poorer together—and more dangerous.** The Smoot–Hawley Act, retaliatory tariffs, currency restrictions, and the struggle for “one’s own” markets compressed world trade along Kindleberger’s spiral. Each country acted rationally for itself and, together with all the others, drove itself into poverty. But autarky—the desire to produce everything at home—had a darker outcome as well. A state that cannot buy oil and grain begins to think about seizing them. Germany’s doctrine of *Lebensraum* and Japan’s “Co-Prosperity Sphere” were not merely ideologies but calculations: if trade is closed, the surplus can be obtained only through conquest. This is the lesson opposite to Norman Angell’s optimism: interdependence really does make war unprofitable, but only while interdependence exists. Destroying it returns the logic of plunder to the world.

**Fifth: a society can redirect its surplus very quickly—and therein lie both its hope and its danger.** The war showed that millions of people and tons of metal could be switched within two or three years from automobiles to tanks, from unemployment to round-the-clock shifts; that shipyards could reduce the time needed to assemble a vessel from eight months to six weeks, and in one celebrated record to five days; and that the state could calculate an entire national economy in a single table of national accounts. Penicillin, radar, the jet engine, and electronic computers appeared or matured because they received resources no one had provided in peacetime. This suggested an enticing conclusion, one drawn by many contemporaries: if such mobilization is possible for destruction, why not for development? That question will become central to the chapters that follow, in the contest between planned and market systems. But the era also offers a warning. Mobilization worked when the objective was singular and obvious—victory; its price—suppressed consumption, forced labor, camps, famine—was hidden behind that objective. The ability to gather the surplus rapidly into a few hands tells us nothing about what those hands will spend it on.

Now for the answer to the question running through this book. Where did the surplus of the years 1914–1945 go? Most went into destruction: shells and bombs, armies that destroyed rather than produced, camps where people were turned into expendable material. A substantial share never came into being at all—it vanished in the Depression’s silent factories and untilled fields. Some went into pure prestige: battleships, dictators’ palaces, projects such as the V-2. Only a smaller share went into what outlived the era: laboratories and medicines, schools and electrification, national accounts and deposit insurance, institutions built in 1944–1945 to keep the catastrophe from recurring. The era’s paradox is that this smaller share proved extraordinarily fruitful: it became the foundation for the fastest growth in history, the subject of Chapter Fourteen.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions. The largest figures in this chapter—the numbers killed by wars, famines, and repression—remain estimates, each contested by historians. The causes of the Depression are still explained in different ways: monetarists point to the errors of central banks, Keynesians to the collapse of demand, adherents of the Austrian school to the consequences of the credit boom of the 1920s; all are probably right in part. In speaking of the “rules after the war,” it is easy to forget that Versailles and Bretton Woods were written under wholly different conditions: in 1919, the victors had neither a common enemy on the horizon nor a single power capable of financing reconstruction, while in 1945 they had both the Cold War and the United States. Finally, it is easy to judge with hindsight: many of those who clung to gold or raised tariffs sincerely believed they were saving their countries—as did those who built the Maginot Line.

How many people died; how far output, trade, and prices fell; how income and life expectancy had changed by 1950; and how widely the fates of countries diverged—all of this appears in the section of figures.

## Figures

For these thirty years, statistics are both better and worse than for the prewar era. Better, because it was then, as described above, that states learned to measure national income, unemployment, and production using a common methodology. Worse, because wars, occupations, and dictatorships destroyed archives, classified data, or falsified them. The number of victims is the most disputed part of this section, and ranges are given throughout.

**People.** By most estimates, the First World War took the lives of nine to ten million military personnel and several million civilians; France alone lost roughly 1.4 million soldiers—about one in every eight men of military age. The war was followed immediately by the “Spanish flu,” the influenza pandemic of 1918–1920. Estimates of its death toll vary especially widely, from seventeen million to fifty million and more, meaning that influenza may have killed more people than the guns. By common estimates, the Second World War cost humanity seventy to eighty-five million lives, most of them civilian. The Soviet Union lost approximately twenty-seven million people according to the official Russian estimate; China lost between fifteen and twenty million by various calculations; and roughly six million Jews were murdered in the Holocaust. Even so, according to Maddison, the world’s population grew from about 1.8 billion in 1913 to 2.5 billion in 1950: medicine and fertilizers outside the war zones worked faster than weapons within them.

**Money that ceased to be money.** In 1914, one dollar was worth just over four German marks; in November 1923, it was worth about 4.2 trillion. The Rentenmark introduced that same month was exchanged for the old paper marks at a rate of one to one trillion. Savings that would have bought a house in 1913 would not cover a tram fare ten years later.

**Collapse.** In the United States, real GDP contracted by roughly one-quarter from 1929 to 1933, industrial production by almost half, and consumer prices by about one-quarter. Unemployment reached approximately one-quarter of the labor force in 1933, while about nine thousand banks—roughly one in three—closed between 1930 and 1933. In Germany, some six million people were officially registered as unemployed at the beginning of 1932. According to Kindleberger’s famous “spiral,” the value of world trade fell by about two-thirds between early 1929 and early 1933; in physical volume it fell by roughly one-quarter, with the rest of the decline due to collapsing prices.

**Mobilization.** According to estimates by Mark Harrison in *The Economics of World War II* (1998), at the height of the war military spending absorbed roughly two-fifths of national output in the United States and more than half in Britain and the Soviet Union; in Germany and Japan, the share was even higher by the end of the war. By comparison, during the peacetime 1920s, the United States federal government spent only a few percent of GDP on everything combined, including the military. American shipyards launched about 2,700 Liberty ships during the war. Lend-Lease—roughly fifty billion dollars at the time—amounted to nearly nine-tenths of the entire American GDP of 1933.

**Income per person.** In Maddison’s tables, measured in “1990 international dollars,” world GDP per person rose from $1,526 in 1913 to $2,111 in 1950—an average of less than one percent a year, markedly slower than during the previous forty years. But the average conceals divergence. The United States nearly doubled income per person, from $5,300 to $9,560. Britain rose from $4,900 to $6,900. After two wars, hyperinflation, and defeat, Germany was only slightly richer in 1950 than in 1913: $3,900 compared with $3,650. By the same estimates, the Soviet Union nearly doubled the figure for the Russian Empire, from $1,490 to $2,840—at a price that will be discussed in the next chapter. China and India, meanwhile, were poorer in 1950 than in 1913, at roughly $450 and $620 respectively. The thirty years of catastrophe widened the gap between America and the rest of the world more than any other period.

**The world closed.** According to Maddison, exports as a share of world output fell from roughly eight percent in 1913 to five and a half percent in 1950—the world retreated half a century in openness.

**Inequality narrowed.** According to calculations by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, the share of American income received by the richest ten percent, about forty-five percent in the late 1920s, had fallen to roughly one-third by the mid-1940s; the decline was even steeper in France and Britain. The causes were the destruction of capital in the wars, inflation that devalued rentiers’ bonds, high taxes, and wage controls. This is the book’s only major episode of leveling achieved largely through destruction. In *The Great Leveler* (2017), Walter Scheidel argues that this has almost always been the pattern in history, although many historians dispute such a bleak regularity.

**Length of life.** Despite everything listed above, life expectancy rose. In England and Wales, it increased from about fifty-three years in 1911 to nearly seventy by 1950; in the United States, to roughly sixty-eight. According to James Riley’s estimate, the world average rose from just over thirty years before the First World War to approximately forty-five to forty-eight by 1950. Vaccines, sulfonamides, penicillin, clean water, and fertilizers saved more lives than wars took—but they would have saved still more had the era’s surplus not been spent taking those lives.

The chapter’s balance sheet in figures is this. By the author’s rough aggregation of disparate estimates, on the order of one hundred million people—between one hundred and one hundred twenty million—died in wars, famines, and terror, not counting the “Spanish flu”; one-quarter of the output of the world’s largest economy vanished without a shot being fired; trade’s share of world output shrank by almost one-third; and yet income and length of life rose where wars were not being fought. How two opposing systems tried to build a new world from these ruins, and how their contest ended, is the story of the chapters that follow—beginning with the planned economies.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; an error by a factor of one and a half to two is possible. The unit is an hour of a typical worker’s labor: an American worker in the 1930s earned about fifty cents an hour—forty-five to sixty-five cents in industry, according to *Historical Statistics of the United States*, and less in the countryside—while a German industrial worker in the late 1930s earned about eighty pfennigs (requires verification). A working life is seventy thousand hours. For reference, a fifty-kilogram sack of wheat cost about $1.50 in the United States in the 1930s and about ten Reichsmarks in Germany (requires verification).

### Great Depression Idleness, United States, 1930–1940

**Inputs.** In Stanley Lebergott’s series, the number of unemployed rose from four million in 1930 to almost thirteen million in 1933 and never fell below seven and a half million before the war—a total of roughly one hundred million person-years over eleven years. I subtract the “normal” three to four percent; Michael Darby, moreover, counts people employed on public works as employed. That leaves sixty to ninety million person-years of involuntary idleness.

**Hours and lives.** One hundred twenty to one hundred ninety billion hours—between one and a half and three million working lives; I use two million. At fifty cents an hour, that is sixty to ninety-five billion dollars, almost the entire GDP of 1929. This agrees with another calculation: over the decade, the shortfall in output relative to the 1929 level adds up to roughly one year’s product. In sacks of grain, it is fifty to sixty billion—more than a century of American wheat harvests. The figure is absurd because grain itself was worth almost nothing during the Depression.

**Alternative.** According to its final report of 1946, the Works Progress Administration spent roughly eleven billion dollars over eight years and provided work for eight and a half million people. America’s idleness alone amounted to six to eight such programs of roads, schools, and bridges, and no resources would have had to be taken from anyone to carry them out: hands, machinery, and steel were standing idle. These two million lives were not cut short, but lived to no purpose. For comparison, the First World War killed nine to ten million military personnel.

### The V-2, Germany, 1936–1945

**Inputs.** Michael Neufeld estimates the program at roughly two billion Reichsmarks. Of the more than sixty thousand prisoners at Mittelbau-Dora, around twenty thousand died by common estimates; the rockets themselves killed between five and nine thousand people in Britain and Belgium (requires verification).

**Hours and lives.** Roughly two and a half billion hours—thirty to forty thousand working lives. In grain, about two hundred million sacks, or roughly two years of Germany’s wheat harvest. And separately, literal lives: for every person killed by a rocket, two to four died assembling it.

**Alternative.** By rough estimates, an apartment in German social housing in the late 1920s cost five to eight thousand marks (requires verification): the program equaled 250,000 to 400,000 apartments, about one year of housing construction in the Weimar Republic at its peak.

**Conclusion.** The rocket consumed tens of thousands of working lives and caused tens of thousands of actual deaths; the Depression consumed two million lives without a shot being fired. The era’s greatest waste was committed not by dictators, but by rules under which people ready to work and factories ready to produce could not find one another.
