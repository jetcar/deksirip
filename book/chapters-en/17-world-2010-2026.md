# Chapter 17. The World, 2010–2026

## A Portrait of the Era

The Suez Canal, the morning of March 23, 2021. A sandstorm rises over the desert, and the wind strikes the side of the *Ever Given*—a container ship about four hundred meters long, almost as long as the Empire State Building laid on its side. Steel boxes, the same containers from the previous chapter, are stacked in tiers on deck—some twenty thousand slots. A gust turns the giant vessel across the narrow channel; its bow digs into one bank and its stern into the other. The canal, which by common estimates carries around one eighth of world seaborne trade, is closed.

Within hours, a photograph spreads around the world and becomes a symbol of the era: a small yellow excavator digs beside the ship’s bow, looking like a child’s toy next to the colossus. For six days, salvage crews, tugboats, and dredgers try to shift the vessel. During that time, a queue of almost four hundred ships forms at the canal’s entrances. The industry publication *Lloyd’s List* estimates the value of cargo left motionless each day at roughly nine to ten billion dollars. Some shipowners choose not to wait and send their vessels around Africa—an extra week to ten days of travel, as in the age of Vasco da Gama.

This scene contains almost the entire history of the decade. The world described in Chapter 16 had learned to make goods where they were cheapest and carry them to where they were dearer along chains half a planet long, with every link operating “just in time”—without excess stock in warehouses. It was incredibly efficient as long as nothing broke. The *Ever Given* became stuck in the canal a year after almost everything had broken at once.

On March 11, 2020, the World Health Organization declared the outbreak of a new coronavirus a pandemic. Within weeks, the streets of Milan, New York, and Moscow were empty; airports became parking lots for aircraft; billions of people were confined to their homes. According to the International Monetary Fund, the world economy contracted by about three percent in 2020—the deepest decline since the Second World War. The WHO estimates that the pandemic claimed around fifteen million lives in 2020–2021 once unrecorded deaths are included (other models put the figure as high as twenty million). Then, when people resumed buying, it turned out that factories, ships, and containers were not where they were needed: the cost of moving one box from China to Europe rose severalfold, and automobile assembly lines stopped for lack of microchips worth a few dollars. Prices, which had barely moved for twenty years in rich countries, began to rise. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, annual inflation in the United States reached 9.1 percent in June 2022, its highest level in forty years; in the euro area, it exceeded ten percent that autumn. Generations raised to believe that prices were stable and major crises belonged to the past learned for the second time in a decade and a half that they did not.

The pandemic was the loudest shock of these years, but not the only one. The era began amid the ruins of 2008: to prevent economic collapse, central banks cut interest rates almost to zero and spent years creating money to buy bonds—the mechanism will be explained below. For a decade, money became almost free, while in Europe and Japan rates even fell below zero: banks and large depositors paid for the privilege of keeping money in a safe place—something no banker in the preceding chapters could have imagined. The prices of housing in major cities, shares, and everything that could be bought on credit rose faster than wages. Young people in London, Sydney, and Toronto discovered that an apartment their parents had bought for a few years’ salaries now cost ten or even fifteen—and around twenty in Hong Kong.

In February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale war against Ukraine. In addition to the human losses and destruction discussed in the section “What Destroyed,” it sent European gas prices soaring, severed trade ties built over thirty years, and unleashed a wave of sanctions unlike any the world economy had known. The word “globalization,” which had sounded like a promise in the 1990s, began to sound like a threat: governments spoke of “resilient supply chains,” bringing factories home, and “friendly countries.” In 2025, the United States, the principal architect of the postwar system of free trade described in Chapter 14, raised import tariffs to levels unseen since the 1930s—and the world remembered Chapter 12.

Against this background, two processes unfolded that may prove more important than all the crises. The first concerned energy. Over the decade, solar panels and wind turbines became so much cheaper that, according to the International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA), electricity from new solar plants fell in price by roughly nine tenths during the 2010s and early 2020s, becoming the cheapest of all sources in many countries. At the same time, according to Europe’s Copernicus service, 2024 became the first calendar year in which the planet’s average temperature exceeded the preindustrial level by more than one and a half degrees. In the Paris Agreement of 2015, governments promised to try not to cross this threshold, but the agreement refers to an average over many years, so one hot year did not yet cross it formally—although climate scientists estimate that the crossing is probably less than a decade away. Energy, with which this book began, once again became the central economic question—only now it was not “how do we get more?” but “how do we obtain it without destroying our home?”

The second process concerned machines. On November 30, 2022, the American research company OpenAI, then known mainly to specialists, opened its ChatGPT chatbot to everyone—a program that answered questions and wrote letters and code in human language. Analysts at the bank UBS estimated that around one hundred million people used it within two months—faster than any product before it. Within a couple of years, according to leaders of Google and Microsoft, similar systems were writing between one fifth and one third of the new software code at their companies, as well as translating, drawing, and advising. The Luddites’ question from Chapter 10—what happens to people if machines learn to do their jobs?—returned, but now it concerned not weavers, but lawyers, translators, programmers, and accountants.

According to the UN, the world’s population reached eight billion on November 15, 2022. Never before had so many people lived so long or had access to so much knowledge. A person with an inexpensive phone in a pocket could ask a machine almost anything and receive an answer within seconds. At the same time—and this is the era’s central puzzle—surveys in rich countries recorded a sense that life was becoming not more secure, but more anxious: young people were less able than their parents to afford housing, work seemed increasingly meaningless, and the future increasingly unpredictable.

Thus, this chapter’s question is the same as before and at the same time more urgent than ever: where did the era’s surplus go? It was greater than in any previous decade—machines produced ever more, while knowledge cost almost nothing. But a substantial share, as we will see, went into raising the prices of things that already existed—land, homes, and shares—into the incomes of those who controlled bottlenecks, and into spending on defense and recovery after shocks. The little excavator at the bow of the giant ship is a fitting metaphor: a colossal, finely tuned system that, at its first serious failure, proved dependent on chance and unprepared for it.

## Energy and Technology

The previous chapter left the world with a paradox: computers were everywhere, yet energy use per person in rich countries had barely risen, and after a brief surge in the late 1990s productivity growth had slowed again. The 2010s did not resolve this paradox, but sharpened it. There was more technology than ever, but its most conspicuous forms changed not how much a person produced in an hour, but how that person spent time, whom they spoke with, and what they watched. The true shifts in the economy’s “heavy” foundation—in energy—proceeded more quietly and almost imperceptibly to ordinary people, until they became obvious to everyone at once.

Let us begin with oil, because the era opened with a surprise that even specialists did not expect. During the 2000s, “peak oil” was seriously debated—the moment after which output would inevitably decline and prices rise forever. Instead, the United States experienced a shale revolution. Two long-established techniques—**horizontal drilling** (a well first descends, then turns and runs for kilometers along a rock formation) and **hydraulic fracturing** (water, sand, and chemicals are injected into rock under enormous pressure to open cracks)—were combined and made cheap enough for industrial use. Oil and gas trapped in the dense shale formations of Texas and North Dakota became profitable to extract. According to the US Energy Information Administration, by 2018 the country had become the world’s largest oil producer, overtaking Saudi Arabia and Russia, while cheap shale gas began displacing coal from American power plants. Shale is a good example of development and destruction intertwined within a single technology: it supplied cheap energy and reduced emissions compared with coal, but left behind disputes over groundwater pollution and methane leaks, as well as many companies that went bankrupt when the price of oil fell by more than half in 2014–2016. Many of them had lived on the very cheap credit discussed in the section “Exchange and Money.”

The second energy story—sun, wind, and batteries—was mentioned at the beginning of the chapter, but it is worth understanding why it matters so much. Throughout almost all the history described in this book, energy was something to be *extracted*: it had to be grown in fields, cut in forests, or dug from the ground, and the more of it was needed, the deeper people had to dig. A solar panel works differently: it is an industrial product, like a microchip, and obeys the same law as all factory production—the more units that have been made, the cheaper each additional one becomes. Economists call this a **learning curve**: with every doubling of cumulative output, the price falls by a roughly constant proportion because factories gain experience, equipment improves, and scale increases. For solar modules, researchers at Oxford (Doyne Farmer’s group) and IRENA data estimate that each doubling of output reduced the price by roughly one fifth, and this continued for decades. The same thing happened with lithium-ion batteries: according to estimates by the analytics company BloombergNEF, the average price of a battery pack fell by about nine tenths between 2010 and 2023.

For the first time since waterwheels and windmills, the principal new source of energy was not a fuel but a machine—and, unlike hydroelectric dams tied to a few rivers, a mass-produced machine that could be installed almost anywhere. This transformed the economics of energy at its root: a solar plant has almost no fuel costs; nearly all its expenditure comes in the initial construction. The main question therefore becomes not “who has the oil?” but “who can borrow cheaply to build, and who has the factories?” The answer to the second question proved unequivocal. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), by the early 2020s China accounted for more than four fifths of world production at every major stage of solar-panel manufacture, from purified silicon to finished modules. China also became the largest producer of batteries and electric vehicles: according to the IEA, around seventeen million electric cars were sold worldwide in 2024, nearly two thirds of them in China. The country that was only beginning to emerge from poverty in Chapter 15 now set the price of the century’s central energy technology.

It would be a mistake, however, to think the world had “switched” to new energy. It had *added* it. According to the Energy Institute, successor to the BP Statistical Review, fossil fuels—oil, gas, and coal—still supplied around four fifths of all humanity’s primary energy in 2023, while world coal consumption remained near record levels: it was driven upward by China and India, where hundreds of millions of people acquired refrigerators and air conditioners for the first time. Energy historians, above all Vaclav Smil, have long reminded us that no energy transition in the past was rapid: coal took almost a century to displace firewood, oil spent another half-century displacing coal, and the old fuel did not disappear, but merely lost share. The transition of the 2010s and 2020s is faster than its predecessors, but follows the same pattern. This is an honest counterargument to optimistic forecasts—and at the same time a reminder that the learning curve has already confounded skeptics once: in 2010, few predicted that within ten years solar electricity would become cheaper than coal-fired power in many countries.

Now to machines that think. Here the era divides into two parts. In the 2010s, the defining technology was the smartphone connected to the “cloud”—vast data centers where thousands of servers store and process information for millions of users. By the end of the decade, most adults on the planet owned a smartphone, including in poor countries: a Kenyan farmer learned market prices and transferred money by phone through M-Pesa before many Europeans stopped writing paper checks. By this book’s measures, this is genuine development—especially by the third and fifth criteria: billions of people gained access to knowledge and communication, while the costs of exchange fell more sharply for the poor than for the rich.

The second half of the era belongs to machine learning. Its idea is simple: instead of a programmer writing rules, the machine is shown millions of examples and finds for itself the internal settings that produce correct answers. The idea itself was old, but three conditions came together in the 2010s—enormous volumes of data from the Internet, cheap computing power from graphics processors (chips originally created for video games), and new software designs. In 2012, a neural network from the University of Toronto decisively surpassed all previous methods of image recognition; in 2016, DeepMind’s AlphaGo program defeated Lee Sedol, one of the strongest players of Go—a game thought to be beyond machines for decades to come; in 2017, Google researchers proposed the “transformer” architecture on which large language models, including ChatGPT, were later built. In 2020, another DeepMind system, AlphaFold, learned to predict protein shapes from the sequence of their building blocks with high accuracy—a problem biologists had wrestled with for half a century; in 2024, its creators Demis Hassabis and John Jumper received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry, together with David Baker, who had learned to design new proteins. This is perhaps the purest example of development: a tool that accelerates the production of knowledge itself.

But machine intelligence proved to have a very material body. Training a large model requires tens of thousands of specialized chips operating for months, while data centers consume electricity like cities. According to the IEA, the world’s data centers used around one and a half percent of the planet’s electricity in 2024, and the agency expected their consumption to roughly double by 2030. Giant companies began signing long-term contracts with nuclear plants and investing in new reactors. The “weightless” digital economy returned to where this book began: energy.

And to bottlenecks. By the early 2020s, the world’s most advanced microchips were, in effect, manufactured by one company—Taiwan’s TSMC—while the machines without which those chips could not be made, extreme-ultraviolet lithography systems, were also produced by a single company, the Netherlands’ ASML. Chips for training neural networks were designed mainly by the American company Nvidia, whose market value surpassed three trillion dollars in 2024. Advanced chip production in these years depended on a small number of suppliers; this figure alone cannot compare its degree of concentration with technologies of earlier eras. Compare it with the steam engine from Chapter 10: a capable mechanic with a forge could build one, and within half a century they were being made throughout Europe. Almost no one can manufacture a leading-edge microchip, and this turned the technology into an object of great-power politics—sanctions, export bans, and government subsidies, discussed in the section “Power and Institutions.”

Finally, medicine gave the era one of its most convincing successes. The technology of **mRNA vaccines**—in which a cell receives not a weakened virus but a short instruction molecule that causes the body itself to produce a harmless fragment of viral protein and learn to recognize it—had been under development for around thirty years, largely through modest scientific grants, and was long regarded as unpromising. One of its pioneers, Katalin Kariko, struggled for years to obtain funding and was demoted at her university in the 1990s. When the pandemic arrived, this accumulated groundwork made it possible to create a vaccine less than a year after the viral genome was decoded—previously this had taken years or even decades. In 2023, Kariko and Drew Weissman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. This story is a parable about where surplus should be directed: money invested in “useless” fundamental science paid off in a moment of crisis in a way no stock-market speculation could have done. In fairness, risky private money also helped turn the discovery into medicine: venture investors spent years putting billions into Moderna and BioNTech before either had a single approved drug, while the US military agency DARPA had funded mRNA research since the early 2010s. But the chain began with decades of grants for work regarded as unpromising.

Technologically, then, 2010–2026 was one of the richest eras in history. It gave humanity cheap solar electricity, vaccines created within months, and machines that help us think. Yet productivity statistics in the rich countries remained sluggish throughout the 2010s: according to the OECD, growth in output per hour worked was markedly lower than in the 1950s through the 1970s and even than in the late 1990s. Economists disagree over why. Some, such as Robert Gordon, believe the new inventions are simply less important than electricity and indoor plumbing. Others, such as Erik Brynjolfsson, say statistics poorly measure free digital goods (although Chad Syverson’s checks explain only a small part of the slowdown this way; see Chapter 16). A third group—and this answer comes closest to the thesis of this book—includes OECD economists Dan Andrews, Chiara Criscuolo, and Peter Gal, as well as Thomas Philippon and Jan De Loecker and his co-authors: they point out that new technologies were spreading ever more slowly from frontier firms to the rest, while the markups of the largest companies were rising; this may have limited the diffusion of technology and increased the incomes of the leaders. But these studies do not measure what share of the total technological gain went into buying competitors and patent disputes; a rise in share prices is not the same as expenditure of surplus at all. The following sections examine where exactly that surplus was flowing.

## Organization of Labor

Friday evening in any large city of the 2020s. Couriers race along sidewalks and bicycle lanes with enormous square backpacks—yellow, green, orange, the colors of different delivery services. Many have come from other countries or the provinces; many do not know the language in which the restaurant signs are written. Their boss is not a person, but an app on a phone: it assigns an order, maps a route, counts the minutes, gives a rating, and decides how much to pay. The app cannot be argued with; one cannot explain to it that the elevator was broken. If the rating falls, fewer orders arrive. Formally, the courier is not an employee but an independent contractor, a “partner” of the platform; there is no paid vacation, sick leave, or employer pension contribution because, legally, there is no employer.

This scene is the face of what the 2010s called the **gig economy** (from the English *gig*, a musician’s one-off performance): work sliced into individual tasks, each paid separately and allocated by an algorithm. Uber, founded in 2009, made this model widespread in taxis, followed by services for food delivery, cleaning, and small repairs. The phenomenon’s scale was often exaggerated: according to estimates by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics and researchers such as Lawrence Katz and Alan Krueger, platform work remained a sideline for most people, while the share for whom it was the main source of income ranged from one to several percent of workers in rich countries (Katz and Krueger’s first, higher estimates were later revised downward by the authors themselves). But in meaning, the shift was enormous. The reader will remember from Chapter 10 how the factory brought people under one roof and subjected them to the clock, and from Chapter 11 how Taylor broke work into timed motions. The platform carried both principles to their limit while abolishing the obligations that a century of trade-union struggle had attached to them. The overseer became invisible, and the contract one-sided.

For years, courts and parliaments could not decide what to call these people. In 2021, the United Kingdom’s Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers were workers entitled to minimum pay and holidays. In 2024, the European Union adopted a directive on platform work requiring a presumption that a person effectively managed by an algorithm is an employee unless the platform proves otherwise. In California, by contrast, companies secured a referendum measure in 2020 preserving drivers’ status as contractors—after spending, according to press reports, more than $200 million on the campaign, a record sum for such a ballot. The dispute was not about technology, but about the same question that runs through this book: who receives the surplus created by a new organization of work—those who perform it, those who own the app, or the buyer in the form of cheap delivery?

The same “algorithmic boss” arrived in workplaces where people had ordinary employment contracts. In the vast warehouses of online retailers, a scanner in the worker’s hand measured the time for every operation; journalists and trade unions reported systems that automatically flagged pauses deemed too long. This was not a new idea, but Taylor with a computer instead of a stopwatch. There was, however, an important difference: where the old methods engineer saw the workshop, the program saw every person all the time. Economists call such a situation **information asymmetry**—when one party to a transaction knows incomparably more about the other than the other knows in return. The stronger it is, the easier it becomes for the better-informed party to take the gains from greater efficiency.

The second great story in the organization of labor during these years was the pandemic. In the spring of 2020, within a few weeks, something futurists had discussed for thirty years came to pass: a huge share of office workers began working from home. Economists Jose Maria Barrero, Nicholas Bloom, and Steven Davis, who regularly surveyed Americans beginning in 2020, estimated that at the height of the lockdown more than half of all paid working days were performed at home; after the pandemic, the share did not return to the previous few percent, but settled at around one quarter. Video calls, cloud documents, and workplace chats had all existed before, but it took a shock for organizations to adapt. For many people this was liberation: hours in traffic and commuter trains disappeared, combining work and children became easier, and people began moving from expensive capitals to smaller cities. But remote work sharply divided society. A programmer or accountant could work from the kitchen; a cashier, nurse, bus driver, or warehouse picker could not. They were the people called “essential workers” and applauded from balconies—and they were the ones at risk of infection. The pandemic showed whose labor truly sustained material life, and it did not always coincide with who was paid the most.

When the economy reopened, something unexpected happened. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the number of Americans voluntarily leaving their jobs each month in 2021–2022 reached the highest levels in the history of the series, which began at the end of 2000—more than four million people a month. The phenomenon was called the “Great Resignation.” In part it was simply reshuffling: people changed jobs for better pay because workers were scarce. But surveys also revealed fatigue with work itself. In China in 2021, the phrase *tang ping*—“lying flat”—spread across the Internet: young people rejected the race for a career under the exhausting “996” schedule, from nine in the morning to nine at night, six days a week, of which technology companies boasted. The authorities disapproved of the sentiment, but its very appearance in a country where hundreds of millions had escaped poverty within a single generation said a great deal: rising income was no longer regarded as sufficient reward for a life surrendered entirely to work.

Here we reach the era’s most uncomfortable question. In 2013, the American anthropologist David Graeber published an essay in a small magazine about what he called **“meaningless work”** (the original expression was considerably cruder). His argument was that enormous numbers of people in rich countries were occupied with work they themselves considered useless—filling in reports no one read, holding meetings about meetings, servicing internal bureaucracy, persuading people to buy things they did not need, or defending one company from another doing exactly the same thing. The essay resonated so strongly that Graeber expanded it into a book in 2018. Soon after the essay appeared, the British polling organization YouGov asked people in the country whether their work made a meaningful contribution to the world; according to its results, around one third answered “no,” while another share found it difficult to answer.

Graeber’s thesis deserves to be stated fairly—together with the objections. Critics pointed out that a feeling of meaninglessness is not the same thing as meaninglessness: an accountant tired of reports may not see that those reports protect the company from fraud. Researchers Magdalena Soffia, Alex Wood, and Brendan Burchell tested the thesis against large European surveys and found that the share of people who regarded their work as useless was markedly smaller than Graeber claimed and was not growing over time; moreover, such judgments most often came not from office clerks but from people with bad bosses and degrading conditions, including cleaners and sales workers. This is an important correction. But the core observation remains: in an economy where machines produce ever more goods, an ever greater share of human labor goes not into creating benefits, but into distributing and recording them, protecting them from competitors, and fighting for other people’s money. This is what the framework of this book calls **deadweight**: resources are spent, while productivity and well-being do not rise. We will return to it in the relevant section.

Over all this hung the question first posed in Chapter 10: what happens to work when machines learn to perform it? In 2013, Oxford researchers Carl Frey and Michael Osborne estimated that almost half of US jobs belonged to occupations at high risk of automation in the coming decades. The figure was cited thousands of times, though later OECD estimates by Ljubica Nedelkoska and Glenda Quintini, which accounted for the fact that individual tasks rather than whole occupations are automated, produced much smaller shares—around one seventh of jobs at high risk and another third facing substantial change. The reality of the 2010s proved more complicated than either forecast. Unemployment in rich countries was low before the pandemic; there was plenty of work. But research by Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo showed that employment and wages fell significantly in American areas where factories adopted industrial robots more intensively, while David Autor and the British economists Maarten Goos and Alan Manning described a “hollowing out of the middle”: well-paid routine jobs—machine operators, clerical staff, assemblers—disappeared, while jobs grew at the two poles, high-paid work for specialists and low-paid service work where machines could not yet cope with the unpredictable physical world. The courier with the square backpack is a product of precisely this hollowing out.

Generative AI shifted the boundary again in the 2020s—this time toward the very intellectual work thought to be protected. Early studies of adoption, such as Erik Brynjolfsson and his colleagues’ work on customer support, showed a curious picture: an assistant program raised the output of novices and weaker workers most of all, narrowing the gap between them and experienced staff. This could mean the democratization of expertise—or, conversely, the devaluation of experience for which people had previously been paid. In 2023, Hollywood screenwriters held a strike lasting almost five months, and one of their demands was to limit the use of AI in writing scripts. This was perhaps the first major labor conflict in which knowledge workers, like weavers two centuries earlier, argued not only about wages but about the machine itself. The resulting agreement did not prohibit the technology, but established that its gains could not be obtained at writers’ expense.

One final detail remains. In 1930, Keynes predicted that within one hundred years—around 2030—the standard of living in rich countries would rise four- to eightfold and people would work fifteen hours a week. The first half of the forecast almost came true: income per person rose by roughly as much as he expected. But according to the OECD, average annual working hours in rich countries declined very slowly after the 1980s and barely changed in some places. The surplus created by rising output in this era was scarcely converted into free time. It became new needs, more expensive housing for which people had to work just as much, and—partly—work that existed for its own sake. The next section will show where it went in monetary terms.

## Exchange and Money

To understand where the surplus went in this era, we must first understand what happened to money itself. Something occurred that none of the preceding chapters had seen: for more than a decade, money became almost free.

Recall how Chapter 16 ended. In the autumn of 2008, banks ceased to trust one another, credit froze, and central banks—the institutions that issue money in each country and guard its value—cut their interest rates almost to zero. Usually this is enough: when borrowing is cheap, firms take loans for new factories and families for homes, and the economy revives. But in 2009, rates were already at zero and the economy was not reviving. The US Federal Reserve, the Bank of England, and later the Bank of Japan and the European Central Bank then deployed an instrument called **quantitative easing**. It works like this: the central bank creates new money—not by printing banknotes, but simply by entering it into accounts—and uses that money to buy bonds from banks and investors, meaning the IOUs of governments and large companies. The bond sellers receive fresh money in exchange and have to invest it somewhere. The expectation was that this money would flow into lending and investment, while rates on long-term loans would fall along with short-term rates.

The scale was unprecedented. According to the Federal Reserve itself, before the 2008 crisis its balance sheet—the name for everything it owns—held less than one trillion dollars in assets. By 2014 it held around $4.5 trillion, and after its pandemic purchases, by 2022, around $9 trillion. In 2014, the European Central Bank made its deposit rate negative for the first time in its history and kept it below zero for eight years; the Bank of Japan introduced a negative rate in 2016 and abandoned it only in 2024. According to calculations by Bloomberg, almost eighteen trillion dollars’ worth of bonds with negative yields circulated worldwide at the end of 2020: investors were effectively paying the governments of Germany, Switzerland, and Japan for the right to lend them money. Any lender from Chapter 3, any Genoese banker from Chapter 8, would have regarded this as madness.

It was not madness—the central banks had serious reasons. Most economists still believe that without them, 2009 and 2020 would have ended in another Great Depression: the lesson of the 1930s, examined in Chapter 12, was that allowing the monetary system to collapse is the worst possible mistake. In this sense, cheap money was an antidote to destruction. But the medicine had a side effect, and that is what matters for the question of this book.

Central banks bought bonds in financial markets, and this affected asset prices, the cost of credit, employment, and output. Who ultimately received the gains cannot be determined solely from who sold the bonds first. As early as the eighteenth century, the banker Richard Cantillon, an Irishman working in Paris, observed that new money never spreads evenly through an economy: whoever receives it first buys at old prices, while those it reaches last face new prices. Economists call this the **Cantillon effect**. During the 2010s, shares and housing in several countries rose in price faster than the consumer basket. But this comparison does not prove that the order in which new money was received determined the distribution of gains. According to market data, the S&P 500 index of the largest American companies rose roughly sevenfold from its low point in the spring of 2009 to the end of 2021. Wages grew incomparably less over the same period.

Hence the apartments costing fifteen years’ salary discussed at the beginning of the chapter. When a mortgage costs one or two percent a year, a buyer can offer much more for the same apartment, and the seller receives that price. According to the OECD, real house prices rose markedly faster than incomes in most rich countries during the 2010s—especially in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and the major cities of Europe. The supply of new housing did not grow much faster: land in a desirable location cannot reproduce, while building rules slowed construction. Here lies the era’s central mechanism, and it should be stated plainly: a substantial share of the cheap money intended for new factories went instead into raising the prices of things that already existed. The owner of a London apartment grew richer while sleeping; the young Londoner who wanted to buy it grew poorer despite working more. Surplus flowed from those who worked and rented housing to those who owned the land beneath it—a story familiar from the systems of rent discussed in Chapter 7.

This is not the only interpretation, and it should be weighed against the strongest objections. ECB economists Michele Lenza and Jiri Slacalek, as well as Ben Bernanke himself, have shown that by saving jobs, bond purchases ultimately narrowed the income gap between rich and poor, while the resulting increase in wealth inequality was moderate. Others—Lawrence Summers, and Bank of England economists Lukasz Rachel and Thomas Smith—reminded us that interest rates had been falling for decades before the crisis: aging populations saved more while firms needed less investment, so cheap money was not only a choice by central banks, but also a symptom. Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko showed that apartments rose most in price where regulations obstructed construction. But for the question of this book, the overall result matters: under any of these explanations, the era’s surplus settled into the price of what already existed.

Companies also found uses for the money, and not always the ones expected. Alongside capital investment, many large American corporations directed funds to **buybacks** of their own shares: fewer shares remain outstanding, but the effect of a buyback on the price and on the company’s investment depends on the circumstances. According to S&P Dow Jones Indices, companies in the S&P 500 spent about $900 billion on buybacks in 2022—a record. Defenders of the practice rightly reply that a buyback is simply a way to return money to shareholders, who can invest it in younger and more promising firms; it is not necessarily waste. The total value of buybacks alone does not show how many profitable investments companies passed up: Federal Reserve research has found no consistent relationship across countries between rising buybacks and falling capital expenditure.

There was also a quieter side effect. In several reports, the Bank for International Settlements—the “central bank of central banks” in Basel, Switzerland—noted that prolonged ultra-low rates in rich countries were accompanied by a growing share of so-called **zombie firms**: companies that for years fail to earn even enough to cover the interest on their debts, but remain alive because they are refinanced again and again. BIS economists Ryan Banerjee and Boris Hofmann calculated that in fourteen rich countries the share of such firms rose from about two percent in the late 1980s to twelve percent in 2016. Zombies hold workers and capital that could move to more productive competitors. Economists dispute how large this effect is and whether it explains the sluggish productivity discussed in the section “Energy and Technology”; Federal Reserve researchers, for example, found few zombies in the United States. But the mechanism is clear: when money costs almost nothing, the market becomes worse at distinguishing the living from the dead.

Against this background arose the strangest money in history. In 2008, a person or group under the name Satoshi Nakamoto published a description of **Bitcoin**—a digital currency issued by no government and no bank. Records of every payment are stored simultaneously on thousands of computers around the world, while new coins go to whoever spends the most computing power solving a special mathematical problem (this is called “mining”). The idea was a direct response to 2008: money that cannot be printed and that no banker can control. According to services that track them, the total value of bitcoins and thousands of imitating “cryptocurrencies” approached three trillion dollars at its peak at the end of 2021.

Bitcoin saw limited adoption in ordinary mass payments: substantial exchange-rate fluctuations make it inconvenient for buying inexpensive goods. Instead, it became an object of speculation—fueled in large part by the same cheap money. And “mining” became an energy race: according to estimates by the Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance, Bitcoin mining consumed roughly as much electricity as an entire medium-sized country. Mining verifies records and protects the network; it is a service for its users. The question is whether its benefit is proportionate to the energy spent and whether the same protection can be provided less expensively. Energy consumption alone does not reveal the “deadweight” share. Tellingly, the second-largest cryptocurrency, Ethereum, switched in 2022 to a method of validating records without mining and, according to its developers, reduced energy consumption by more than 99.9 percent. In November 2022, FTX, one of the world’s largest cryptocurrency exchanges, collapsed; its founder Sam Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud and sentenced in 2024 to twenty-five years in prison. In fairness, one must add that banks and governments are seriously studying distributed-ledger technology, while for residents of countries with ruined currencies—Venezuela, Lebanon, and Argentina—cryptocurrency has at times been one of the few ways to preserve anything at all. But the history of the 2010s mostly confirmed the lesson of Chapter 9: trust in money is created not by mathematics alone, but by institutions accountable for their promises.

Far more important for ordinary people was a revolution that rarely made the front pages—the payments revolution. We have already seen Kenya’s M-Pesa. By the end of the 2010s in China, almost any purchase, from a car to a pastry from a street vendor, was paid for by showing a QR code on a phone in the Alipay or WeChat apps. In 2016, India launched the public instant-payment system UPI, and according to its operator, by the middle of the 2020s it processed more than ten billion transactions a month—more than any other system of its kind in the world. Brazil’s central bank launched the Pix system in 2020, and within a couple of years it was used by most of the country’s adult population. A money transfer that had required a visit to a bank and a fee as recently as the 2000s became instant and almost free. By the measures of this book, this was genuine development: a sharp reduction in the costs of exchange, with the poor benefiting most—street vendors, migrants sending money home, and rural families with no nearby bank branch. Curiously, India and Brazil achieved this because the state built a common “road” for payments and opened it to everyone, rather than leaving the market to two or three private companies taking a cut from every purchase.

Then cheap money ended. When the inflation discussed at the beginning of the chapter broke loose in 2021–2022, central banks reversed course. From March 2022 to July 2023, the Federal Reserve raised its rate from almost zero to 5.25–5.5 percent—the fastest increase in four decades. The European Central Bank, the Bank of England, and most others did the same. Debate over the causes of that inflation has not subsided: some economists chiefly blame supply disruptions and the surge in energy prices after the war began, while others blame generous government payments and the prolonged maintenance of zero rates; most believe both played a part. But the consequences of the abrupt reversal were plain to see. In March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank in California, one of the twenty largest banks in the United States, collapsed within two days: it had invested technology companies’ deposits in long-term bonds bought when interest rates were at zero, and when rates rose those bonds lost value. After depositors learned this, regulators reported that in a single day they tried to withdraw more than forty billion dollars—the first bank run of the smartphone age, when the queue outside the bank formed in messaging apps. The authorities guaranteed every deposit, including amounts above the insured limit, and the question from Chapter 16 arose again: if the state always insures, who bears responsibility for risk?

The era’s final monetary detail concerns trust between countries. After February 2022, Western countries froze a substantial share of the Russian central bank’s foreign-exchange reserves—an estimated $300 billion held in European and American institutions. This was an unprecedented step: the reserves of a major country’s central bank had been regarded as the most untouchable money in the world. Supporters called it a lawful measure against an aggressor. But other governments drew their own conclusion: money held in a foreign currency is ultimately permission that can be revoked. According to the World Gold Council, central banks bought gold at record rates in 2022–2024—more than one thousand tonnes a year. The metal described in Chapter 8 as a mercantilist delusion returned as insurance against politics.

Let us sum up the era’s monetary history. Land, homes, and shares rose in price alongside cheap money. But revaluing an existing asset changes the valuation of wealth; it does not show what share of produced surplus was spent. Monetary policy affects asset prices and the distribution of wealth, but the magnitude and aggregate effect remain subjects of research. According to Federal Reserve data on the distribution of wealth, by the early 2020s the richest one percent of American families held almost one third of all wealth in the country, while the bottom half held a few percent. In *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, Thomas Piketty described precisely such a mechanism: when returns to ownership exceed the growth rate of the economy as a whole, wealth concentrates by itself. His conclusions were challenged—for his methods and because a substantial share of the wealth at the top was created by new companies rather than inherited; Matthew Rognlie showed that almost all the increase in capital’s share of income came from housing. But changes in wealth concentration cannot be inferred from changes in asset prices alone without accounting for employment, income, and new investment. Who decided where to direct these flows, and how, is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

London, July 26, 2012. European Central Bank president Mario Draghi speaks at an investment conference. The euro area is cracking: Greece is on the verge of leaving the common currency, Spain and Italy are borrowing at rates that would bankrupt them within a few years, and investors are betting on the euro’s collapse. Draghi says the ECB is ready to do “whatever it takes” to preserve the euro—and adds that it will be enough. Nothing more. Six weeks later, the ECB formalized the promise in a program to buy the bonds of troubled countries—and in all the years that followed, it did not purchase a single bond under the program. The promise alone was enough: interest rates on Italian and Spanish debt fell over the following months, and the acute phase of the crisis ended. A few words from one unelected official accomplished what years of negotiations among elected governments had not.

This scene is the key to the question “who decided?” in 2010–2026. The reader will remember from Chapter 9 how, after the Glorious Revolution, the English Parliament took control of public debt and thereby made the Crown’s promises credible; and from Chapter 15 how central banks in many countries were made **independent** in the 1980s and 1990s—that is, monetary decisions were removed from government control so politicians could not print money before elections. This remedy for inflation worked. But in the 2010s, its reverse side became apparent. Central banks, created to guard the value of money, became the chief stewards of the surplus: their decisions—to keep rates near zero and buy trillions in bonds—largely determined who grew richer and who did not. As we saw in the previous section, these decisions raised the prices of homes and shares most strongly. Yet no one could vote for or against them. Economist Paul Tucker, who spent more than thirty years at the Bank of England, the last several as deputy governor, posed the question honestly in *Unelected Power* (2018): how far can decisions be delegated to technocrats without undermining the foundations of democracy? Even he has no final answer. A technocrat is protected from the temptation to hand out money before elections—but also from accountability to those affected by his decisions.

The same euro crisis showed how decisions were distributed among countries. Between 2010 and 2015, Greece received several rescue loan packages from other euro-area countries and the IMF in exchange for severe **austerity**—spending cuts and tax increases. According to Eurostat, by 2013 Greece’s economy was roughly one quarter smaller than before the crisis, while unemployment exceeded one quarter of the workforce and half among young people. The decline was comparable to the American Great Depression described in Chapter 12. Debate over it continues. Some economists argue that Greece, which had lived on debt for years and concealed its true budget deficit, had no alternative, and that the creditors’ conditions merely compelled long-overdue reforms. Others—including IMF economists Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh in a 2013 paper and the Fund’s Independent Evaluation Office in a 2016 report—acknowledged that the damage from austerity had been underestimated and the debt should have been restructured earlier. Chapter 3 is relevant here: Mesopotamian kings periodically canceled debts because they understood that unbearable debt destroys society. In the twenty-first century, no such mechanism existed for countries within a currency union, and the price of its absence was an entire nation’s lost decade.

The era’s second center of power was private. In Chapter 11, we saw how Rockefeller’s Standard Oil became so powerful that antitrust laws were required, and how the US Supreme Court split it into pieces in 1911. A century later, a similar question arose about the technology giants. By the early 2020s, five American companies—Apple, Microsoft, Alphabet (Google), Amazon, and Meta (Facebook)—together were worth more on the stock market than the annual GDP of any country except the United States and China. Their power was unusual. They did not so much raise prices like a classical monopolist—many of their services were free—as control **bottlenecks**: the app store through which alone one could reach a phone; the search engine through which people found everything else; the marketplace where sellers competed, including with the marketplace’s own owner. Chapter 16 showed where such power comes from: network effects make the largest platform larger still. Whoever owns such a bottleneck can charge everyone who passes through it—rent in the precise sense of the word, like the bridge toll in Chapter 7. Apple and Google, for example, charged as much as thirty percent on in-app payments for years, and bypassing them was almost impossible.

Governments responded slowly and in different ways. Between 2017 and 2019, the European Commission fined Google three times, for a total of more than eight billion euros, for abusing its dominant position; in 2022, the EU adopted the **Digital Markets Act**, which prohibits the largest platforms—called “gatekeepers”—from giving privileged treatment to their own services. In the United States, a federal judge ruled in August 2024 that Google illegally maintained a monopoly in Internet search—the first decision of such scale against a technology company since the Microsoft case at the turn of the century. In fairness, the counterargument must also be stated: many economists noted that the giants invested more in research than any companies in history—according to their own accounts, tens of billions of dollars a year each—and that they financed a substantial share of the breakthrough in artificial intelligence. Monopoly here is two-sided: it both extracts rent and directs part of it into development. As with the temples of Chapter 3, the question is one of proportion—and of who determines it.

The third strand concerns how money became influence. According to OpenSecrets, a nonprofit organization that compiles lobbyists’ reports, expenditure on federal lobbying in the United States remained around three to four billion dollars a year during the 2010s and early 2020s—and that is only the officially disclosed portion. In the 2010 *Citizens United* case, the US Supreme Court removed limits on independent political spending by corporations and organizations, and outside spending in election campaigns multiplied. Economists call such a situation **regulatory capture**: the industry meant to be controlled largely determines the rules of its own control. The concept was introduced by Chicago economist George Stigler as early as 1971. This is not necessarily corruption in the criminal sense; more often it takes the form of endless consultations in which one side has hundreds of lawyers and the other none. Within the framework of this book, it is classic deadweight expenditure: enormous resources are spent redistributing what has already been created rather than creating anything new.

Perhaps the era’s most conspicuous shift was the return of the state as investor. After the 1980s, as Chapter 15 showed, the prevailing belief in rich countries was that government should not “pick winners”—decide which industries to support. During the 2010s and early 2020s, this belief weakened, and China was the first to reject it openly. The Made in China 2025 program, announced in 2015, explicitly named ten industries—from microchips to electric vehicles and robots—in which the country intended to become a leader through government loans, subsidies, and procurement. In some fields, as we saw in the section on energy, it succeeded beyond expectation; in others, such as the most advanced microchips, it did not. The United States responded with two laws in 2022: the **CHIPS and Science Act** (around fifty billion dollars for domestic microchip manufacturing and research) and the **Inflation Reduction Act**, which despite its name was principally a program of tax credits for clean energy and electric vehicles. Europe, Japan, South Korea, and India adopted programs of their own. A new subsidy race began. How much of it will foster development—new factories and new technology—and how much will be deadweight competition to lure the same factories away from neighbors with taxpayers’ money is not yet known. The experience of Chapter 14 suggests—at least according to Alice Amsden and Robert Wade—that the answer depends not on support itself, but on whether the state demands results: Korean subsidies in the 1960s through the 1980s were granted in exchange for export success and withdrawn from those who failed to deliver it; Latin American protective tariffs generally imposed no such condition. Skeptics, including the authors of the World Bank’s 1993 report on the “East Asian miracle,” nevertheless considered the role of such industrial support more modest.

Prohibitions returned alongside subsidies. In October 2022, the United States banned the sale to China of advanced microchips for artificial intelligence and the equipment used to make them, then secured similar restrictions from the Netherlands and Japan. Sanctions against Russia after 2022 disconnected its largest banks from the SWIFT international interbank messaging system, prohibited imports of many technologies, and banned exports of certain goods. The American tariffs of 2025, mentioned at the beginning of the chapter, marked the sharpest turn in a major country’s trade policy since the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of 1930 discussed in Chapter 12. Economic policy in the era was increasingly governed not by the question “how can we become richer?” but “how can we become safer?” Where the boundary lies between prudent insurance and deadweight expenditure on isolation is one of the central questions of the next decade.

Against the background of these enormous shifts, small experiments in *how* society makes decisions passed almost unnoticed. In 2021, around 140 countries agreed within the OECD on a **global minimum tax** of fifteen percent on the profits of large corporations: the goal was to end the “race to the bottom” in which countries competed to tax multinational companies least, while companies shifted profits into tax havens. Implementation was slow and uneven, and the United States withdrew from participation in 2025, but the attempt to agree on rules for the entire world was itself remarkable. In Ireland in 2016–2018, a **Citizens’ Assembly**—around one hundred citizens selected randomly, by lot—spent several months hearing experts and discussing the country’s most painful question, the ban on abortion; its recommendations became the basis of the 2018 referendum. Selection by lot was an Athenian idea from Chapter 5, returning after two and a half thousand years. In Taiwan, a group of civic programmers together with officials, among them the future digital affairs minister Audrey Tang, built online platforms where thousands of people discussed rules for new technologies and the software highlighted not majority positions, but proposals accepted by people with different views. In Estonia, almost every public service, from filing taxes to voting—roughly half of voters cast ballots online—moved onto the Internet, and according to the country’s government, most citizens could file a tax return in a matter of minutes. None of these experiments changed the world economy. But all tried to answer the question the era posed with particular urgency: how can decisions about distributing the surplus be made transparently and with the participation of those affected, rather than only in a technocrat’s office, a platform’s boardroom, or a lobbyist’s reception room? Skeptics rightly note that such forms work well in small countries and on individual issues, while how to scale them to decisions involving trillions remains unclear.

The section’s conclusion can be formulated as follows. Institutions built in the twentieth century to protect against earlier ills—independent central banks against inflation, free trade against the protectionism of the 1930s, privatization against an unwieldy state—continued to function in the 2010s, but answered the new questions ever less adequately. Some important decisions were made by central banks, the owners of digital platforms, and government agencies. But the degree of decision-making concentration and the trajectory of trust differed by country and institution; a general claim that trust fell “in most rich countries” requires separate comparable time series. And falling trust, as this book has shown since Chapter 4, is one of the surest predictors of rising exchange costs.

## What Fostered Development

Shanghai, January 11, 2020. Virologist Zhang Yongzhen’s group at the Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center has finished decoding the genome of the new coronavirus causing a strange pneumonia in Wuhan. Through a colleague, the Australian biologist Edward Holmes, the sequence is posted on an open scientific website—free for everyone, without patents, and without waiting for official permission (press reports say that Zhang’s laboratory was closed for “rectification” the next day). Two days later, according to its own account, the American company Moderna has already designed a future vaccine on a computer. The first vaccinations are given less than a year later. Researchers at Imperial College London estimated in *The Lancet Infectious Diseases* that this chain prevented between fourteen and twenty million deaths in the first year of vaccination alone, depending on whether officially recorded COVID-19 deaths or all excess mortality is counted. It began when knowledge was given away for free.

This scene is the best starting point for discussing what genuinely made the world richer in 2010–2026. The preceding sections have spoken mainly about how surplus flowed into asset prices and bottlenecks. But it would be dishonest to stop there: the era also produced an enormous amount of development, often in places overlooked by both stock-market reports and newspaper headlines.

**First, poverty continued to retreat.** According to World Bank estimates, the share of people living in extreme poverty—on less than $2.15 a day at 2017 prices adjusted for differences in purchasing power among countries; beginning in 2025, the World Bank uses a three-dollar line, under which poverty levels are higher but the direction is the same—fell from around one sixth of humanity in 2010 to less than one tenth by 2019. That means hundreds of millions of people—in India, Bangladesh, Vietnam, and Ethiopia—who for the first time could eat enough every day. According to the UN Inter-agency Group, global child mortality fell by roughly half between 2000 and 2022. The UN estimated that average life expectancy worldwide approached seventy-three years before the pandemic. The pandemic reversed this progress: according to the same World Bank, around seventy million people fell back into extreme poverty in 2020—the first increase in a quarter-century—while average life expectancy fell by almost two years in 2020–2021. But by the middle of the 2020s it had recovered. Compared with any previous chapter in this book, this continued the fastest retreat of poverty and early death in all of history, a retreat that began in the 1990s—although it slowed by the end of the 2010s. Another point matters too: most researchers believe the main contribution came from growth within poor economies themselves as they gained access to fertilizer, communications, and markets; aid from rich countries played an important role above all in health—in vaccines, malaria nets, and HIV medicines—although economists such as Jeffrey Sachs and William Easterly still dispute its overall return.

**Second, knowledge became more widely shared than ever.** The section on energy and technology discussed expensive bottlenecks such as advanced microchips. But an opposite economy—the economy of openness—grew alongside them. Wikipedia, founded in 2001 and written by unpaid volunteers, contained tens of millions of articles in hundreds of languages by the 2020s and became one of the world’s most visited websites. Almost the entire Internet rested on **open-source software**—programs whose source code anyone can read, use, and improve: systems in the Linux family ran most of the world’s servers and, through Android, most smartphones; the Python programming language, also open and free, became the principal tool of data researchers and artificial-intelligence developers. Hundreds of thousands of people around the world wrote this software—some paid by companies, some volunteering and choosing their own tasks. As early as 2006, legal scholar Yochai Benkler called this **commons-based peer production** in *The Wealth of Networks*: people decide for themselves what to work on, and the result belongs to everyone. Economic theory long struggled to explain why this worked at all; in the 2010s, it simply worked, and at a scale that rivaled the largest corporations. The corporations themselves, it should be noted, became the largest users and sponsors of this common resource—without it, their own products would have cost incomparably more.

The same logic spread to science. Preprint services—open preliminary versions of scientific papers—went from a custom among physicists to the main channel of scientific communication: during the pandemic, medical researchers posted results within days rather than the months required for journal peer review. Speed came at a price—weak studies entered the stream as well—but the benefit plainly outweighed it. Free educational platforms such as Khan Academy gave a village schoolchild access to explanations unavailable to a student in the capital a generation earlier. By the middle of the 2020s, even powerful artificial-intelligence models had become open: in 2023, Meta released the Llama family of models openly, though with license restrictions, and in January 2025 the Chinese company DeepSeek published a model comparable to the best closed systems on a number of tests—and the stock market briefly doubted that the AI bottleneck was so insurmountable after all. By the book’s second measure—the accumulation and transmission of knowledge—the era performed more strongly than any since the invention of printing.

**Third, public “roads” for exchange.** In the section “Exchange and Money,” we saw India’s UPI and Brazil’s Pix. They were part of a broader phenomenon called **digital public infrastructure** in India: the state builds basic systems—identity, payments, document exchange—as a common road open to all companies and citizens. According to its operator, India’s Aadhaar digital identity system covered more than 1.3 billion people by the 2020s; it allowed hundreds of millions of poor people to open a bank account for the first time and receive government payments directly, bypassing intermediaries who had previously taken a substantial share along the way. The system was not flawless—critics pointed to failures that left people without the food to which they were entitled and to the threat of surveillance—and disputes over it reached India’s Supreme Court. But the economic effect was clear: the cost of obtaining the most basic goods fell for those at the very bottom. According to the mobile operators’ association GSMA, by the early 2020s the world had more than 1.5 billion registered mobile-money accounts, most of them in sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. For a rural family in Kenya, this meant the same thing as a bill of exchange meant for the medieval merchant in Chapter 7: money no longer had to be carried along a dangerous road.

**Fourth, investment in the learning curve.** The decline in the cost of solar energy discussed above did not happen by itself. In 2000, Germany introduced guaranteed feed-in tariffs for solar and wind electricity, and for many years German consumers paid through a surcharge on their bills for panels that were still very expensive. It looked wasteful, and many economists, including Manuel Frondel and his colleagues at the RWI economic research institute, assessed it that way: the same money could have reduced emissions far more cheaply. But as technology-policy scholar Gregory Nemet showed, this money created demand that allowed Chinese factories—with generous support from their own government—to expand output, and output, by the law of the learning curve, drove down the price. In the end, the main beneficiary was not Germany but the whole world—including poor countries that obtained cheap electricity without coal-fired power stations. According to the World Bank, by the end of the 2010s several million rural homes in Bangladesh had their own solar installations—the first electricity in their history. This is a rare example in the book of one country investing surplus in development while everyone received the benefits. It is also two-sided: German panel production eventually lost to Chinese competition, and German families paid some of Europe’s highest electricity prices for years.

**Fifth, fundamental science and society assuming the risk.** The stories of mRNA vaccines and AlphaFold were already told in the section on energy and technology; what matters here is their common denominator. Both grew out of research funded for decades by public foundations and universities without any guarantee of a return—and AlphaFold was also trained on an open international database of protein structures that scientists had been building since 1971. During the pandemic, this was joined by what might be called de-risking: the governments of the United States, Europe, and other countries guaranteed vaccine purchases in advance, before knowing whether they would work, allowing companies to build factories at the same time as they conducted trials. A vaccine’s path, previously stretching over years, was shortened to months not because anyone cut corners on safety testing, but because stages proceeded in parallel and society assumed the financial risk. The counterargument must be stated too: the state assumed the risk, while profits amounting to tens of billions of dollars went mainly to the shareholders of a few companies, and poor countries received vaccines substantially later than rich ones.

Taken together, these five strands reveal a common pattern familiar from earlier chapters. What fostered development in this era was above all what *expanded access*: knowledge placed in the commons; payment roads open to everyone; cheap energy that reached the village; science funded without a demand for immediate profit. Very often this development was created not where money was concentrated, but where people voluntarily devoted time to a common cause or where society deliberately assumed risks the market was unwilling to take. In this part of the era, the answer to the question “where did the surplus go?” is encouraging: where it was placed in common use, it returned many times over. But the same years, as the next section will show, also knew the reverse—the destruction of accumulated people, capital, and trust.

## What Destroyed

Kakhovka, the morning of June 6, 2023. The Kakhovka Hydroelectric Power Plant dam on the Dnipro, built in the 1950s as one of the great construction projects of the Soviet era discussed in Chapter 13, is destroyed by an explosion. The water of the enormous reservoir—almost eighteen cubic kilometers—rushes downstream. Dozens of villages and part of Kherson are flooded; people take refuge on rooftops, while boats carry away the elderly and dogs. Within weeks, a cracked, muddy bed lies where the reservoir once stood, and the irrigation canals of southern Ukraine that supplied hundreds of thousands of hectares of fields run dry. At the time this book was written, no official international investigation into who destroyed the dam or how had been completed, but independent researchers—seismologists at Norway’s NORSAR center, who recorded an explosion, and engineers who advised journalists at *The New York Times*—concluded that a charge had detonated inside the dam, in an area then controlled by Russian forces; the Russian authorities blame Ukraine. Seventy years of accumulated labor—turbines, canals, fields, and fish farms—vanished within hours.

This scene is the clearest illustration of what the book’s framework calls **destructive**: not merely expenditure, but the destruction of what had already been created. The years 2010–2026 contained more such scenes than people raised in the 1990s, believing that major wars belonged to the past, had hoped.

**First and foremost, war.** Russia’s full-scale war against Ukraine became the largest war in Europe since 1945. Its human losses have not yet been fully counted and remain disputed: by mid-2025, military casualties exceeded one million killed and wounded on both sides according to estimates by Western intelligence services and researchers such as the Washington-based Center for Strategic and International Studies, with the dead numbering in the hundreds of thousands; neither side publishes official data, while the UN Human Rights Office, which counts only individually confirmed civilian deaths, had recorded more than twelve thousand by 2025 and cautioned that the true number was substantially higher. According to the UN Refugee Agency, around six million Ukrainians were abroad and several million more had left their homes within the country. According to Ukraine’s statistical service, its GDP contracted by almost one third in 2022. At the beginning of 2025, a joint assessment by the World Bank, the Ukrainian government, the European Union, and the UN put the country’s reconstruction needs at more than half a trillion dollars—roughly two and a half times Ukraine’s annual prewar economy.

War destroyed wealth not only where shells landed. According to official statistics, the Russian economy, placed on a war footing, grew for a time—factories worked three shifts and wages in defense plants rose. The reader of Chapter 12 knows how to read such growth: a tank that burns a month after production counts toward GDP just like a tractor that will plow for twenty years. Another form of damage went deeper. After February, and especially after mobilization was announced in September 2022, hundreds of thousands of people left Russia by demographers’ estimates, with programmers, scientists, engineers, and entrepreneurs disproportionately represented among them. Chapter 9 showed how the expulsion of the Huguenots from France in 1685 enriched Prussia, Holland, and England; Chapter 12 showed how the flight of scientists from Nazi Germany helped American science become the world’s foremost. The loss of specialists is one of the costliest forms of destruction because knowledge leaves with people and returns, if it returns at all, only after a generation.

The war in Ukraine was not the only one. According to the UN Refugee Agency, the Syrian civil war, which began in 2011, forced more than half the country’s prewar population from their homes; around six million people ended up abroad. Aleppo, one of the world’s oldest continuously inhabited cities and a hub of the caravan routes discussed in Chapter 6, was largely destroyed. The war in Yemen caused one of the decade’s gravest humanitarian crises, according to the UN. The war in Sudan, which began in April 2023, forced more than ten million people from their homes according to the International Organization for Migration—the world’s largest displacement crisis. The war in Gaza, which began after Hamas attacked Israel on October 7, 2023, destroyed or damaged most buildings in the territory according to UN estimates. Each of these wars destroyed the same things as the wars in every preceding chapter: people, housing, roads, schools—and what takes longest to rebuild, trust between neighbors.

**Second, the pandemic as the destruction of people and skills.** We have already cited the WHO estimate: around fifteen million excess deaths in 2020–2021. But the pandemic also caused less visible damage spread over time. Schools in different countries were closed for periods ranging from several weeks to one and a half or two years. Together with UNESCO and UNICEF, the World Bank estimated that the share of ten-year-old children in poor and middle-income countries unable to read and understand a simple text—a condition called **“learning poverty”**—rose from around one half before the pandemic to roughly seven tenths. The same organizations calculated that if the loss is not recovered, today’s generation of schoolchildren could forgo lifetime earnings on the order of seventeen trillion dollars, or twenty-one trillion in a revised 2022 estimate. The figure is hypothetical, like any estimate of this kind, but the direction is clear: by the book’s second measure—the accumulation and transmission of knowledge—school closures were among the era’s costliest losses. In fairness, during the first months, when almost nothing was known about the virus, closing schools was caution rather than folly; the dispute concerned whether many countries kept them closed too long. And those without a computer, a quiet room, or parents able to help suffered most—that is, the losses fell on the poor.

**Third, the destruction of money and states from within.** Venezuela, the country with the world’s largest proven oil reserves, experienced something in the 2010s that rarely occurs in peacetime. When oil prices fell in 2014, it became clear that for years the government had spent its oil rent on handouts and control rather than production: nationalized enterprises decayed, price controls emptied shelves, and the budget deficit was covered by the printing press. The IMF estimated that annual inflation exceeded one million percent in 2018; Venezuela’s central bank, publishing data for the first time in three years, gave a figure around ten times smaller—about 130,000 percent—which still meant prices almost doubling every month; by various estimates, the economy had contracted by around three quarters by the early 2020s. According to the UN inter-agency platform R4V, more than seven million people—around one quarter of the population—had left the country by the middle of the 2020s. The government blamed everything on American sanctions imposed in 2017–2019, and some economists, including Mark Weisbrot and Jeffrey Sachs, believe they sharply deepened the contraction; but oil extraction and production had begun collapsing before the sanctions struck the country’s finances. This is a story familiar from third-century Rome in Chapter 5 and Germany in 1923 in Chapter 12, but without a major war and in a resource-rich country—a classic **resource curse** like the Spanish silver of Chapter 8. In Lebanon, a banking system that had paid depositors high interest for years using new deposits collapsed in 2019; the World Bank called the crisis one of the world’s worst since the middle of the nineteenth century. In August 2020, the explosion of almost three thousand tonnes of ammonium nitrate stored without supervision for years in the port of Beirut killed more than two hundred people and destroyed entire neighborhoods—almost a symbol of a state in which everyone divided the rent while no one watched the warehouse.

**Fourth, the climate began presenting its bill.** In every preceding chapter, nature destroyed economies from time to time—through drought, crop failure, or epidemic. In this era, it became clear that the emissions accumulated over two centuries were turning rare shocks into frequent ones. The floods in Pakistan in the summer of 2022, when roughly one third of the country was under water by the government’s estimate, affected around thirty-three million people; a joint assessment by the government, the World Bank, the UN, and the EU put damage and losses at more than thirty billion dollars. Wildfires in Australia in 2019–2020, Canada in 2023, and Los Angeles in January 2025 destroyed thousands of homes. Insurers were the first to turn this into figures: in its annual reviews, the reinsurance company Swiss Re noted that insured losses from natural disasters consistently exceeded one hundred billion dollars a year during the 2020s. The insurers themselves, however, regard the principal cause of rising losses not as climate but as the fact that people are building more—and more expensively—in dangerous places: by the sea, in forests, and on floodplains; warming amplifies the increase. Attribution researchers, who compare real events with models of a world without warming, found, for example, that climate change made Pakistan’s 2022 rainfall substantially heavier, though the precise contribution is difficult to estimate. This is destruction of a special kind: its cause is the surplus of past eras burned as coal and oil, while the bill reaches even those who had almost no access to that surplus. Researchers estimate that Pakistan accounts for only a fraction of one percent of accumulated global emissions.

**Fifth, the destruction of health in the richest country.** Life expectancy in the United States stopped rising even before the pandemic. Economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton called the principal cause **“deaths of despair”**: deaths from overdoses, alcohol, and suicide, above all among middle-aged people without a university degree—the very people whose jobs were “hollowed out” in the section on the organization of labor. The opioid epidemic began with the aggressive promotion of painkillers that the pharmaceutical company Purdue Pharma advertised as rarely addictive; the company later pleaded guilty twice to federal crimes, and its owners became defendants in thousands of lawsuits. According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than one hundred thousand people a year died from drug overdoses in 2021–2022—more Americans than were killed in the entire Vietnam War. This destruction is particularly instructive for the book’s thesis: it grew where the profits from selling went to some and the costs fell on others, and where people lost not only income but also their place in the world.

**Sixth, the destruction of a shared understanding of the world.** This is the hardest damage to measure, but perhaps not the smallest. The platforms discussed in Chapter 16 and above earned money from attention, and fear and anger hold attention best. In a 2018 study of around 126,000 stories on Twitter, MIT researchers Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy, and Sinan Aral found that false news spread faster and more widely than true news. How responsible social networks are for rising polarization is disputed: Levi Boxell, Matthew Gentzkow, and Jesse Shapiro found that polarization grew fastest among older Americans, who use the Internet less than others, while polarization in the United States began rising long before smartphones; experiments in which people were paid to deactivate Facebook nevertheless found a small but real effect. But within the framework of this book, something else matters. Trust among strangers—the force that, beginning in Chapter 4, reduced the costs of exchange—is itself a form of capital. When citizens of one country cease to agree even on facts, this capital is depleted: reaching agreement on vaccines, taxes, or rules becomes more expensive. During the pandemic, this cost lives: a study by Jacob Wallace and co-authors found that after vaccines became available, excess mortality among Republican supporters in Ohio and Florida was substantially higher than among Democrats.

To sum up, some of the destruction described here could have been reduced by human decisions, although the degree of preventability differed across events. The dam did not have to be blown up, money did not have to be printed, the ammonium nitrate warehouse could have been guarded, schools in many places could have reopened sooner, and medicines did not have to be presented as safe. Some destruction was the price of power, some the price of rent that no one controlled, and some the price of the surplus of past centuries invested in energy without regard for its consequences. But something new also appeared in this era: destruction became instantly visible to the whole world—and continued all the same. Knowledge of harm is not the same as the ability to stop it. The next section concerns this gap, and expenditure that did not even destroy anything but simply burned resources to no purpose.

## What Was Deadweight Waste

Ordos, Inner Mongolia, the early 2010s. In the middle of the steppe stands the new city of Kangbashi—broad avenues, a museum shaped like a giant metal boulder, a library, an opera house, a stadium, and districts of high-rise apartment buildings. It was built with money from the coal mined nearby, and local-government plans anticipated hundreds of thousands of residents. Western journalists who visited in 2010 and 2011 described nearly empty streets where one could walk for a long time without meeting a single pedestrian and windows that remained dark at night. Kangbashi was nicknamed a “ghost city.” In fairness, the city partially filled over the following decade—government offices and schools were moved there—and Chinese authorities repeatedly pointed out that the Western press had rushed to judgment. But as a symbol it outlived that judgment because it proved to be only the opening image of a much larger story.

That story is Chinese real estate. After 2008, in rescuing its economy from the global crisis, China sharply increased housing and infrastructure construction; the share of the total surplus directed there is not determined here. Economists Kenneth Rogoff and Yuanchen Yang estimated that in 2021 real estate and the demand associated with it accounted for about one quarter of China’s economy, or roughly twenty-two percent excluding the imported component; this is an estimate of real estate and related demand as a share of GDP, not of empty apartments or deadweight expenditure. Some of this construction was genuine development: hundreds of millions of people moved from villages to cities and acquired apartments unlike anything their parents had known. Some homes were bought as stores of value and remained unoccupied for a time. No reliable overall estimate of the share of such homes or their later use is available here. When the authorities restricted developers’ borrowing in 2020, the pyramid began to crumble. The largest developer, Evergrande, with liabilities exceeding $300 billion, was ordered into liquidation by a Hong Kong court in January 2024; other giants then ran into trouble as well. By the measures of this book, it recalls both the pyramids of Chapter 3 and the Spanish silver of Chapter 8: the construction boom combined necessary housing and infrastructure with projects whose return depended on prices continuing to rise. The construction sector’s share of GDP does not reveal the share of unnecessary structures.

China is the largest-scale example, but not the only one. A pattern runs throughout the era: as the economy grew, so did some expenditures on competition for advantage. Their share of the total surplus is not measured here. Economists call such contests **zero-sum games**. Consider several.

**The race for thousandths of a second.** The Spread Networks cable through the Appalachians from Chapter 16—around $300 million for a few thousandths of a second—became obsolete within a few years: competitors built chains of microwave towers, and the signal traveled through the air faster still. In a 2015 paper, economists Eric Budish, Peter Cramton, and John Shim showed that such a race is almost a pure loss to society: the very design of exchange trading encourages endless investment in speed, while one firm’s gain is exactly the amount other market participants fail to receive. Prices do not become appreciably more accurate and no new goods appear. Defenders of high-frequency trading reply that robotic intermediaries made transactions cheaper for everyone—the debate was discussed in Chapter 16. But the speed race itself is the arms race of Chapter 11 in miniature, only with cables and towers instead of dreadnoughts.

**The race for attention.** According to estimates by the advertising group GroupM, worldwide advertising expenditure exceeded one trillion dollars a year for the first time in 2024, most of it online. Advertising is not entirely deadweight: it tells buyers about a new product, helps small firms find customers, and pays for free services used by billions. Some advertising serves to contest already existing customers, but its share of total expenditure has not been established: two producers of nearly identical soda spend billions to lure the same people away from each other, and if both simultaneously cut their spending in half, no one would lose anything. Jeff Hammerbacher’s bitter remark from Chapter 16—about the best minds of a generation thinking about how to make people click on advertisements—is a personal opinion, but it captures the point precisely: some of the labor of programmers and mathematicians went into the fight for attention; total advertising expenditure does not reveal its share among all uses of those skills.

**Work that serves itself.** In the section on the organization of labor, we promised to return to Graeber. The debate over how much work is “meaningless” remains unresolved, and the corrections offered by his critics must be remembered. But there are fields in which the deadweight portion can be measured almost directly. The best example is American health care. A team of researchers led by David Himmelstein and Steffie Woolhandler estimated in a 2020 publication that around one third of all US medical spending went into administration—billing, disputes between insurance companies and hospitals, verification of eligibility for treatment—roughly twice the share in neighboring Canada with its unified public payment system. These figures, produced by authors who openly advocate a single public system, are disputed: David Cutler and co-authors calculate a smaller administrative share, around one quarter of expenditure, but even by their estimate roughly a quarter of a trillion dollars a year could be saved simply by making the rules less complex. Some record-keeping is necessary, of course: without it, fraud cannot be caught and treatment quality cannot be checked. But few dispute the order of magnitude: hundreds of billions of dollars a year and an enormous army of workers engaged not in treatment, but in shifting costs from one organization to another. It is the same logic as lobbying, described in the section “Power and Institutions”: resources go into deciding *who* receives what has already been created.

**Prestige—an old acquaintance.** The pharaohs of Chapter 3 would recognize much in this era. In 2021, Saudi Arabia’s crown prince unveiled The Line, a proposed linear city 170 kilometers long between two mirrored walls in the desert, part of the NEOM megaproject estimated to cost hundreds of billions of dollars. According to business-press reports, by 2024 the scale of the first phase had already been sharply reduced and the deadlines postponed. In July of that same year, 2021, two billionaires, Richard Branson and Jeff Bezos, flew to the edge of space aboard their own companies’ spacecraft nine days apart; these flights were spectacles above all. A qualification in the spirit of the ambiguous cases in Chapter 0 is needed here, however: the same private space race produced SpaceX’s reusable rockets, which by common estimates reduced the cost of launching payloads into orbit severalfold—and that is a tool for science, communications, and climate observation. The market for NFT digital tokens—entries in a cryptographic ledger recording rights to digital objects—included prestige purchases and speculation; its total costs and benefits cannot be inferred from the prices of individual sales. In March 2021, a digital collage by the artist Beeple sold at Christie’s for almost $70 million; a year or two later, tracking services reported that trading volume in such tokens had fallen by nine tenths or more. The trading price of a token is itself a transfer of money between people, not an equivalent expenditure of energy or labor. Assessing the costs requires separate accounting of the network’s operation, creation of the work, and other real resources.

**Weapons.** Military expenditure is large in absolute terms: SIPRI estimated it at 2.5 percent of world GDP in 2024. According to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, global military spending reached around $2.7 trillion in 2024—a record for the entire period covered by its data—while the increase in a single year was the fastest since the end of the Cold War. In June 2025, NATO countries agreed to raise defense and defense-related spending to five percent of their GDP by 2035. Within the framework of this book, the arms race belongs to deadweight expenditure, and this should be explained honestly rather than asserted as a slogan. A country under attack cannot afford not to spend on defense—Ukraine proved that; deterring an aggressor may prevent destruction far more costly than any budget. But from the perspective of humanity as a whole, weapons in storage produce nothing, while weapons that are used destroy. Each side acts rationally, yet in the aggregate the world’s surplus burns in a contest where both participants, having invested twice as much, remain in the same position relative to each other. This is a trap, not stupidity—and it cannot be escaped alone, only by agreement, as occurred with arms-limitation treaties in the 1970s and 1980s.

Finally, a question the era has not yet answered. In 2025, according to their own reports, the largest American technology companies planned to invest a combined total of more than $300 billion in the construction of artificial-intelligence data centers in a single year—sums comparable to the largest investment waves in history. Will this foster development or prove deadweight? If AI accelerates science as AlphaFold did, the investment will repay itself many times over. If a substantial share of the capacity goes into several companies racing to do the same thing, into advertising, and into an endless stream of content designed to hold attention, the era will leave behind something like the nineteenth-century railway manias: some of the tracks will serve posterity, while many investors’ capital burns. Most likely, both will occur. The honest answer is that we do not yet know.

Compared side by side, these examples reveal one mechanism of the era’s deadweight expenditure. Neither China’s empty apartments, the cable through the Appalachians, nor the army of insurance administrators was the product of someone’s stupidity. Each decision was rational for the person making it: the developer built, the trader accelerated, the insurer disputed the bill, the country armed itself. It became deadweight only in aggregate, from society’s perspective—and almost no one was making decisions from that perspective. An open accounting would compare the resources spent on such races with the potential return from other projects; their results are unknowable in advance. And so the era of the largest surplus in history gave people neither Keynes’s fifteen-hour workweek nor a sense of security: some resources went into such contests, but their share of the total surplus has not been determined.

## The Lesson of the Era

The years 2010–2026 form the first era in this book that cannot be described as lacking anything. It had enough energy: solar electricity became, in the words of the International Energy Agency, the cheapest in history. It had enough knowledge: almost everything humanity knew was openly accessible, and machines began helping to discover more. It had enough money: for ten years, it cost almost nothing. Yet by the middle of the 2020s, most people in rich countries felt less secure and more anxious than their parents. The answer to the question “where did the era’s surplus go?” explains this paradox better than any single figure, and can be reduced to several lessons.

**First lesson: a revaluation of wealth must not be mistaken for expenditure of surplus.** In earlier chapters, surplus either became something—canals, cathedrals, armies, factories—or was consumed. This era was especially notable for rising prices of already-existing assets. A central bank’s bond purchases could affect asset prices, employment, and output at the same time; their ultimate effect on inequality is disputed. An increase in an asset’s price is a revaluation of the stock of wealth, not a separate channel through which produced surplus is spent. This was no one’s conspiracy: as we have seen, central banks had good reasons, and most economists believe the world would have suffered another depression without them; debate continues over how much they increased inequality. But the lesson does not change. It is important to study separately how monetary policy and changes in asset prices affected different groups; the claim that the first recipient invariably takes the largest share has not been demonstrated here.

**Second lesson: what produced the most development was what expanded access—and often where society assumed the risk.** The viral genome placed in the open; Wikipedia and Linux, written by people who chose their own tasks; Indian and Brazilian payment “roads” open to all; German electricity surcharges that drove down the price of solar panels for the entire world; decades of grants without which there would have been neither mRNA vaccines nor AlphaFold. These stories differ greatly in form, but share a mechanism: the result was not locked away, but given out, and it returned many times over. The other side of the lesson is equally plain: society often assumed the risk, while a few received the profit, and poor countries received the benefits later than rich ones. Development does not guarantee a fair division—the division has to be arranged separately.

**Third lesson: individually rational decisions can produce aggregate expenditure without comparable benefit.** The cable through the Appalachians, empty apartments used as savings boxes, the army of insurance administrators, advertising that lures the same buyers back and forth, and a new arms race—every decision was rational for the person making it. Only in aggregate did it become waste. Unlike a single government project, the spending of many participants is harder to see in one budget; that does not mean no one has studied or estimated it. The experience of arms-limitation treaties and the global minimum tax shows that such traps can be escaped only by agreement—when there is a place where the trade-off is laid openly on the table: this much for one purpose, which means this much will not go to another. The era had almost no such places, and those that did exist—citizens’ assemblies and open platforms for discussing rules—worked on a small scale; no one yet knows how to extend them to decisions involving trillions.

**Fourth lesson: efficiency without reserves is fragility.** “Just-in-time” supply chains, the most advanced microchips in the hands of one or two companies, foreign-exchange reserves held by those who can freeze them—all were highly profitable as long as nothing broke. The book’s fourth measure, resilience to shocks, was scarcely reflected in prices during the 2010s, and the pandemic and war presented the bill all at once. The response was “resilient supply chains,” subsidies, and tariffs. But here the lesson divides in two: insurance against a shock is development, while isolation for its own sake is deadweight waste; Chapter 12 showed where a spiral of mutual prohibitions leads. The boundary between them is drawn not by a slogan, but by the question the Korean officials of Chapter 14 asked every recipient of a subsidy: what will you deliver in return?

**Fifth lesson: machines bring back Keynes’s question—and make it central.** For two hundred years, the Luddites’ fear did not generally come true: machines destroyed some occupations but created others, and that is an honest argument against panic. Yet the era showed something else as well. Research on robots and the “hollowing out of the middle,” couriers managed by an app, and the first labor disputes over generative AI all say the same thing: the machine itself does not decide who receives its gains. Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson make precisely this argument across a thousand years of evidence in *Power and Progress* (2023): the direction of technology and the division of its fruits have always been determined by who had the power to negotiate the rules. Critics reply that over long periods the benefits of machines nevertheless spread widely; but they, too, agree that this happened neither automatically nor immediately. If machines reach ever further into intellectual work, then labor ceases to be the economy’s only bottleneck. The question of this book—where to direct the surplus—then ceases to be a historian’s question and becomes everyone’s: what will be done with the time machines free—will it go to a fifteen-hour week, new knowledge, or another lap on the treadmill?

Reduced to one sentence, these lessons are this: the years 2010–2026 showed that an abundance of possibilities does not by itself become a secure life for the majority. Humanity has learned to create surplus better than ever; it has not yet learned to decide openly and together where to direct it. The final section of the chapter shows how this gap looks in numbers, while Chapter 18 shows how it repeats across every era in the book.

## The Numbers

The figures for this era are recent, and many will still be revised: statistics for the latest years are always adjusted in hindsight. The figures below are therefore limited to those published by international organizations and statistical agencies themselves, with a source named for each. As in the previous chapter, they should be read in pairs: what grew in the world of goods and skills, and what grew only in prices and accounts.

**Access and knowledge.** According to the International Telecommunication Union, around two billion people used the Internet in 2010; by 2024, the figure was around 5.5 billion, roughly two thirds of humanity. According to estimates by the US National Human Genome Research Institute, the cost of reading a human genome fell below one thousand dollars. The UN estimates that the world’s population grew from 6.9 billion to more than eight billion, while average life expectancy, after briefly falling during the pandemic—to around seventy-one years in 2021—had exceeded seventy-three years again by the middle of the 2020s, according to UN data.

**Poverty.** According to World Bank estimates, using a poverty line of $2.15 a day at 2017 purchasing power—the new three-dollar line adopted in 2025 produces higher levels, but the trend is the same—the share of people in extreme poverty fell to around nine percent by 2019. In 2020, it rose for the first time in a generation: the pandemic pushed around seventy million people back below the line, according to the same estimates. The decline then resumed, but much more slowly than in the 1990s and 2000s, and an increasing share of the extremely poor live in sub-Saharan Africa and countries at war. At the same time, the International Labour Organization estimates that around six in ten workers worldwide are still employed informally—without a contract, insurance, or pension.

**Energy and climate.** According to IRENA, installed solar-power capacity worldwide grew from around forty gigawatts in 2010 to almost two thousand by the end of 2024—roughly a fiftyfold increase. Yet according to the international Global Carbon Project, carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels did not fall over the same period, but rose from around thirty-three to thirty-seven billion tonnes a year. According to the Copernicus service, the average temperature in 2024 was roughly 1.6 degrees above the preindustrial level. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers consumed around one and a half percent of world electricity in 2024, and the share was growing rapidly.

**Cheap money and asset prices.** According to the Federal Reserve’s own data, its balance sheet—the total bonds and other assets it had bought—grew from around $900 billion before the 2008 crisis to almost $9 trillion in 2022. Bloomberg calculated that the total value of negative-yielding bonds worldwide reached around $18 trillion at the end of 2020. According to the Case–Shiller index, US home prices more than doubled from their 2012 low to the summer of 2022. The IMF estimated that total world debt—of governments, companies, and households—jumped to 256 percent of world product in 2020, the largest one-year increase since the Second World War. By the IMF’s calculations in the autumn of 2021, the budgetary support alone that governments directed toward combating the effects of the pandemic totaled around $17 trillion.

**Who received it.** According to the Federal Reserve, in the middle of the 2020s the top one percent of American families held almost one third of all household wealth, while the bottom half held two to three percent. Apple’s market value exceeded one trillion dollars in August 2018 for the first time in history, and three trillion in January 2022; in July 2025, the AI chipmaker Nvidia became the first company to pass four trillion. According to the companies’ own reports, the four largest American technology corporations invested around $350–400 billion in data centers and equipment in 2025—more than the annual product of most countries. No one yet knows whether this construction will foster development like the railways of Chapter 11 or prove deadweight like the cable through the Appalachians in Chapter 16.

**Deadweight and destructive items.** The Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI) estimated that world military spending reached around $2.7 trillion in 2024 and grew by more than nine percent in a single year—the fastest increase since the end of the Cold War. At the beginning of 2025, the World Bank, together with the Ukrainian government, the European Union, and the UN, estimated the country’s reconstruction needs at around $524 billion over a decade—and the figure continued to grow. According to estimates by the Budget Lab at Yale University, the average effective rate of US import tariffs rose in 2025 to its highest level since the 1930s. And when a 2015 YouGov survey asked whether their work made a meaningful contribution to the world, thirty-seven percent of employed Britons answered “no”—a figure David Graeber later relied on in his book about “meaningless work” (its disputed nature was discussed above).

The chapter’s conclusion in numbers is this: access to communications and computing expanded to billions of people, while the cost of solar energy fell. In the United States, home prices more than doubled from 2012 to the summer of 2022 and the Federal Reserve’s balance sheet reached almost $9 trillion; world military spending equaled 2.5 percent of GDP in 2024. These are different indicators: an increase in the price of existing property is not the same as expenditure of produced surplus. The share of resources that went into development, defense, and recovery from disasters has not been calculated here.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; an error by a factor of one and a half to two is possible. As in the previous chapter, the unit of measure is one hour of work by a typical worker in the country and period concerned; a working life is seventy thousand hours; for reference, a sack of wheat weighs fifty kilograms and cost around twelve to fourteen dollars on the world market in the 2020s. I chose one destructive expenditure of the era and one deadweight expenditure.

### Destruction in Ukraine, 2022–2024

**Input data.** The Fourth Rapid Damage and Needs Assessment (World Bank, government of Ukraine, European Commission, and UN, February 2025) estimated direct damage through the end of 2024 at $176 billion. This is an estimate of the value of destroyed homes, roads, businesses, and other property, not a count of hours expended by Ukrainian workers.

**Hours and lives.** The value of destroyed property cannot be divided by the median wage: its price includes materials, equipment, imports, and labor from different years and countries. I therefore do not calculate an equivalent in “working lives” here. The destruction of already-created capital is established by the direct damage estimate.

The toll of the dead in this war, as in Chapters 12 and 16, is not included here: more than one million soldiers on both sides have been killed or wounded, more than twelve thousand civilian deaths have been confirmed, and the true figure is higher. Nor does this include the parties’ military expenditure, lost output, or the six million who left the country.

**Alternative.** Comparing destroyed property with education expenditure would require a verified budget and recognition that reconstruction cannot automatically replace current services. I do not establish a number of “years of education” here.

### Bitcoin Mining, 2009–2025

**Input data.** Glassnode estimates that by the end of 2024 Bitcoin miners had received around $71 billion in rewards and fees at the exchange rate prevailing on the day of mining. This is their revenue, not their cost. The Cambridge Centre for Alternative Finance estimated the network’s electricity use in 2024 at around 140 terawatt-hours. Estimating real costs requires local and contemporaneous prices for electricity, equipment, and labor.

**Hours and lives.** Miners’ revenue cannot be treated as the value of resources consumed or divided by an American wage to produce a global labor equivalent. Energy-consumption data show the scale of one cost item, but do not provide a total sum or a number of “working lives.”

Bitcoin’s defenders regard the energy expenditure as payment for protecting the network against falsified records; for users in countries with unstable currencies, this may be a valuable service. Greater computing power may raise the cost of attacking the network, but does not by itself measure the additional social benefit of each kilowatt-hour. Comparison with other ways of protecting records requires a separate calculation.

**Alternative.** IRENA publishes the cost of building solar power plants, but miners’ revenue cannot be converted into gigawatts in place of their verified costs. Alternatives can be compared after the real resources used by the network have been assessed.

**Conclusion.** Direct damage in Ukraine and the costs of operating the Bitcoin network measure different phenomena: war destroys existing capital and takes lives, while mining consumes energy to protect records. The data presented here do not allow their full costs to be compared in “working lives” or the entire operation of the network to be declared deadweight waste.
