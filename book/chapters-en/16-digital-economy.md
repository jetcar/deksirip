# Chapter 16. The Digital Economy and the Second Globalization (1990–2010)

## A Portrait of the Era

San Francisco, January 9, 2007. The annual Macworld conference is under way at the Moscone Center. Steve Jobs, head of Apple, walks onto the stage in a black turtleneck and jeans and promises to unveil three new devices: a music player, a mobile phone, and a “window onto the Internet.” The audience expects three boxes, but within minutes it becomes clear that they are one and the same thing—a flat glass slab without a keyboard, controlled with a finger. The hall rises in applause. Six months later, on June 29, lines will form outside stores in American cities, and people will sleep on the sidewalk to be among the first to buy an iPhone.

On the back of the phone’s box, a line in small print describes the era better than any textbook: “Designed by Apple in California. Assembled in China.” The whole world fits between those two sentences. The touchscreen, memory, chips, camera, and battery came from Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, Germany, and the United States. They were assembled at the vast factories of the Taiwanese company Foxconn in Shenzhen, China—a city that thirty years earlier had been a modest border town in a rural county beside Hong Kong (the legend of the “fishing village” greatly exaggerates its humbleness), and that became one of the first “special economic zones” discussed in Chapter 15. Journalists estimated that hundreds of thousands of people worked at the Longhua complex—the population of a medium-sized European city—most of them young migrants from villages who lived in factory dormitories. Finished phones were loaded into steel containers, the containers onto ships, each carrying thousands of such boxes, and within weeks they reached warehouses in California, Germany, or Japan.

Who received the money that the buyer paid for this phone? In 2010, economists Yuqing Xing and Neal Detert took an iPhone 3G apart component by component. They estimated that of roughly $179 in factory cost, assembly in China itself accounted for about $6.50. The rest consisted of components made in other countries, while Apple itself—the owner of the design, software, and brand—kept the largest portion of the retail price: more than half, according to calculations by Kenneth Kraemer, Greg Linden, and Jason Dedrick. These figures are debatable—estimates differ among researchers, and calculating a “country’s contribution” from such teardowns is not simple—but the picture is clear: in the new economy, the largest reward went not to whoever made the product by hand, nor even to whoever made its components, but to whoever devised how to make it and owned the rights to that knowledge.

This scene contains all three forces examined in this chapter. The first is **digital technology**: computers, the Internet, and mobile communications. In two decades, they went from tools of scientists and the military to things ordinary people carried in their pockets. According to the International Telecommunication Union, tens of millions of people used the Internet in the mid-1990s; by 2010, the figure was around two billion, while the number of mobile subscriptions had surpassed five billion. The second force is **the second globalization**: the standardized shipping container, cheap communications, and the opening of China, India, and the former Soviet bloc split production into stages and scattered them across the planet. This is precisely what the term “global supply chain” means: one product passes successively through factories in different countries, each adding its share of value. The decisive date was December 11, 2001, when China joined the World Trade Organization—the club of countries that had agreed to lower tariffs and observe common rules of trade. The third force is **finance**, which had grown in the preceding era and now acquired computers and a global network, learning to turn almost any promise of payment into a commodity, even the mortgage of a working family in Nevada.

Now consider the second scene, the one that brought the era to an end. New York, Monday, September 15, 2008, Seventh Avenue. Employees emerge from the glass tower of the investment bank Lehman Brothers carrying cardboard boxes filled with their belongings. Overnight, the bank, which had operated for more than 150 years and survived the Civil War and the Great Depression, filed for bankruptcy—the largest in US history. Beneath it lay hundreds of billions of dollars in debt, and beneath that debt lay the homes of Americans bought on credit they could not repay. Within weeks, credit froze around the world: banks stopped lending even to one another, orders dried up, and container ships carrying those same Chinese goods lay idle outside ports. In 2009, world trade contracted more sharply than in any year since the Second World War.

Only twenty months separate the two scenes—January 2007 and September 2008. Yet they reflect, as if in a drop of water, the central question of this book. The years 1990–2010 created a surplus of unprecedented scale. The cost of computing and communication fell thousands of times over; hundreds of millions of people in Asia moved from rice fields to factories and earned cash wages for the first time; things that had been luxuries in the 1980s—a computer, a telephone, an airline ticket abroad—became commonplace. Where did this surplus go? Some went toward development: into networks, knowledge accessible to all, new industries, and the incomes of people who had previously lived by subsistence farming. Some became rent for those who controlled platforms, brands, and patents. And a substantial part went into debt and the prices of homes and shares, which kept rising until they collapsed. Each path had its own mechanisms, and we will follow them in the same order as in earlier chapters: first energy and technology, then labor, money, and power—and in the end ask what created during these twenty years made the world richer, what destroyed wealth, and what merely burned resources while creating an appearance of growth.

## Energy and Technology

Every era in this book began with the question of what people used to produce: muscle, water, coal, or oil. For 1990–2010, the answer sounds strange. The era’s defining technology lifted and moved almost nothing—it transferred and processed information. Yet to understand where the surplus came from, we must see that beneath this seemingly weightless economy lay a very heavy foundation of silicon, glass, steel, and coal.

Let us begin with silicon. Gordon Moore’s observation, discussed in Chapter 15—that the number of transistors on a chip doubles roughly every two years—continued to hold throughout these two decades. A transistor is a tiny electrical switch, the “building block” of any computer; the more of them there are on a single chip, the more calculations it can perform each second. Intel’s Pentium processor of 1993 contained about three million transistors, while processors of the late 2000s contained hundreds of millions or more. What matters for the economy is not the figure itself, but its consequence: the cost of a single computation fell by about half every eighteen months to two years. No product in history had become cheaper so quickly for so long. Had cars fallen in price at the same rate, one would have cost less than a cup of coffee. Memory became cheaper in much the same way: a hard drive that held a few dozen megabytes in the early 1990s could hold thousands of gigabytes by 2010—tens of thousands of times more—yet cost less.

The second layer was glass. Information had not only to be processed, but also transmitted, and here fiber optics played the decisive role: strands of ultra-pure glass through which signals travel as pulses of light. The first transatlantic fiber-optic cable, TAT-8, was laid in 1988, and during the 1990s the ocean floors were covered by a network of such cables. A single system of hair-thin fibers carried more telephone calls than all the copper transatlantic cables combined. Mobile communications spread alongside fiber. The digital GSM standard, agreed upon by European countries, went into service in Finland in 1991 and soon became common across most of the world—an example of how an open agreement on rules, like the protocols of the Internet, can accelerate the spread of technology more than any isolated inventive breakthrough. In poor countries, the mobile phone was often the first telephone of any kind: Africa and South Asia leapfrogged the era of landlines that had never had time to be laid there.

The third layer was software, and here the era invented something entirely new in economic terms. Once written, a program can be copied a million times at almost no cost. Economists call this **zero marginal cost**: marginal cost is the cost of producing one more, additional unit of a good. For bread, it is the flour, the oven, and the baker’s labor; for a copy of a program or a song in digital form, it is almost nothing. In 1994 Jeff Bezos founded the online store Amazon; in 1998 two Stanford graduate students, Larry Page and Sergey Brin, founded the Google search engine; in 2001 Wikipedia appeared, written by volunteers; Facebook followed in 2004 and YouTube in 2005. All of them rested on the same foundation: the Internet’s open protocols, open-source code such as Linux, and cheap servers. Knowledge that had previously been stored in libraries, reference books, and the minds of specialists became available, for the first time in history, to anyone with a network connection for the price of that connection.

The fourth layer was steel—in other words, transport. The container, born in the 1950s (Chapter 14), reached maturity in this era. Ships grew ever larger: in 2006, the Danish company Maersk launched the *Emma Maersk*, which could carry more than ten thousand standard containers. Shipping a television from Shanghai to Rotterdam came to cost only a few dollars, a negligible share of its price. The combination of cheap transport and cheap communications produced the global supply chain: a company in California could send a design to a factory in China in the morning and receive the finished product a few weeks later. The division of labor that Adam Smith described using the example of a pin factory (Chapter 10) now stretched across the entire planet.

And finally, the fifth and deepest layer was energy. Here the digital era was not weightless at all. Billions of people moving from villages to factories needed electricity, steel, and cement, and coal supplied them above all. China’s electricity generation roughly tripled between 2000 and 2010, with coal-fired power stations accounting for most of the increase; by the end of the decade, China was burning almost half the coal mined worldwide. Thus, the iPhone from the chapter’s opening scene was not only a product of Californian thought, but also of Chinese coal. Oil was cheap in the 1990s—a barrel cost around ten dollars at the end of 1998—but in the 2000s its price rose with Asian demand and reached almost $150 in July 2008. Expensive oil, in turn, spurred a technology whose significance became clear later: in the United States, independent oil and gas companies, above all Mitchell Energy, owned by the Texas oilman George Mitchell, had spent almost twenty years doggedly searching for a way to extract gas from dense shale. In 1997 Mitchell’s engineers found a cheap form of hydraulic fracturing—injecting water and sand under pressure to crack the rock—and after Devon acquired the company in 2002, it was combined with horizontal drilling. The government also helped, through a federal tax credit for “unconventional” gas and research by the Department of Energy, though scholars disagree over how decisive these were. By the end of the 2000s, this method had opened access to shale gas, and then oil, previously regarded as inaccessible.

The result was a paradoxical picture. Energy use per person in the rich countries barely increased during these years—in the United States and Western Europe, per-capita consumption remained roughly level or even declined slightly. But the amount of information produced by each unit of energy grew beyond comparison: the same kilowatt-hour now yielded millions of calculations and thousands of transmitted messages. Growth came not from new strength in muscles and motors, but from learning to direct the same strength more precisely. On a global scale, meanwhile, energy use per person began rising again because China and other catch-up countries were repeating the path of coal-based industrialization that England had followed two hundred years before them.

One question remained, and it tormented economists throughout the era. Robert Solow’s paradox, discussed in Chapter 15—the computer age was visible everywhere except in the productivity statistics—continued to haunt them. Companies bought computers by the million, yet output per hour of labor in the United States grew slowly. The answer came with a delay: from roughly 1995 to 2004, productivity growth in the American economy almost doubled compared with the preceding two decades. In *The Rise and Fall of American Growth*, Robert Gordon shows that this surge was real but brief: after the mid-2000s, output growth slowed again, and in his assessment the digital revolution had less effect on everyday life than electricity, indoor plumbing, and the internal-combustion engine. His opponents, such as Erik Brynjolfsson, argue that statistics do a poor job of measuring free goods—search, encyclopedias, and maps used at no charge. Checks by Chad Syverson and others, however, have shown that undercounted free goods can explain only a small part of the slowdown. The debate is unresolved. But for the question of this book, it matters for this reason: the era’s technology created an enormous opportunity, while the degree to which that opportunity became greater well-being for the majority no longer depended on engineers, but on how work was organized and who received the gains. That is where we turn next.

## Organization of Labor

In the spring of 2010, photographs appeared in newspapers around the world that sat uneasily beside the image of the sleek glass phone: nets were being stretched beneath the windows of Foxconn dormitories in Shenzhen. In the space of a few months that year, more than a dozen young workers at the complex took their own lives. Each tragedy had its own causes, and comparison with China’s overall suicide rate offers no simple answer, but journalists and researchers who visited the factory described the same daily life: shifts of ten to twelve hours; overtime without which decent earnings were impossible; an assembly line where a single movement was repeated thousands of times a day; militarized discipline; and rooms with eight to twelve beds. The company raised wages, brought in psychologists, and installed nets. The scene stayed in people’s memory because it revealed the underside of the same supply chain in which assembly cost six and a half dollars.

Who were these people? The defining worker of the era was not a programmer in California, but the Chinese **migrant worker**—*nongmingong* in Chinese, or “peasant-worker.” This was a person formally registered in a home village under the *hukou* household-registration system, but working in a city. Registration determined where children could attend school free of charge and where a person could receive medical care and a pension, so migrants remained half-guests in the city: their children usually lived with grandparents in the village, while they themselves returned home once a year for Lunar New Year. According to China’s National Bureau of Statistics, by the end of the 2000s more than 200 million peasants were working outside agriculture, and around 145 million of them had left their home areas to do so—more than the entire population of Russia at the time. This was probably the largest human migration in history. What England experienced in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, when peasants left villages for the factories of Manchester (Chapter 10), happened here in a single generation and on an incomparably greater scale.

Was this a disaster or a stroke of good fortune? The honest answer is both, and therein lies the central difficulty of the question. Earnings on an assembly line were several times higher than the income from a tiny family plot, and the money migrants sent home built brick houses in villages and paid for children’s education. According to World Bank estimates, between 1990 and 2010 the share of Chinese people living in extreme poverty fell from a majority of the population to a small fraction—never before had so many people escaped deprivation so quickly. But prosperity was bought at a price that income statistics do not capture: divided families, hazardous workplaces, and the absence of a right to strike or form independent trade unions. The worker surrendered most of his surplus—the difference between what he produced and what he received—and that share went into new factories and roads, the profits of brand owners overseas, and the foreign-exchange reserves discussed in the next section.

At the other end of the chain, the picture was reversed. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the number of manufacturing workers in the United States fell from about seventeen million in 2000 to fewer than twelve million by 2010. Machines and software replaced some of those jobs; competition from Asian imports carried away others. In a study that became known as the “China shock,” economists David Autor, David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson compared American counties exposed to Chinese imports to differing degrees. Their work and a sequel written with Daron Acemoglu and Brendan Price estimated that the growth of these imports in 1999–2011 cost around two million jobs, including indirect losses. Other economists who account for the gains from cheap components and exports produce estimates two or three times smaller; but these authors’ research shows persistent losses in the worst-affected counties. Workers laid off from furniture and textile factories in North Carolina did not move to places where new jobs were being created; instead, they remained out of work for long periods, went onto disability benefits, and watched their towns decline. Economic theory promised that the gains from trade would outweigh the losses and that the winners could support the losers. The gains were real—cheap goods for every buyer—but meaningful support for the losers was never organized. Similar stories unfolded in England, France, and the industrial regions of Eastern Europe, where factories closed even faster after the collapse of the planned economies (Chapter 15). Germany, however, was a net winner according to calculations by Wolfgang Dauth, Sebastian Findeisen, and Jens Suedekum: exports to the East created more jobs than imports destroyed.

Within the rich countries, the structure of work itself was changing. Autor and the British economists Maarten Goos and Alan Manning called it **labor-market polarization**: above all, formerly middle-ranking routine occupations were disappearing—the bookkeeper checking invoices, the typist, the machine operator—because a routine that can be written as a set of rules is the easiest kind of work to give to a computer. The two extremes grew: well-paid work requiring judgment and education—engineers, lawyers, analysts—and poorly paid work requiring hands and physical presence—cleaners, security guards, care workers, couriers. The middle on which the “middle-class” way of life had rested during the postwar decades (Chapter 14) was thinning out. A degree became the principal passport upward, and the earnings gap in the United States between a university graduate and someone with only a high-school diploma widened markedly.

The firm itself changed as well. Nike had owned almost none of its own factories even in the 1980s; in the 1990s and 2000s, this model became the norm across entire industries. A company kept the concept, the brand, and sales for itself, while contracting out manufacturing, shipping, cleaning, accounting, and even conversations with customers—this is called **outsourcing**, literally “sending outside.” Companies such as Infosys and Wipro grew in Bangalore, India, where hundreds of thousands of engineers wrote software and maintained systems for American and European banks; one impetus was the “Y2K problem,” when the world urgently needed programmers to repair old code that stored years with two digits. Fiber-optic cables on the ocean floor made commonplace what had once seemed absurd: an American company’s help desk in an Indian city. Walmart, the largest private employer in the United States, turned its stores and warehouses into a single computing system: every bottle of shampoo sold immediately became an order to a supplier. Productivity in retail did indeed rise as a result, and economists regard retailing—and Walmart above all—as a significant source of the acceleration in American productivity at the end of the 1990s. But pressure on prices traveled down the chain—to suppliers, their workers, and the stores’ own employees.

Finally, the era produced a form of labor with almost no precedent. Wikipedia, the Linux operating system, and the Apache web server were written largely by volunteers—without pay, in their free time, out of interest, for reputation, or from conviction. An enormous number of hours of skilled labor became a common resource used by everyone. For the question of this book, this is an important case: part of the surplus freed by cheap technology and the leisure of educated people went not into consumption or profit, but into knowledge accessible to all—a pure example of development-enhancing expenditure. Yet commercial giants also grew on top of open-source code, taking the commons for free and selling services for money—a duality to which we will return.

Taken together, these developments reveal a common feature. From 1990 to 2010, productivity grew rapidly worldwide, but in many countries the share of that growth received by wage earners declined. In their surveys, the International Labour Organization and the IMF noted a fall in the **labor share**—the portion of national income paid as wages rather than profit, interest, and rent—in most developed countries and many developing ones. The explanations are disputed: some economists, including Loukas Karabarbounis and Brent Neiman, blame cheaper technology that replaces routine labor; others point to globalization, which allowed capital to choose the cheapest workers on the planet; still others cite weaker trade unions and the market power of the largest “superstar firms” (Autor and co-authors). There are skeptics as well: Matthew Rognlie showed that a substantial part of the decline was connected with rising income from housing, rather than factories and machinery. Most likely, all these forces operated at once. And the question of where the rest of the surplus went, if not into wages, takes us away from the assembly line and toward money—toward the way the era distributed and redistributed its output.

## Exchange and Money

In the autumn of 1995, programmer Pierre Omidyar, who lived in California, listed a broken laser pointer on his small website. Someone bought it for about fourteen dollars. Surprised, Omidyar wrote to the buyer to make sure he understood that the pointer did not work. The buyer replied that he collected broken pointers. From this episode grew the eBay auction site—one of the first examples of what the network could do to exchange: bring together people who would otherwise never have met and find a buyer for even the strangest object. The medieval fairs of Champagne (Chapter 7) brought merchants from across Europe together six times a year, almost without interruption; eBay operated around the clock for the entire world. But for strangers to trust one another, a new invention was needed: the **rating**. After each transaction, the parties left feedback about each other, making a seller’s reputation visible to everyone. In essence, it was a digital version of the same mercantile trust once sustained by guilds and personal letters.

Here appeared the feature that became central to the economy of the era: the **network effect**. The more people use a marketplace, the more valuable it becomes to every new participant. Sellers went where there were more buyers, and buyers where there were more goods. As a result, such markets rarely support many equal players: one wins, and the winner takes almost everything. This happened with eBay in secondhand trade, Google in search, and Facebook in social interaction. Economists call such companies **platforms**—they produce almost nothing themselves, but earn money by bringing others together. The second feature of digital goods is their **near-zero marginal cost**—the cost of making one more copy. Writing a program or recording an album is expensive; copying it for the millionth time costs almost nothing. In 1999, student Shawn Fanning launched Napster, a service through which people shared music files for free. The courts shut it down by 2001, but the process itself could no longer be stopped: according to the industry federation IFPI, global sales of recorded music fell by about half during the 2000s. Economists disagree over how much of this was caused by free file sharing and how much by the sale of individual songs instead of albums and competition from other forms of entertainment: Felix Oberholzer-Gee and Koleman Strumpf found no significant harm from file sharing, while Stan Liebowitz and most subsequent studies did. But when a copy is free, the old rule “pay for each copy” ceases to work, and revenue is sought elsewhere: in advertising, subscriptions, and the sale of data about people’s attention. Advertising is precisely how Google and Facebook paid for services that were free to users—a bargain in which payment is made not with money but with attention, and to which this book will return.

Money itself changed as well, though most people barely noticed. On January 1, 1999, eleven countries of the European Union introduced a common currency, the euro; on January 1, 2002, German marks, French francs, and Italian lire in the pockets of more than 300 million people gave way to new banknotes. It was the largest monetary experiment since Bretton Woods (Chapter 14): countries gave up their own printing presses for the sake of a single market without currency-exchange counters. The benefit was tangible—travel, trade, and price comparisons became easier. But the design of the euro contained a fissure that at the time was discussed mainly by specialists: the money became common, while budgets and banking supervision remained national. Credit from the wealthy core poured into the periphery—Spain, Ireland, and Greece—where interest rates fell to German levels for the first time. The next chapter will show what came of it.

Monetary flows between continents were more conspicuous still. The Asian financial crisis of 1997–1998, when the currencies of Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea collapsed within a few months and Russia defaulted in August 1998, taught developing countries a harsh lesson: without a stockpile of dollars, a country is defenseless in a panic. Many drew the conclusion, China foremost among them. Its trade surpluses became **foreign-exchange reserves**—stocks of foreign money and bonds held by the central bank. According to the People’s Bank of China, by 2010 they exceeded $2.5 trillion, the largest such stockpile in the world, and a substantial share was invested in US government bonds. The result was a remarkable arrangement: through his bank and his government, a Chinese worker earning one or two hundred dollars a month lent money to an American buyer so that the buyer could purchase the phone assembled by that worker. In 2005 Ben Bernanke, soon to become chair of the US Federal Reserve, called this phenomenon a “global saving glut.” Money from around the world sought a safe home and found it in American securities, keeping lending rates low. Claudio Borio and Piti Disyatat of the Bank for International Settlements objected that the mortgage bubble was fed less by Asian savings than by European banks, which borrowed in dollars and bought American mortgage securities; China, meanwhile, held mainly government bonds. Both channels probably operated.

Within rich countries, an ever greater share of income flowed through finance. Economists call this **financialization**—the growing weight of banks, funds, and securities transactions in the economy. According to calculations based on data from the US Bureau of Economic Analysis, the financial sector’s share of American corporate profits in the early 2000s ranged, by different estimates, from one quarter to almost forty percent—several times its level in the 1950s and 1960s. Banks learned to turn ordinary loans into commodities. A mortgage issued to a family in Arizona was no longer kept by the bank until repayment: it was pooled with thousands of others, sliced into “tranches” carrying different levels of risk, and sold to investors around the world. This procedure is called **securitization**, from *securities*. Above it rose another structure of **derivative instruments**, or derivatives—contracts whose price depends on the price of something else: an interest rate, a currency, another person’s debt. According to the Bank for International Settlements, the notional value of over-the-counter derivatives exceeded $600 trillion by 2008—about ten times the world’s annual output. The figure itself is misleading, because the actual sums at risk were much smaller, but it clearly shows how far the financial superstructure had become detached from factories and shops.

The new system had real achievements. Credit became more accessible: young families obtained mortgages that their parents would not have received, small firms obtained loans, and inventors obtained money for experiments. **Venture-capital funds**—investors that put money into risky young companies in exchange for an ownership stake—financed the birth of Google, Amazon, and hundreds of other firms. Cards could be used to pay almost everywhere, and in 2007 the Kenyan operator Safaricom launched M-Pesa, a money-transfer system using a basic mobile phone that allowed millions of people without bank accounts to store and send money. A study by Tavneet Suri and William Jack, published in *Science* in 2016, estimated that access to M-Pesa helped roughly two percent of Kenyan households escape extreme poverty. The estimate was disputed—critics questioned the method—but both sides acknowledge that mobile money sharply reduced the cost of transfers for the poor. This is an example of how the network lowered the costs of exchange for the poorest, not only the richest.

But the same system had a reverse side familiar from earlier chapters. Cheap credit supported both new investment and demand for existing assets; the balance between them is not assessed here. The first instance involved shares in Internet companies. The NASDAQ technology index rose above five thousand points in March 2000 and had lost almost four fifths of its value by October 2002: firms with no profit, valued in the billions, disappeared within months. The second involved housing: according to the Case–Shiller index, US home prices more than doubled between 1997 and 2006, and rose still more in Ireland and Spain. An increase in the price of an already-built home does not in itself add living space. It changes the valuation of property and the terms on which buyers can obtain housing, but it is not the same thing as spending produced surplus. Economists of the time spoke proudly of the “Great Moderation”—twenty years of low inflation and mild recessions—and prices in shops did indeed rise slowly. Many economists—including those at the Bank for International Settlements and John Taylor at Stanford—believed that excess money had simply moved to the place where inflation was not measured: asset prices. Bernanke objected that low interest rates explained only a small part of the increase in home prices and that careless lending was the main cause; the debate remains unresolved.

Thus, for these two decades, the answer to the question “where did the surplus go?” divides into several streams. Some reached buyers around the world in the form of cheap goods and free digital services. Some went to the hundreds of millions of new urban workers in Asia who escaped destitution for the first time. A substantial share became knowledge and infrastructure—cables, software, encyclopedias open to all. But an ever larger portion settled on financial balance sheets: in central-bank reserves, intermediaries’ profits, and inflated prices for homes and shares. How much of this was development and how much was a wager that would one day have to be paid depended on the rules of the game. The next section examines who set them and how.

## Power and Institutions

Marrakesh, April 1994. Here the final act of years of negotiations known as the Uruguay Round is being signed—a package of agreements from which the World Trade Organization will emerge on January 1, 1995. Newspapers focus on tariffs on steel and textiles, but among the documents is an agreement with the unexpressive name TRIPS—Trade-Related Aspects of Intellectual Property Rights. **Intellectual property** is the right to own not an object, but an idea: an invention (patent), a text or program (copyright), a name and symbol (trademark). TRIPS required every WTO member to protect patents for at least twenty years and respect foreign copyrights. Poor countries that might otherwise have freely adopted foreign technology, as the United States did in the nineteenth century, accepted this in exchange for access to rich-country markets for their textiles and agricultural goods. Recall the iPhone teardown at the beginning of the chapter: the owner of the design and brand took most of the price. The right to that share was secured not on the factory floor, but at the negotiating table.

This is a useful starting point because the era’s central institutional story concerns neither the strength nor the weakness of the state, but **which rules it wrote and for whom**. The familiar picture of the 1990s is one of a “retreat of the state”: privatization, deregulation, and the free movement of capital. In 1989, economist John Williamson called the set of prescriptions offered by the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank to countries seeking their help the **Washington Consensus**: balanced budgets, low inflation, open trade, privatization, and freedom for foreign investment. In some Latin American and Eastern European countries, these measures stopped hyperinflation and opened markets; in others, especially where there were neither functioning courts nor an honest tax service, they placed common property in the hands of a few, as Chapter 15 showed in the case of Russia. In reality, however, the state went nowhere. It changed roles: it ceased to own factories and became the author of rules—and rules proved more powerful than factories.

The first example is the digital markets themselves. In 1996, the United States adopted a telecommunications law whose famous Section 230 freed Internet platforms from liability for material published by their users. Lawyer Jeff Kosseff called its provisions “the twenty-six words that created the Internet”: in his view, without them neither eBay, YouTube, nor Facebook could have existed in their present form, because a lawyer would have had to review every post. The 1998 Digital Millennium Copyright Act, by contrast, strengthened protections for content owners and prohibited circumvention of copy protection—but it, too, gave platforms a “safe harbor”: they would not be liable for others’ infringements if they removed disputed material after a complaint from the owner. Each law was reasonable in its own way, but together they created an asymmetry: a platform was barely responsible for the material of others so long as it removed it on demand, while the rights holder was protected more strongly than ever. There was also an attempt to curb market power: in 1998, the US Department of Justice filed an antitrust suit against Microsoft, which bundled its own browser with Windows, installed on almost every personal computer. In 2000, a trial court ordered the company split in two, but an appeals court overturned the remedy, and in 2001 the case ended in a settlement imposing moderate restrictions. Lawyer Tim Wu and some economists believe that even this outcome cleared room for Google and other newcomers; skeptics reply that Microsoft would have missed the technological shift even without the lawsuit. But the next giants—search engines and social networks—grew with almost no antitrust oversight: the prevailing view was that if a service was free, the consumer could not be harmed.

The second example is finance, where the rules moved in one direction. In 1999, the United States repealed part of the Glass–Steagall Act of 1933, which, after the crash of the Great Depression era (Chapter 12), had separated ordinary banks that accepted deposits from investment banks that traded securities: the two could now belong to a single group. The amendment merely legalized what had already happened: a year earlier, the Travelers insurance group had merged with the bank Citicorp to form the giant Citigroup. Most researchers, however, do not regard this repeal as the principal cause of the coming collapse: Bear Stearns and Lehman, the first to fail, were pure investment banks. In 2000, the Commodity Futures Modernization Act removed a substantial share of over-the-counter derivatives from supervision. In 2004, the Securities and Exchange Commission allowed the largest investment banks to calculate their required capital using their own risk models, and by 2007 the **leverage** of some—the ratio of borrowed money to their own—was around thirty to one: a loss of just over three percent of assets was enough to wipe out all their capital. How much the rule itself was to blame remains disputed, since these banks had also carried high leverage in the late 1990s. The rating agencies that assessed the safety of mortgage packages were paid by the same banks whose securities they rated—a conflict of interest known to everyone. Alan Greenspan, who chaired the Federal Reserve from 1987 to 2006, was convinced that market participants were better at monitoring risk than regulators; at a congressional hearing in October 2008, he admitted that he had found a flaw in the worldview on which he had relied.

Why did the rules change in precisely this way? “Malice” explains little. It is more accurate to speak of **regulatory capture**, an idea formulated by economist George Stigler in 1971: an agency created to supervise an industry gradually begins to see the world through that industry’s eyes. People moved from Wall Street to the Treasury and back again; the financial industry spent enormous sums on lobbying; and, above all, while prices were rising, asset owners and financial intermediaries benefited, making the rise difficult to challenge. In his 2009 article “The Quiet Coup,” Simon Johnson, a former chief economist of the IMF, compared the influence of the financial elite in the United States with that of the oligarchs in developing countries whom he had observed through his work at the Fund. The comparison is debatable—the United States retained elections, independent courts, and a free press—but it expressed an important idea: recipients of large incomes can influence the rules governing their distribution; the extent of that influence must be examined in each case.

The third example is the catch-up countries, where events ran counter to the Washington Consensus. China did not privatize its largest banks and state-owned companies; it kept capital movements and the yuan’s exchange rate under control and directed credit into roads, ports, and factories. Such a system is called **state capitalism**: markets and private firms exist, but the commanding heights of the economy remain under the control of the state and the party. Growth was nevertheless the fastest in the world. Advocates of the free market reply that China’s success began precisely with the state’s retreat—peasants were allowed to sell their crops, private citizens to open workshops, and foreigners to build factories—while debt and empty construction projects accumulated where the state continued to command. Both sides are half right. Economist Dani Rodrik perhaps put the conclusion most precisely: successful catch-up countries—South Korea, Taiwan, and China—did not follow a single recipe, but experimented, adapted foreign rules to local conditions, and protected their industries until they were strong enough for open competition.

The final test came in 2008. When the crisis struck, the state that had supposedly “retreated” returned instantly and with enormous sums. On October 3, 2008, the US Congress approved the $700 billion TARP program to rescue the financial system; the Federal Reserve supplied trillions in loans; Britain took control of the Royal Bank of Scotland and a large stake in Lloyds; and in November, China announced a stimulus package of four trillion yuan, about $600 billion, aimed chiefly at construction and infrastructure. That same month, the Group of Twenty met in Washington at head-of-state level for the first time, bringing together not only rich countries but also the largest developing ones. Most economists believe these measures prevented the crisis from becoming a new Great Depression. Yet they also made explicit a rule that had previously remained in the shadows: the profits of the largest financial firms were private, while in an emergency their losses became public. Banks deemed “too big to fail” were rescued; families that lost their homes, for the most part, were not. The direct monetary loss was smaller than feared: according to the final estimate of the Congressional Budget Office, TARP cost the Treasury about $30 billion, while investments in banks even produced a return. But the main cost lay elsewhere—in the promise now visible to everyone: the largest institutions were allowed to take risks because they would be rescued.

This section’s answer to the book’s central question is that in 1990–2010 the surplus was distributed not only by the market, but also by the rule-makers—in Marrakesh and Geneva, Washington, Brussels, and Beijing. Rules that opened trade and allowed platforms to grow aided development. Rules that allowed risk to be shifted onto others and debts to be hidden off balance sheets turned the surplus into a wager for which everyone eventually had to pay. We turn next to the final balance: what fostered development, what destroyed wealth, and what was burned to no purpose.

## What Fostered Development

The Indian state of Kerala, the late 1990s. A fisherman sets out onto the Arabian Sea at dawn and returns by noon with a catch of sardines. Where should he land? Dozens of small markets line the coast, each with its own price: at some there are many buyers and little fish, at others the reverse. The fisherman does not know what is happening in the next bay, and sardines spoil within hours. So he lands at the nearest market, and if it is already glutted with fish, the catch is sold for next to nothing or simply thrown back into the sea. Economist Robert Jensen, who studied these markets, found that before mobile communications arrived, on some days a substantial share of the catch was discarded at certain markets while people only a few kilometers away went home without fish. Between 1997 and 2001, cellular towers were erected along the coast, and fishermen began calling buyers on shore from their boats to ask about prices. According to Jensen’s estimates (the 2007 article “The Digital Provide”), fish waste almost disappeared, prices converged across markets, fishermen’s incomes rose by about eight percent, and prices for buyers fell by about four percent. Not one new boat, not one new fishing net—knowledge of where one was needed had simply become cheap.

This small story reveals the era’s central mechanism of development better than an account of Silicon Valley. **Cheap information reduces waste.** Chapter 0 listed coordination costs among the criteria of development—the cost to people of reaching agreement, finding one another, and establishing whether someone can be trusted. Throughout almost all of history, these costs were enormous: a messenger, a letter, a fair held once a season, an intermediary who knew more than you did and took a share for it. The mobile phone, the search engine, and the seller rating on eBay struck precisely here. The surplus that had once rotted on the quay or settled in an intermediary’s pocket now reached those who worked and bought.

The second mechanism was that **knowledge became a common resource**. In 1989, physicist Tim Berners-Lee, working at the European research center CERN near Geneva, proposed a system of interlinked pages that came to be called the World Wide Web. In April 1993, CERN announced that the Web’s software was being placed in the public domain: anyone could use it and build anything upon it without paying a penny. This decision is worth comparing with Watt’s patent on the steam engine, discussed in Chapter 10: Michele Boldrin and David Levine believe that it delayed improvements to the engine for years, while their opponents argue that without the patent the engine itself would not have existed. Here the result was the opposite: an open standard became the soil in which Wikipedia, online stores, and scientific archives all grew. By 2010, a schoolchild in Nairobi with Internet access could read more than a professor at a major university half a century earlier. Scientists placed articles in open archives such as arXiv, created by physicist Paul Ginsparg as early as 1991, and new results spread around the world in days rather than months. The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003 by an international consortium, published decoded sequences in open databases as soon as they were produced—the participants made a specific agreement to do so, preventing anyone from patenting the genome itself. According to the US National Human Genome Research Institute, the cost of reading a human genome fell from roughly $100 million in 2001 to tens of thousands by 2010—faster than the price of microchips.

The third mechanism was **escaping poverty by joining global production**. This was the era’s greatest achievement and the one most resistant to simple assessments. According to World Bank estimates, between 1990 and 2010 the share of the world’s population living in extreme poverty fell from about thirty-six to sixteen percent, and their number fell by almost one billion, even as the world’s population grew by one and a half billion. The goal the UN set in 2000 for 2015—to halve the share of people in extreme poverty—was achieved around 2010, five years early. China accounted for the lion’s share of this success, with India, Vietnam, and Indonesia contributing substantially. The supply chain whose dark side was discussed in the section on labor was also a school. A factory assembling someone else’s phones taught workers discipline and technique, engineers quality standards, and local suppliers how to make components that a demanding customer would accept. Economists call this **learning by doing**. Firms in Shenzhen that began by assembling and reselling other people’s products were themselves producing telecommunications equipment, batteries, and electric vehicles fifteen or twenty years later. Japan and South Korea had learned in the same way before them (Chapter 14); now the same ladder became available to hundreds of millions.

Honesty requires qualifications here. Debate continues over how much of this ascent was due to global trade and how much to domestic reforms and public investment in roads, electricity, and schools. Sub-Saharan Africa, less integrated into supply chains, reduced poverty far less, and the number of people in extreme poverty there actually rose during these years. Moreover, the “extreme poverty” line itself—less than two dollars a day at prices adjusted for differences in purchasing power (in 2025 the World Bank raised it to three dollars, and poverty levels under the new line are somewhat higher)—is very low: a person who crosses it is still far from affluent. Even so, history had never seen such a rapid and widespread escape from hunger. If we ask where the era’s surplus went, rising incomes and falling poverty in Asia were among the most conspicuous results by number of people affected. The data presented here do not reveal what share of the total surplus they required.

The fourth mechanism was the **cheapness of goods and services**, a benefit enjoyed by everyone. The container, Walmart’s warehouse logistics, and competition among Asian factories lowered prices for clothing, footwear, household appliances, and electronics. For a low-income family in Ohio or Poland, this meant that the same wage bought more. Economists including Pablo Fajgelbaum and Amit Khandelwal, and Christian Broda and John Romalis, estimated this gain at several percent of household income, with poor households receiving a larger proportional benefit than rich ones because they spend more of their money on mass-market goods. Free digital goods—search, maps, encyclopedias, and email—stand apart: GDP statistics barely see them because no one pays for them. This is precisely the subject of the dispute between Robert Gordon and his opponents: perhaps the era’s development was greater than the figures show, or perhaps it was less than the prophets of the “new economy” promised.

Finally, there is an ambiguous case familiar from earlier chapters, in which nineteenth-century railway bubbles left tracks behind them (Chapter 11). During the boom at the end of the 1990s, telecommunications companies laid enormous quantities of fiber-optic cable underground and across the ocean floor—far more than was needed. When the bubble burst, many of them went bankrupt, but the cables remained: much of the fiber lay “dark,” or unused, for years. Investors lost money, but the world acquired cheap bandwidth on which online video, cloud computing, and that same Indian help desk later grew. In *Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital*, economist Carlota Perez describes this sequence as a pattern: speculative fever pays for the construction of a new era’s infrastructure, while those who arrive after the crash reap the benefits. It is development bought at the price of ruin—and the question of whether the same thing could have been built without a bubble remains open.

Taken together, the development-enhancing part of the surplus of 1990–2010 went wherever the costs of knowledge and exchange were falling: into networks and open standards, publicly accessible stores of knowledge, the skills of hundreds of millions of new workers, and goods made cheap for everyone. These mechanisms shared one feature: they expanded the opportunities of the many, not only of those who already owned wealth. But other mechanisms operated beside them—mechanisms that destroyed capital, trust, and entire cities. They are the subject of the next section.

## What Destroyed

Newfoundland, July 2, 1992. At a hotel in St. John’s, Canada’s fisheries minister John Crosbie announces a two-year moratorium on the northern cod fishery. In the room are fishermen whose grandfathers and great-grandfathers caught the fish on the Grand Banks of Newfoundland. For five hundred years the cod here had seemed inexhaustible: the first European sailors wrote that they could be scooped up in baskets. But in the 1960s through the 1980s, factory trawlers with enormous nets and echo sounders arrived at the Banks, and for a time the catch increased severalfold. Inshore fishermen and some scientists raised the alarm, but the official stock assessment, based on the trawlers’ own catches, overestimated it for years, while quotas remained high because too many jobs depended on them. By 1992, Canadian fisheries scientists estimated that the spawning stock had fallen to a negligible fraction of its former size. The moratorium put around thirty thousand people out of work and is often called the largest single layoff in Canadian history. It was extended again and again; even decades later, the cod had not returned to its former abundance.

This was destruction in the precise sense defined in Chapter 0: capital that no one had created and no one could recreate was destroyed. New technology—the same force that reduced waste among Kerala’s fishermen in the section on development—here made it possible to consume in one generation what nature had accumulated over centuries. In GDP statistics, these years appeared as growth in catches and incomes; the depletion of the stock did not appear at all. This is the era’s central trap: much of what destroyed wealth was recorded as profit at the moment it happened. In much the same way, Amazon forests were being cleared for cattle pasture and soybeans during these years: according to Brazil’s National Institute for Space Research (INPE), about twenty-seven thousand square kilometers were deforested in 2004—an area nearly the size of Belgium in a single year.

**Destruction of trust.** The second strand concerns the invisible capital without which markets cannot function. In December 2001, the Texas energy company Enron, which *Fortune* magazine had named America’s most innovative company for six consecutive years, went bankrupt. It emerged that a substantial share of its profits existed only in its accounts: debts were hidden in shell companies, and future revenue was recorded as current income. Enron brought down Arthur Andersen with it—one of the world’s five largest accounting firms, which had certified those statements—and tens of thousands of its employees around the world lost their jobs. Six months later, it emerged that the telecommunications giant WorldCom had credited itself with around eleven billion dollars in nonexistent profit. Enron employees who held their retirement savings in their own company’s shares often lost most of them. Congress responded with the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, tightening reporting rules—but the blow struck at the very principle on which the stock market rests: the belief that figures in a report mean what they say.

The 2008 crisis repeated this lesson on a global scale. Mortgage loans were issued without checking whether borrowers could repay them—the industry gave such loans the ironic nickname “liar loans” because no one verified the borrower’s income. Economists dispute who took out most of these loans—poor families, or the middle class and speculators buying homes for resale (Atif Mian and Amir Sufi versus Manuel Adelino, Antoinette Schoar, and Felipe Severino). The loans were packaged into securities, and rating agencies paid by the packagers themselves gave them the highest safety rating. In its final report of 2011, the congressional Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded—with some members dissenting and placing more blame on government housing policy—that the crisis was avoidable, and identified the rating agencies as one of the disaster’s critical links. In December 2008, as markets were already collapsing, Bernard Madoff, a former chair of the NASDAQ exchange, was arrested: for decades, his investment fund had paid “returns” to old investors with money from new ones. On paper, his pyramid scheme was valued at about $65 billion. Every such case raised the cost of trust for everyone who traded honestly.

**Destruction through crisis.** The crisis itself destroyed value on a scale comparable to war. According to the Federal Reserve, the net worth of American households fell by more than ten trillion dollars between 2007 and 2009. US unemployment reached ten percent in October 2009; in Spain it exceeded twenty percent by 2010. Millions of American families lost their homes through mortgage foreclosure. Some of these losses were “on paper”—inflated prices had burst—but many were entirely real: people who remained unemployed for long periods lost skills and connections, while research shows that graduates entering the labor market at the worst moment earned less than luckier peers for years. Economists call such long-term scarring **hysteresis**—an effect in which a temporary shock permanently lowers the path the economy would otherwise have followed. According to estimates by Laurence Ball and others, output in the rich countries never returned to the trend on which it had been growing before 2008.

**Debasement of money.** The era also furnished two classic examples of destruction familiar from earlier chapters. In Argentina, which had rigidly pegged the peso to the dollar in the 1990s, debts accumulated by the end of the decade while the economy contracted for several years. In December 2001, the government restricted cash withdrawals from bank accounts—a measure nicknamed the *corralito*, or “little corral”—and riots broke out in the streets; several presidents came and went within two weeks, and the country declared what was then the largest sovereign default in history. The peso was unpegged from the dollar, and the savings of millions of people lost value within months; according to the national statistics agency INDEC, more than half of all Argentines were living below the poverty line in 2002. Still worse occurred in Zimbabwe. Beginning in 2000, Robert Mugabe’s government seized commercial farms from their owners and distributed them mainly to its supporters, many of whom neither knew how nor intended to farm. A food-exporting country became dependent on humanitarian aid, while the budget gap was covered by the printing press. Field research by Ian Scoones and his colleagues later showed that many of the new smallholders eventually established productive farms and that droughts had compounded the crop failures; but the collapse of the first years and the inflation driven by money printing are beyond dispute. By 2008, inflation had reached astronomical levels—according to calculations by economist Steve Hanke, prices were doubling roughly every day in November—and in January 2009 the central bank issued a one-hundred-trillion Zimbabwean-dollar banknote. Soon the national currency had to be abandoned altogether. Almost every destructive action from the table in Chapter 0 came together in this one story: politically motivated redistribution of property, the expulsion of skilled owners, and the debasement of money.

**Wars and the destruction of institutions.** The two decades initially called the “end of history” were not peaceful. The breakup of Yugoslavia in 1991–2001 was accompanied by wars that killed more than one hundred thousand people by researchers’ estimates—around one hundred thousand in Bosnia alone, according to the Sarajevo Research and Documentation Center—while the siege of Sarajevo lasted almost four years. In Rwanda in the spring of 1994, as Chapter 15 noted, between half a million and one million people were killed in one hundred days. The war that followed in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, drawing in the armies of several neighboring countries, claimed several million lives through hunger, disease, and violence according to the humanitarian organization International Rescue Committee; the figure is disputed by the authors of the *Human Security Report* (2010), but even cautious estimates are enormous. Joseph Stiglitz and Linda Bilmes calculated that the 2003 invasion of Iraq cost on the order of three trillion dollars once future spending on veterans and interest on the debt were included (*The Three Trillion Dollar War*, 2008); the estimate is disputed, but not its order of magnitude. In the first days after Baghdad fell, the National Museum of Iraq was looted, while the occupation authority’s decisions to remove members of the former ruling party from public service and disband the army deprived the country of tens of thousands of specialists and put hundreds of thousands of armed men onto the streets—an example of how breaking institutions destroys accumulated knowledge and order.

**Rejecting knowledge and selling its appearance.** In South Africa, where the HIV epidemic had infected millions of people by the early 2000s, the government of President Thabo Mbeki questioned the link between the virus and AIDS for years and obstructed the distribution of antiretroviral drugs. Researchers at the Harvard School of Public Health (P. Chigwedere and co-authors, 2008) estimated the cost of this policy at more than three hundred thousand lives that could have been saved. In the United States, destruction came from the opposite direction—not from rejecting science, but from selling its appearance. OxyContin, a painkiller released in 1996, was promoted as carrying a low risk of addiction; in 2007, its manufacturer pleaded guilty to misleading doctors and patients. This, as Abby Alpert and her co-authors showed, was a major starting point of the opioid epidemic that claimed hundreds of thousands of lives over the following decades. In 2015, economists Anne Case and Angus Deaton showed that mortality among middle-aged white Americans without a university degree rose between 1999 and 2013—from poisoning, liver disease, and suicide—while it fell in other rich countries. They later called these “deaths of despair” and connected them with the collapse of the same industrial communities discussed in the section on labor. Economist Christopher Ruhm objects that the chief driver was the supply of cheap opioids rather than economic despair; both probably played a part.

If we now ask where the era’s surplus went, the destructive part of the answer looks like this. Some was simply consumed—along with the cod, the forest, and the pensions of Enron employees. Some burned in wars and printing presses. Crises and fraud could also undermine trust in reports, ratings, and banks. These losses matter, but no comparable monetary estimate is available here; it cannot be claimed that they cost more than all the other consequences. There was, however, another kind of expenditure—one that destroyed almost nothing directly, but created almost nothing either. That is the subject of the next section.

## What Was Deadweight Waste

San Francisco, February 2000. Pets.com, an online store selling home-delivered dog and cat food, goes public. Its mascot—a sock-puppet dog with a microphone—has already appeared in an advertisement during the Super Bowl, the final of the American football championship, where a minute of airtime costs millions of dollars. Investors snap up the shares. The problem is that sacks of pet food are heavy, delivery is expensive, and prices are set below wholesale to attract customers: the company loses money on every order. In November of the same year, nine months after going public, Pets.com announces its liquidation. The grocery-delivery service Webvan met a similar fate: according to press reports, it raised around $800 million from investors, built enormous automated warehouses, and went bankrupt in 2001.

This is classic deadweight waste in the sense of Chapter 0: resources were spent, but neither productivity nor well-being increased. The money went neither into invention nor into infrastructure that would outlive the crash, like the fiber-optic cable discussed in earlier sections, but into advertising, a race for market share at any cost, and the wages of people building something that could never pay for itself. It is important to distinguish among such cases. The dot-com bubble left behind cables, experience, and survivors—Amazon also lost money for years, but it was building warehouses and software that later became the foundation of its business. Pets.com left behind a sock-puppet dog. The boundary between a bold investment and empty expenditure becomes visible only in hindsight, and this is the central difficulty of any assessment: a market that finances risk inevitably finances much that proves useless. The questions are only how much—and who pays for the mistakes.

**Homes built for prices, not for living.** Another possible source of deadweight waste was housing construction that remained unused for long periods. At the height of Spain’s construction boom in the mid-2000s, widely cited estimates said that almost as many new homes were started there each year as in Germany, France, and Britain combined, though the three countries had several times Spain’s population. Alongside homes for future residents, housing was also built for resale in anticipation of rising prices; the relative importance of these motives in construction is not established here. After the crash, the expression “ghost estates” appeared in Ireland: a government survey in 2010 counted almost three thousand unfinished or half-empty housing developments, some standing in fields far from any jobs. In China, new districts such as Kangbashi in Inner Mongolia became famous by the end of the decade—broad avenues, museums, and squares designed for hundreds of thousands of residents but, according to journalists, occupied by only a small fraction. Some such districts filled up over time, and critics of China’s “ghost cities” often drew conclusions too hastily. But where they did not, cement, steel, and labor had been spent on structures that served not as housing but as wagers on rising prices. In the terms of this book, these were the pyramids of the era: surplus invested in stone whose function was to preserve the owner’s wealth rather than create new wealth.

**A race no one wins.** Economists call the third kind of deadweight waste a zero-sum game: rivals spend resources to overtake one another, but the total gain does not grow. The stock market supplied the clearest example. In 2010, Spread Networks completed an almost straight fiber-optic cable between Chicago and New York, running through the mountains of Pennsylvania, for one purpose: to cut the transmission time between exchanges by a few thousandths of a second. According to journalist Michael Lewis (*Flash Boys*, 2014), the project cost around $300 million. In **high-frequency trading**, where computer programs buy and sell securities in fractions of a moment, those milliseconds meant profit: whoever learned of a trade first could buy before everyone else. But society as a whole does not care whether prices in Chicago and New York converge three thousandths of a second earlier or later. Competitors soon began building chains of microwave towers that were faster still, and the former advantage lost its value. This is the precise analogue of an arms race: every participant is forced to spend because a neighbor is spending, and in the end all remain in the same relative positions. Defenders of high-frequency trading reply that robotic intermediaries narrowed the difference between buying and selling prices and made transactions cheaper for everyone; economists Eric Budish, Peter Cramton, and John Shim acknowledge this benefit but show that the race for milliseconds itself is pure waste that could be eliminated by a simple change in trading rules.

The same mechanism operated more broadly. Economist Thomas Philippon calculated what financial-sector intermediation cost the American economy for every dollar it raised and allocated, and found that over more than a century this cost had barely fallen—despite computers, networks, and all the financial ingenuity of the era (*American Economic Review*, 2015). In retail and communications, technology made services many times cheaper; in finance, he estimated, the gains from technology went mainly to the industry itself rather than its clients. Added to this was a drain of talent: Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz’s study of Harvard graduates found that by the 2000s, a substantial share of the best graduates from leading universities—including physicists and mathematicians—were going into finance and consulting. Every brilliant mind occupied with beating another brilliant mind on the stock exchange by a fraction of a second was a mind not occupied with medicines, energy, or schools. Data scientist Jeff Hammerbacher, one of the first people to build data operations at Facebook, spoke even more sharply about his own industry in 2011: the best minds of his generation, he said, were thinking about how to make people click on advertisements.

**Advertising and the war for attention.** This observation takes us to the digital sector. Free search and free email were paid for by advertising, and there was nothing empty about that: advertising can provide buyers with useful information—where to buy and what is new. Some advertising spending goes into luring buyers from one similar brand to another; its scale is not estimated here. If two companies selling nearly identical soda double their advertising budgets, there is no more soda; only the price both sellers pay to hold their ground rises. As advertising moved online, it required ever more refined ways of keeping a person in front of the screen: collecting data about that person and selecting what would engage that individual in particular. It is impossible to calculate how much of this spending was a service and how much a race for other people’s attention. But the question the era posed—where to direct engineers’ abilities—became one of the central questions of the next.

**Prestige, patent rent, and weapons.** There were also forms of deadweight waste familiar from earlier chapters. According to the Census Bureau, the average floor area of a new detached house in the United States grew from about 150 square meters in the early 1970s to more than 230 by 2007, even though families became smaller over the same period; Americans nicknamed such houses “McMansions”—fast-food mansions. Heavy SUVs in the suburbs served more as status symbols than as vehicles for off-road travel. In the realm of knowledge, an industry arose that Adam Smith could never have imagined: firms that produced nothing themselves bought patents and made money by suing those who did produce something. They were nicknamed **patent trolls**; the small Texas town of Marshall became a favored venue for such cases because its court was regarded as friendly to plaintiffs. Economists James Bessen and Michael Meurer estimated the direct cost of these suits to American companies by the early 2010s at tens of billions of dollars a year; critics consider the estimate too high, but even they acknowledge that the industry existed by taxing other people’s inventions. This is rent in its pure form: a right intended to reward the inventor became a tax on invention. Finally, according to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute (SIPRI), US military spending almost doubled in current dollars over the decade after 2001. As before, some such spending had technological side effects—GPS satellite navigation, created by the military, became an everyday civilian tool during these very years—but the total does not reveal how much was necessary defense, how much produced useful spin-offs, and how much was an excessive race.

**An ambiguous case: the “Y2K problem.”** In fairness, we should recall an example that many were quick to classify as deadweight waste. In the late 1990s, companies and governments around the world spent, by various estimates, hundreds of billions of dollars fixing old programs that stored the year in two digits and risked “deciding” that 1900 followed 1999. The night of January 1, 2000, passed with almost no failures, and many concluded that the threat had been invented and the money wasted. Specialists object that there were no failures precisely because they had been prevented—the same paradox as vaccination, whose benefit is known by the absence of disease. Skeptics, in turn, point out that countries that spent far less on preparations—Italy, Russia, and South Korea—also experienced no serious failures. The boundary between necessary insurance and overinsurance was never established. Moreover, the Y2K problem forced organizations to review outdated systems and, as already noted, spurred the growth of India’s software industry. The line between expenditure and investment is not always visible at once.

These examples show three possible kinds of deadweight expenditure: races for an advantage in speed or advertising, unused construction, and status consumption. They do not show whether these kinds were the largest by total expenditure in 1990–2010. In each case, some costs may have failed to produce the expected return; isolated examples cannot establish the net benefit or who received it for most of the population. What did the era reveal when development, destruction, and deadweight waste are considered together? That is the lesson of the chapter.

## The Lesson of the Era

Taken together, the preceding three sections make the years 1990–2010 look strangely divided. Never before had humanity received so much almost for free: knowledge once kept in the libraries of major cities became accessible from a village fisherman’s phone; a copy of a program, song, or article ceased to cost anything at all; hundreds of millions of people in China, India, and Vietnam escaped extreme poverty. At the same time, the prices of real estate and financial assets rose, and the crisis of 2008 required government support measures. Without a separate calculation, neither trading turnover nor the fall in the market value of assets can be treated as a share of the surplus wasted. The answer to the question “where did the era’s surplus go?” is therefore twofold, and this duality is the principal lesson.

**First lesson: what fostered development most was also cheapest.** The World Wide Web placed in the commons by CERN, the open preprint archive, Wikipedia, Linux, and open data from the human genome—all had low marginal cost for a new user because of open access; their total costs of development and maintenance were not zero and are not compared here with other expenditures of the era. Their strength lay in removing a barrier for everyone at once: each new user took nothing away from those already using them. This is the same mechanism as writing, the printing press, and the scientific journal in earlier chapters, but in its ultimate form: when copying is free, knowledge ceases to be a scarce good. And the lesson is the same as before—the development-enhancing effect arose where knowledge remained open and faded where knowledge was turned into patent or platform rent.

**Second lesson: markets can finance risk, but cannot distinguish a building site from a casino.** Economist Carlota Perez, discussed above, showed that speculative fever has repeatedly financed infrastructure in history—canals, railways, fiber-optic cable. This is an honest argument for free capital: a government planner would hardly have laid so much cable so quickly. But the housing boom and complex securities left behind not only debt and empty developments after the crisis, but also constructed housing, some of which was used. The net result depends on place and time. As early as the 1970s and 1980s, the American economist Hyman Minsky described this trap: a long period of calm teaches people to take ever greater risks, and stability itself breeds instability. The “Great Moderation” of the 1990s and 2000s became almost a textbook illustration of his point. The counterargument from market advocates also deserves a hearing: many believe the bubble was inflated less by markets than by central banks’ cheap money and government promises to rescue the largest players. Both sides agree on one point, however: when profit is private but loss is public, the surplus begins to flow into risks for which others pay.

**Third lesson: the gains from globalization were enormous, but unevenly distributed—and that has political consequences.** Economist Branko Milanovic studied changes in the incomes of every stratum of the world’s population from the late 1980s to the late 2000s and produced a picture nicknamed the “elephant curve”: the incomes of Asia’s middle class and the very top of rich countries grew fastest, while workers and the lower middle class in the West barely moved (*Global Inequality*, 2016). More than a billion people gained substantially, while hundreds of millions—the lower middle class of the West, the former Soviet bloc, and parts of Latin America—made almost no progress. Milanovic’s critics note that the elephant’s shape is partly explained by the stagnation of Japan and the former USSR, and that in rich countries alone the incomes of poorer people did rise, only slowly; but what matters more politically is that many of those who fell behind lived in the very countries whose voters determine the rules of world trade. David Autor and his colleagues’ work on the “China shock” showed that the losses did not simply dissipate: entire industrial towns failed for years to replace the factories that had left. As early as 2000—and in detail in *The Globalization Paradox* (2011)—Dani Rodrik warned of a “trilemma”: a country cannot simultaneously have deep globalization, democracy, and complete autonomy in choosing its policies; something must be surrendered. If the gains are not shared, the losers sooner or later refuse to play. The next chapter will show how this warning came true.

**Fourth lesson: technology does not decide where the surplus goes.** The computer, the Internet, and the container dramatically expanded the capacity to produce and exchange. But the same technology allowed some people to build an encyclopedia for everyone and others to lay a cable for the sake of three thousandths of a second on the stock exchange. Robert Solow marveled that computers could be seen everywhere except in the productivity statistics; Robert Gordon showed that the acceleration of the late 1990s was brief. Part of the explanation probably lies in measurement—free goods are poorly captured in GDP. Thomas Philippon’s calculations show that the unit cost of financial intermediation in the United States did not fall for a long time; they do not measure what share of the new technological possibilities went into financial or advertising races. The tool is neutral; what matters is where society directs its abilities and money.

Reduced to one sentence, these lessons are this: the years 1990–2010 showed that cheaper access to knowledge does not automatically eliminate financial and social risks. The surplus was enormous; its development-enhancing share—open knowledge, communications, and Asia’s escape from poverty—changed the world for the better, while the extractive and deadweight shares—financial races, wagers on the price of stone, and monopoly rent—prepared a crisis whose cost was shared by all. The world entered the 2010s carrying the legacy of this duality: cheap money, angry losers, and powerful platforms.

## The Numbers

The figures for this era are better documented than those of any previous one: statistical agencies, international organizations, and companies themselves counted almost everything. Yet this is precisely where measurement lags furthest behind life. A free encyclopedia or search engine barely enters gross product, while the price of a home or share can double without any increase in the world’s stock of goods and skills. The figures below should therefore be read in pairs: what grew in reality, and what grew only on paper. Almost all are estimates, and a source is named for each.

**Communications and computing.** Contemporary estimates (Nua, Internet World Stats) suggest that in 1995 the Internet had perhaps fifteen to forty million users—less than one percent of the world’s population; according to the International Telecommunication Union, by 2000 it had more than 300 million and by 2010 around two billion, nearly one person in three. There were more than five billion mobile subscriptions in 2010, most of them in poor and middle-income countries. According to Intel itself, its Pentium processor of 1993 contained around three million transistors, while mass-market processors of the late 2000s contained hundreds of millions or more than a billion. According to estimates by the US National Human Genome Research Institute (NHGRI), the cost of reading a human genome fell from around $100 million in 2001 to tens of thousands by 2010—faster than microchips themselves became cheaper. This is the part of the surplus almost invisible in monetary statistics: the ability to know and communicate became thousands of times cheaper.

**Trade and the container.** UNCTAD estimates that the world’s ports handled around ninety million standard twenty-foot containers in 1990 and more than half a billion in 2010—roughly six times as many. According to the World Bank, foreign trade’s share of world product rose from about two fifths in 1990 to more than half on the eve of the 2008 crisis. China, which accounted for less than two percent of world merchandise exports in 1990, overtook Germany in 2009 to become the world’s largest exporter, according to the WTO. By the end of 2010, its foreign-exchange reserves approached three trillion dollars according to China’s State Administration of Foreign Exchange—a substantial share of this money was invested in American bonds and returned to the United States as cheap credit. According to the WTO, world trade contracted by about twelve percent in volume in 2009, the steepest decline since the end of the Second World War.

**Poverty and life.** According to World Bank estimates, using a poverty line of $1.90 a day at 2011 purchasing power, about thirty-six percent of humanity—almost two billion people—lived in extreme poverty in 1990; in 2010, the figures were around sixteen percent and just over one billion. China accounted for more than half of this reduction, with the share of its population in extreme poverty falling from roughly two thirds to one tenth by the same estimates. According to the UN Inter-agency Group, mortality among children under five fell from ninety-three per thousand live births in 1990 to a little over sixty in 2010; according to the UN, average life expectancy worldwide approached seventy years. The world’s population grew from 5.3 billion to almost seven billion.

**Who lost.** According to the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, about seventeen million people worked in American manufacturing in 2000 and fewer than twelve million in 2010. Daron Acemoglu, David Autor, and their co-authors estimated that imports from China cost the American economy on the order of two million jobs in 1999–2011 (in a model that accounts for gains in other industries, Lorenzo Caliendo, Maximiliano Dvorkin, and Fernando Parro find a loss of about 800,000 in manufacturing); the remaining losses came from automation and recession, and economists dispute the relative weight of these causes. According to estimates by the International Labour Organization and the IMF, labor’s share of national income had been declining in most rich countries since the 1990s.

**Growth on paper.** The NASDAQ technology index closed above five thousand points on March 10, 2000, and had fallen by almost four fifths by October 2002. According to the Case–Shiller index, US home prices roughly doubled between 1997 and 2006 and then fell by more than a quarter. According to the Federal Reserve, American household debt rose from around nine tenths of annual disposable income in the early 1990s to around 130 percent in 2007. According to the Bank for International Settlements, the notional value of over-the-counter derivative contracts exceeded $600 trillion in 2008—about ten times total world product (this figure indicates the scale of the wagers, not the actual sums at risk). By some calculations based on Bureau of Economic Analysis data, the financial sector’s share of American corporate profits reached almost two fifths in the early 2000s. Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez calculate that on the eve of the crisis, including capital gains, the richest one percent of Americans received almost the same share of income as at the 1928 peak—around 23.5 percent—while the share of the top ten percent surpassed even that peak. Gerald Auten and David Splinter, who count income after taxes and benefits, find a much smaller increase (see Chapter 15 for the debate). According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, US unemployment reached ten percent in the autumn of 2009.

**The deadweight item.** Military spending began rising again after 2001. According to the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, by 2010 the world was spending more than $1.5 trillion a year on defense—more than at the end of the Cold War in constant prices—with the United States accounting for almost half. Joseph Stiglitz and Linda Bilmes calculated that the Iraq War alone cost on the order of three trillion dollars once future expenses were included. The “peace dividend” discussed in the previous chapter proved brief.

The chapter’s conclusion in numbers is this: communication and knowledge became thousands of times cheaper and accessible to billions; roughly one billion people escaped extreme poverty, children died less often, and lives grew longer. These are examples of development whose scale can be seen in the number of people reached; their share of the total surplus has not been calculated. At the same time, the prices of homes and shares, household debt, and the financial sector’s share grew in rich countries; some industrial towns lost jobs, and military spending increased once more. Yet the revaluation of shares and the turnover of wagers are not the same as resource expenditure. The data in this chapter are insufficient to say how much of the surplus went in each direction. The world entered the 2010s with this dual legacy—the subject of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; an error by a factor of one and a half to two is possible. The unit of measure is one hour of work by a typical worker in the country and period concerned; a working life is seventy thousand hours; for reference, a sack of wheat weighs fifty kilograms. I chose two expenditures of the era: one destructive, made by the state, and one deadweight, made by the market on credit.

### The Iraq War, 2003–2011

**Input data.** According to calculations by the US Congressional Research Service (reports by Amy Belasco), direct appropriations for the operation in Iraq totaled around $800 billion by 2011. Joseph Stiglitz and Linda Bilmes arrived at about $3 trillion after adding future medical care and pensions for veterans, interest on the debt, and losses to the economy; many regard their estimate as too high and direct appropriations as an understatement of the cost. According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, the median hour of American labor was worth around fifteen to seventeen dollars in 2005–2010.

**Hours and lives.** Eight hundred billion dollars represents about fifty billion hours, or roughly 700,000 working lives. Under the Stiglitz and Bilmes calculation, the figure is around 2.5 million. A fair range is from 700,000 to 2.5 million lives. For reference, at a wheat price of about ten dollars per sack in those years, the direct expenditure was equivalent to eighty billion sacks—roughly six global wheat harvests.

Here, as in Chapter 12, the accounting is not only in hours. By 2011, the Iraq Body Count project had recorded more than one hundred thousand civilian deaths from press and morgue reports; sample surveys produce higher figures—from 150,000 by the middle of 2006 (the WHO-sponsored Iraq Family Health Survey) to more than 600,000 (Burnham et al., *The Lancet*, 2006; many specialists consider this estimate too high). These lives cannot be converted into either hours or sacks.

**Alternative.** Comparing war expenditure with education funding is useful as a question of opportunity cost. But the estimate of the annual shortfall in school funding cited in the chapter’s sources has not been verified here; I therefore do not derive from it a number of years of universal education.

### Spanish Apartments That No One Bought, 2009

**Input data.** According to Spain’s Ministry of Housing and Ministry of Development, between 650,000 and 690,000 new homes stood unsold in the country at the end of 2009; even at the end of 2014, more than 500,000 remained. These are numbers of homes on particular dates, not numbers of buildings that remained useless forever. A comparable construction cost for one apartment has not been verified in this chapter.

**Hours and lives.** Without a verified construction cost and information on what share of the homes were eventually used, it is impossible to calculate either the total cost, the share of deadweight expenditure, or its equivalent in working lives. Prolonged vacancy and unfinished construction were real losses, but their scale requires a separate study.

**Alternative.** The number of unsold apartments cannot be converted into “years of education”: doing so would require verified data on apartment costs, final losses, and comparable expenditure on schools and universities.

**Conclusion.** Direct budget expenditure on the war is better known than the final cost of unwanted Spanish housing. Both cases show that current expenditure and construction can increase GDP even though their long-term benefits and costs differ.
