# Chapter 9. Institutions, Science, and Escape from the Malthusian Trap

## Portrait of the Era

London, Wednesday, November 28, 1660. At Gresham College, an educational institution bequeathed to the city by a wealthy merchant, a lecture on astronomy has just ended. It was delivered by twenty-eight-year-old Christopher Wren—the man who, a few years later, would rebuild fire-ravaged London and raise St Paul’s Cathedral. After the lecture, a dozen members of the audience remain behind: in the geometry professor’s rooms, they agree to create a society for “the promotion of physico-mathematical experimental learning.” Among them are the chemist Robert Boyle, the future bishop John Wilkins, diplomats, physicians, and courtiers. Two years later, King Charles II, only recently returned from exile, grants the society a charter, and it becomes the Royal Society.

The society chose the Latin words *Nullius in verba*—“take nobody’s word for it”—as its motto. Not Aristotle’s, Galen’s, a priest’s, or a king’s. A claim was accepted if it could be demonstrated: experiments were performed at meetings, and the society’s curator, the indefatigable Robert Hooke, was required to bring in new ones every week. They pumped the air from a glass sphere with an air pump and watched to see whether a candle went out and a bell still rang. They examined a flea and a piece of cork under a microscope—it was there that Hooke saw the compartments he called “cells.” Beginning in 1665, the society’s secretary, Henry Oldenburg, published the journal *Philosophical Transactions*, which still appears today: an experiment described in London could be repeated in Leiden or Florence, and its failure reported.

Now move twenty-eight years ahead. On November 5, 1688, an army led by the Dutch stadtholder William of Orange—around fifteen thousand soldiers—lands at Torbay in southwestern England. The English king James II, a Catholic long at odds with Parliament, offers almost no resistance and flees to France. A few months later, Parliament offers the crown to William and his wife Mary, James’s daughter, but on one condition: the new monarchs accept the Bill of Rights. The king can no longer impose taxes, maintain a standing army in peacetime, or suspend laws without Parliament’s consent. The English called these events the Glorious Revolution—“glorious” because in England it was almost bloodless; Ireland and Scotland still faced years of war.

Six years later, in the summer of 1694, the Bank of England opens in the London hall of the cloth merchants’ guild; a few months later it moves to the neighboring hall of the grocers’ guild and remains there for forty years. It was founded by private subscribers—around thirteen hundred people, from the lord mayor to widows and shopkeepers—who together provided the king with a loan of £1.2 million. The loan was needed for war with France, and there was nothing new in that: kings had always borrowed. The novelty lay elsewhere. Interest payments were secured by specific taxes approved by Parliament, while Parliament itself consisted to a considerable extent of the very people who had lent the money. Refusing to pay at the monarch’s whim became, at the very least, extremely difficult—exactly how difficult remains a subject of debate among historians, as discussed below.

And one final scene. The year is 1712, near Dudley Castle in central England. At the entrance to a coal mine stands a strange structure: an enormous beam rocking above a brick tower, a boiler pouring out steam, and a cylinder as tall as a man. This is the engine of Thomas Newcomen, an ironmonger from Dartmouth. Steam fills the cylinder, injected cold water condenses it, atmospheric pressure drives the piston down—and a pump at the other end of the beam raises water from the flooded mine. The engine is fantastically voracious and practical only where coal costs almost nothing, at the mouth of the coal mine itself. But for the first time, heat from burned fuel is reliably converted into mechanical work—not in a toy like that of Hero of Alexandria, nor in a temperamental predecessor pump, but in a working mechanism needed for a real task.

These four scenes—a society of scholars, a change of power, a bank, a steam engine—seem unrelated. Chapter 9 is about how they are connected. Chronologically, it covers roughly 1600–1800, reaching partly backward into the age of Copernicus and Galileo and forward to the threshold of the factory. Its action centers on England and neighboring countries of northwestern Europe—not because the rest of the world stood still, but because it was here that something unprecedented in history occurred.

To understand what that event was, recall Thomas Malthus. In 1798, this English clergyman published *An Essay on the Principle of Population*. His argument was simple: unless checked, population grows faster than food production. Every improvement—a new plow, a new crop, new land—gives people a little more bread, but within a generation or two additional mouths “consume” the additional bread, and the average person’s life returns to its former level, on the edge of subsistence. Famine, epidemics, and wars restore the balance again and again. Economists later called this the Malthusian trap, and everything we have seen in preceding chapters fits the description: from Sumer to the Spain of Philip II, average income fluctuated within a narrow corridor, while the gains of an era became growth in the number of people rather than growth in their well-being.

The irony is that Malthus described the trap just as his own country began to escape it. According to Wrigley and Schofield’s reconstruction, England had about five million inhabitants in 1700 and roughly eight and a half million by 1800, yet income per person did not fall, but rose slowly and steadily. In the nineteenth century, population multiplied several times over—and income rose with it. For the first time, growth in production outpaced growth in mouths not for a single generation, but permanently.

There are many explanations, and we will examine the principal ones. Some historians give pride of place to coal and high wages. Others emphasize science and the culture of testable knowledge born at Gresham College and places like it. Still others point to institutions: Parliament, the courts, patents, and the protection of property from arbitrary action by the crown. A fourth group emphasizes the colonies and slavery discussed in the previous chapter. Most likely it was the combination that mattered, and this chapter’s task is to show how one element caught onto another.

And here the book’s recurring question is sharper than ever. Where did the era’s surplus go? Until now, the answer has always proved discouraging: a substantial share dissolved into population growth, wars, and elite prestige. Now, for the first time, a noticeable share went into things that generated new surplus—knowledge, machines, and credit for enterprise. Yet, as we shall see, England also fought almost continuously during this era, while the debt for which the Bank of England was created grew chiefly to pay for cannon. That makes it all the more interesting to understand why, this time, development nevertheless outran destruction.

## Energy and Technology

Everything we saw in previous chapters lived on the current year’s sunlight. The bread eaten by a stonemason, hay for a horse, firewood in a brewer’s furnace, charcoal in a blacksmith’s forge—all were sunlight captured by leaves during the preceding months or decades. Historian Anthony Wrigley called such a world an “organic economy.” Its ceiling was set by the area of land: more heat required more forest; more plowing required more horses, which meant more oats and meadow. Every sector took land from another. That is why the Malthusian trap was not a pessimist’s invention, but an accurate description of the physical world: growth in people ran up against the same hectares.

Sixteenth- and seventeenth-century England reached this ceiling sooner than many. The forests around London had been cut down, firewood was growing more expensive, and the city, followed by the rest of the country, increasingly shifted to coal mined in the northeast around Newcastle and carried by sea. Coal smoked and stank; the king complained about its fumes and preachers cursed them, but it was cheaper. Historians of the coal industry such as John Hatcher and Michael Flinn estimate that British output grew from several hundred thousand tons in the mid-sixteenth century to several million tons by 1700 and roughly fifteen million by 1800. These are estimates, not exact records, but the order of magnitude is undisputed. Wrigley calculated that if the same energy had had to come from firewood, by 1800 England would have needed forest covering between a third and almost half the entire country—more than all its arable land; the exact figure depends on assumptions about forest growth. Coal was an “underground forest,” grown over millions of years, that did not have to be taken from fields.

Coal by itself provided only heat. Brewers, soap boilers, glassmakers, saltmakers, and brickmakers all learned to work with coal during the seventeenth century, which was already no small thing: cheap heat made dozens of goods cheaper. But no one knew how to turn heat into motion. Here appears the connection for which we began the chapter with the Royal Society.

In 1643, the Italian Evangelista Torricelli, a pupil of Galileo, inverted a sealed glass tube in a bowl of mercury and saw that the mercury did not fall all the way, but stopped at a height of around seventy-six centimeters. Empty space remained above it. This meant that air had weight and pressed on everything around it. A decade later, Otto von Guericke, burgomaster of Magdeburg, built a pump that removed air and staged a famous demonstration: teams of horses could not pull apart two copper hemispheres from which the air had been evacuated. Boyle and Hooke in London improved the pump and turned it into a laboratory instrument. The Frenchman Denis Papin, who worked for a time with Boyle, invented a steam boiler with a safety valve and described a cylinder with a piston that moved when the steam inside condensed.

Neither Torricelli nor Guericke was thinking about mines. But the knowledge that the atmosphere exerted pressure and a vacuum could perform work became common property: it was published, discussed, and demonstrated in experiments. In 1698, the military engineer Thomas Savery received a patent for the “Miner’s Friend,” a pistonless steam pump. It worked poorly and sometimes exploded. Newcomen, whose engine we described in the opening scene, combined cylinder, piston, and steam condensation in a machine that ran for decades. Whether Newcomen knew the scholars’ work is disputed; most likely only by hearsay. But, as historian Joel Mokyr emphasizes, what matters is not this, but that the very idea of an “atmospheric engine” was in the air, available to practitioners. Mokyr called this convergence of scholars, engineers, and craftsmen the “Industrial Enlightenment.”

Newcomen’s engine was extremely wasteful: it converted less than one percent of the heat from burned coal into work. It therefore took root only at coal mines, where fuel cost almost nothing. In the 1760s, James Watt, a Scottish instrument maker repairing a model of such an engine for the University of Glasgow, understood why it was so voracious: the cylinder was heated with steam and immediately cooled with water every cycle. Watt moved condensation into a separate vessel, allowing the cylinder to remain hot. He obtained a patent in 1769, and from 1775, together with Birmingham manufacturer Matthew Boulton, began producing engines that consumed several times less coal. Here we already stand at the threshold of the next chapter, but one point is important: Watt reached his solution by measuring and calculating—he measured the consumption of steam and heat and discussed his experiments with the chemistry professor Joseph Black, discoverer of latent heat—not by random trial and error. How much Black’s theory helped Watt remains disputed; Watt himself believed he had arrived at the idea independently.

Energy was not the only front. In 1709, the Quaker Abraham Darby at Coalbrookdale learned to smelt pig iron with coke—coal from which the volatile matter had been burned off—instead of charcoal. This freed metallurgy from the forests, although the method did not become widespread until several decades later. In agriculture, English farmers adopted rotations with turnips and clover: clover, it was later discovered, enriches the soil with nitrogen, while turnips provide winter fodder for livestock, producing more manure. Wheat yields rose, although agricultural historians such as Mark Overton dispute when the principal leap occurred—in the seventeenth or the eighteenth century.

And finally, instruments. Christiaan Huygens’s pendulum clock of 1656 reduced a clock’s daily drift from a quarter of an hour to ten or twenty seconds. The telescope and microscope expanded the world in both directions. And the story of longitude shows how society began consciously to buy knowledge. In 1714, the British Parliament established a prize of up to twenty thousand pounds for anyone who found a way to determine longitude at sea. Ships were wrecked because navigators did not know where they were. The solution came not from an astronomer, but a self-taught clockmaker, John Harrison: during a voyage to Jamaica in 1761–1762, his H4 marine chronometer erred by only a few seconds. The Board of Longitude never formally awarded him the prize; he received payments totaling more than twenty thousand pounds in installments and obtained the last major payment directly from Parliament in 1773, at the age of eighty. But the principle itself—paying for the solution to a problem rather than for noble birth—proved durable.

Let us return to the book’s question: where did the surplus go? In this chapter, a special kind of surplus appears for the first time—energy surplus. Coal provided energy without taking land from bread, which meant that growth in one sector no longer necessarily caused hunger in another. But coal also lay beneath the Ruhr and in China, while atmospheric pressure was known in Italy before England. Neither alone guaranteed a breakthrough. Surplus—monetary, intellectual, and human—had to flow into mines, workshops, and laboratories rather than another Escorial. Why that happened more often in England than elsewhere depended on who worked and under what conditions—and on who held power and how it was exercised.

## Organization of Labor

Imagine a village in Leicestershire around 1700. Beside the window in a peasant cottage stands a stocking frame—a frame-knitting machine invented as early as 1589 by the clergyman William Lee. A middleman from town has brought the householder thread; a week later he will collect the finished stockings and pay by the piece. The wife and daughters spin wool, the son helps his father in the fields during harvest, and in winter sits down at the frame. Such a family is no longer entirely peasant, but not yet working class. It lives on two incomes: from the land and from work for a merchant.

Historians call this system “dispersed manufacture,” or the putting-out system, and the process by which craft production for distant markets spread through villages “proto-industrialization.” The term was proposed in 1972 by the American historian Franklin Mendels, who studied Flemish linen weaving. The merchant supplied raw material and sometimes tools, the rural family supplied labor, and the city supplied the market. The merchant benefited because rural labor was cheaper than urban labor and not subject to guild rules: no one checked whether a weaver had completed a seven-year apprenticeship, how many looms he owned, or what price he charged. The family benefited because the previously idle winter months turned into money. Thus wool was woven around Norwich, broadcloth in Yorkshire, a mixture of linen and cotton in Lancashire, and linen in Flanders and Silesia.

The debate over proto-industry remains unsettled. Mendels considered it a direct step toward the factory: it accustomed people to the market, accumulated capital for merchants, and skills for workers. Critics reply that many such regions—Flanders and Silesia among them—never saw factory industry and grew poor in the nineteenth century. The honest answer is that dispersed manufacture was necessary soil, but not a guarantee. Where coal, machines, and capital were added, factories grew; where they were not, rural weavers lost out to factory competition and were ruined.

The second great change occurred in minds and purses. Dutch historian Jan de Vries proposed the concept of the “industrious revolution,” a play on “Industrial Revolution.” He observed that in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, families in Holland and England began working more: fewer holidays, more working days each year, and more market work by women and children. Hans-Joachim Voth’s calculations from London court records point the same way: by the late eighteenth century, the working year had grown noticeably longer, while “Saint Monday”—the custom of not working on the day after Sunday—was gradually disappearing. Why did people do this? De Vries answers: to buy. Tea, sugar, tobacco, calico, earthenware, clocks, mirrors—everything once considered a luxury became accessible, and a family exchanged leisure and household labor for money, and money for goods from the shop.

Here the book’s question takes a new form. The surplus of labor—the additional hours—went into consumption. Was that good or bad? On the one hand, broad demand for cheap goods created a market without which mass production would have been pointless: a cotton mill needed millions of customers, not a hundred dukes. On the other hand, some of these purchases, from sugar to tobacco, were products of slave labor on the West Indian plantations discussed in Chapter 8, and harmed the health of the buyers themselves. A dual case in its purest form.

The third change was the movement of people off the land. Wrigley estimates that around 1600 roughly two-thirds of England’s men worked in agriculture, but by 1800 fewer than half did, around forty percent. Yet the country still fed itself with almost no grain imports. This meant that one agricultural worker fed far more people than before—and the hands set free moved into craft, trade, transport, and services. No large country had previously achieved such a ratio. By comparison, around two-thirds of the population in France during the same era remained on the land, and in China still more.

How this happened is a painful question. Part of the answer is enclosure: the conversion of common fields and pastures into privately owned plots surrounded by hedges. The first wave of enclosure, in the sixteenth century, was for sheep and provoked revolts; the second, parliamentary wave in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries encompassed around a fifth of England’s land by historians’ calculations. The Marxist tradition saw enclosure as the violent expulsion of peasants, who then swelled the ranks of factory workers. Historian Robert Allen disputes this in *Enclosure and the Yeoman* (1992): his data show that enclosure redistributed income from smallholders to large landowners, but rising yields came chiefly from new crop rotations and the labor of farmers themselves, and began long before the parliamentary acts. Most likely both sides are right in their own way: productivity would have risen without enclosure, but those who lost the right to graze a cow on the common meadow lost a real part of their subsistence, and for them enclosure was extraction.

The fourth change was distinctive to England and is emphasized by Joel Mokyr, Morgan Kelly, and Cormac Ó Gráda. England possessed an unusually large stratum of skilled craftsmen: clockmakers, locksmiths, millwrights, turners, and instrument makers. These were people able to machine a part from a drawing, fit a valve, and assemble a mechanism without formal education. Without them, Watt’s idea would have remained on paper: the first cylinders for his engines leaked until the industrialist John Wilkinson learned to bore them accurately on a machine built for cannon barrels. Such craftsmen were cultivated through apprenticeship. The English Statute of Artificers of 1563 required seven years’ training under a master, and although the rule was increasingly evaded by the eighteenth century, the tradition of passing skills from hand to hand endured. Historian Jane Humphries showed that apprenticeship was widespread and accessible not only to the children of the wealthy.

Finally, what kept those at the bottom of society from falling? England had the Poor Law of 1601, under which every parish was required to collect a tax and support its destitute: the elderly, widows, orphans, and the unemployed. Economist Peter Solar considers it Europe’s first nationwide safety net against destitution. Critics—both contemporaries and Malthus himself—claimed it encouraged idleness and early marriage. But historians such as Peter Solar and Steve Hindle point to the reverse: confidence in minimal support allowed people to take risks—to move, change trades, and take on market work. A person without a safety net clings to the land even when it barely feeds him.

Now assemble the picture. Labor in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century northwestern Europe became more mobile, more market-oriented, and more skilled—and a substantial share of it remained hard, dependent, and poorly paid. Alongside the industrious revolution in England stood mass slavery overseas, with which the English economy was tightly intertwined. But the principal difference from earlier eras can be seen in one detail: an English workshop laborer was expensive. Robert Allen calculated that real wages in eighteenth-century London and Amsterdam were noticeably higher than in Paris, Florence, Vienna, or Beijing. Expensive labor made it profitable to replace workers with machines powered by cheap coal. Where hands cost pennies—as on Roman latifundia with their slaves—an invention remained a toy. Where hands were expensive, it became an investment. Thus surplus settled in workers’ pockets, in turn pushing the surplus of capital toward development.

## Exchange and Money

January 1672. London’s goldsmiths—jewelers who for some time had stored far more of other people’s money than they had made rings—learn news that spells ruin for many of them. King Charles II has announced the “Stop of the Exchequer”: he is suspending payments on his debts and will use incoming tax revenue to prepare for another war with Holland. The goldsmiths had lent the crown more than a million pounds—around £1.2–1.3 million—from their customers’ deposits. Now they had nothing with which to repay their depositors. Some bankers failed, depositors lost their savings, and interest on the frozen debt was paid irregularly before ceasing altogether for twenty years.

This episode clearly shows how the finances of almost every monarchy operated before the end of the seventeenth century. A king borrowed when he wished and paid when he could. Creditors had no protection: the courts belonged to the king, and so did the army. As we saw in Chapter 8, the Spanish crown declared itself insolvent more than once; French kings periodically put tax farmers on trial and seized their wealth. Money was therefore lent to monarchs reluctantly, for short periods, and at high interest—often ten percent a year or more. High interest is the price of distrust. Ultimately, taxpayers paid it.

### How Debt Became Reliable

After 1688, the picture changed. Money for war was now requested not by the king, but by a government accountable to Parliament. New loans were secured by specific taxes—customs duties and excises on salt, beer, paper, and windows—and Parliament stipulated that revenue from a given tax would go to pay interest. The Bank of England, founded in 1694, became a permanent intermediary: it accepted subscriptions to loans, kept lists of holders, and paid them interest. In 1751, numerous disparate loans were consolidated into a single security—the consolidated annuity, or “consol”—yielding three percent. A consol could be bought and sold any day, while the state was not obliged to repay the principal—it could redeem the debt at par whenever it wished—but only to pay interest in perpetuity. And throughout the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, it paid without fail.

The result can be seen in the price of money. In the 1690s, the government borrowed at eight to ten percent and higher; by the mid-eighteenth century, at around three percent. Britain could borrow more, and more cheaply, than any rival. In a famous 1989 article, economist Douglass North and political scientist Barry Weingast explained this through “credible commitment”: when a monarch’s power is constrained by a parliament in which the creditors themselves sit, the promise to pay becomes believable and credit grows cheaper. As early as 1967, historian Peter Dickson called the changes of 1688–1756 the “Financial Revolution.”

In fairness, this tidy account is disputed. Gregory Clark, studying yields on private loans and land rents, found no sharp post-1688 leap in the security of private property—private rates had been low even before the revolution. Nathan Sussman and Yishay Yafeh showed that government rates remained high for several decades after the Glorious Revolution and fell noticeably only in the 1720s–1740s, once the new order had proved durable. Trust, it seems, does not arise on the day a bill is signed; it is earned through years of punctual payment.

### Coin, Paper, and Bubbles

The money in people’s pockets also needed order. By the 1690s, England’s silver currency was in a deplorable state: old, hand-struck coins were clipped around the edges, and discs in circulation often weighed far less than they should. In 1696, the government carried out the Great Recoinage: it withdrew the old currency and issued new, machine-struck coins with milled edges that made clipping immediately visible. In the debate over whether to retain the old weight of the coin or reduce it, the philosopher John Locke prevailed: the weight was retained. The recoinage was painful—money became scarce for a time—but restored trust in the currency. Isaac Newton, who joined the Mint in 1696 and oversaw the recoinage, became Master of the Mint in 1699; in 1717, following his report, the price of the gold guinea was fixed at twenty-one shillings. At that price gold became more advantageous than silver, silver gradually left circulation, and Britain moved *de facto* onto the gold standard—roughly a century before it was enshrined in law.

Paper grew alongside coin. The Bank of England issued banknotes—receipts that could be exchanged for coin and therefore circulated as money. Merchants paid with bills of exchange. In the provinces during the second half of the eighteenth century, hundreds of “country banks” appeared—small firms that accepted deposits from local landowners and discounted the bills of merchants and manufacturers. A new industrialist rarely obtained large long-term loans: eighteenth-century factories were built chiefly with the founder’s own money, funds from relatives and partners, and profits reinvested in the business. But banks and bills supplied short-term credit—for raw materials and wages until goods were sold—and this lubricated the entire machinery of exchange.

Paper could deceive as well. In 1720, France and England experienced the first great stock-market bubbles almost simultaneously. In France, the Scotsman John Law, granted by the regent the right to issue paper money and manage the Mississippi Company, inflated the price of its shares many times over; then everything collapsed—and trust in paper money collapsed with the shares. In England, the South Sea Company, which undertook to exchange public debt for its shares, drove their price up roughly eight- to tenfold in six months. Dubious fly-by-night companies multiplied around it. The bubble burst in the autumn, ruining thousands; according to contemporary accounts, Newton himself lost a large sum.

The consequences of the two crashes differed, and the contrast is instructive. In France, Law’s failure created a lasting aversion to paper money and a central bank: the Bank of France did not appear until 1800. In England, the ruin was severe, but the Bank of England and public credit survived. The English did overreact, however: the Bubble Act of 1720 made it difficult for a century to create joint-stock companies without special permission. And from 1714, the usury law prohibited charging more than five percent a year on private loans.

### Where the Surplus Went

The Financial Revolution meant that Britain learned to gather the surplus of the entire nation—the savings of widows, merchants, landowners, and Dutch investors—and direct it wherever the government decided. Most often, it decided on war. Historian John Brewer called eighteenth-century Britain a “fiscal-military state”: taxes per capita were higher there than in France, excise collectors were professional and honest by the standards of the age, and the lion’s share of the budget went to the navy, army, and interest on war debts. By the end of the Napoleonic Wars, public debt, by common estimates, exceeded two years of national income.

Did this debt take money away from factories? Economist Jeffrey Williamson believed it did: the state “crowded out” private borrowers, and during wartime industry grew more slowly than it otherwise might have. In *Prometheus Shackled*, Peter Temin and Hans-Joachim Voth added that with a five-percent ceiling and wartime yields on government securities, private lending became almost unprofitable. Other historians object: factories lived chiefly on profits rather than loans anyway, so the crowding-out effect was modest.

The answer is dual. The new financial system was a developmental breakthrough: trust in promises, low interest rates, securities that could be sold any day, and reliable coin. All reduced the cost of exchange for everyone and later became the foundation for canals, railways, and factories. But war was the system’s first and principal customer, and a considerable share of the surplus it gathered went into cannon and ships. The difference from the Spain of Philip II was not that Britain fought less—if anything, it fought no less. The difference was that it paid its debts, did not debase its coin, and did not rob its creditors. The mechanism created for war therefore continued to operate in peacetime—and surplus gathered through trust could one day flow into something other than destruction.

## Power and Institutions

In 1610, King James I, grandfather of the James II who would flee the country in 1688, issued several proclamations—royal decrees—prohibiting the construction of new houses in London and the making of starch from wheat. Edward Coke, Chief Justice of the Court of Common Pleas, and his fellow judges declared that the king could not create new crimes by proclamation: the law of the land could be changed only through Parliament. James was furious—he sincerely believed his power came from God. But Coke’s formula remained in English law: the king stood beneath the law, not above it. The next eighty years contained a dispute over who was right—with civil war, the execution of Charles I in 1649, Cromwell’s republic, the restoration of the monarchy, and finally the Glorious Revolution.

For the economy, three things emerged from this protracted dispute. The first was the rule that taxes were imposed not by the king but by Parliament. The second was the idea that a subject’s property could not be taken by command, but only according to law and through a court. The third was independent judges: under the Act of Settlement of 1701—a provision that took effect in 1714—a judge could not be dismissed at the crown’s pleasure, only by a decision of both houses of Parliament. For a merchant investing in a ship, or a mine owner hiring Newcomen, this meant something simple: tomorrow a royal official would not arrive and announce that henceforth the business belonged to a favorite.

### Monopolies and Patents

One of the principal causes of conflict between crown and Parliament was purely economic. The Tudors and early Stuarts granted courtiers monopolies—exclusive rights to trade in salt, playing cards, soap, and glass. The monopolist raised prices, while the treasury and favorites divided the income. This was rent in its purest form: money was paid not because someone had done something better, but because he possessed a paper bearing the royal seal. In 1624, Parliament passed the Statute of Monopolies. It declared monopolies illegal—with one exception: whoever first introduced a “new invention” into the kingdom could receive an exclusive right to it for up to fourteen years.

Thus the patent for invention entered English law. The idea was not entirely new—Venice had granted similar privileges under a law as early as 1474—but in England it became a rule rather than a sovereign’s favor. In *Inventing the Industrial Revolution*, historian Christine MacLeod showed that the patent system worked poorly for a long time: obtaining one was expensive and required passing through several offices, and until the mid-eighteenth century usually fewer than ten patents were granted each year. Their number rose noticeably only from the 1760s onward.

Whether patents promoted progress is one of the fiercest debates in economic history. Defenders say that a patent promises the inventor a reward and in return compels him to describe the invention publicly, so the knowledge becomes common property when the term expires. Critics, such as economists Michele Boldrin and David Levine in *Against Intellectual Monopoly*, cite Watt himself. In 1775, he persuaded Parliament to extend his patent until 1800, then used it to sue competitors and fend off those proposing alternative designs. In their view, a quarter-century monopoly slowed development of the steam engine; improvements accelerated after 1800. Mokyr takes a middle position: most inventors of the era never patented their discoveries at all, but were driven by prizes, reputation, commissions, and the desire for fame among fellow enthusiasts of science. The honest conclusion is that a patent was better than a court monopoly because it rewarded novelty rather than proximity to the throne. But it, too, could become rent—the boundary between a reward for invention and a right to exact tribute from others lies not where the law draws it, but where the benefit ends.

### Who Sat in Parliament

It would be a mistake to imagine the eighteenth-century Parliament as a democracy. Only a small minority of adult men could vote—less than a fifth by most estimates—seats in the House of Commons were bought and inherited, and “rotten boroughs,” districts with almost no inhabitants, sent members just like great cities. This was an assembly of landowners, merchants, and financiers. They wrote laws in their own interests: the enclosures discussed above were enacted through private acts of Parliament, while the voice of the person losing access to common grazing was scarcely heard. The number of crimes punishable by death grew to roughly two hundred during the eighteenth century, many involving offenses against property—even the theft of a deer from a lord’s park.

Why, then, did such a parliament prove more useful for development than a royal court? The answer proposed by Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson in their 2005 article on Atlantic trade is that revenues from overseas trade enriched a broad stratum of merchants in England and the Netherlands who did not depend on the crown, and this stratum became strong enough to demand constraints on royal power. In Spain and Portugal, the same revenues went directly to the monarch’s treasury and strengthened his arbitrary power. An elite that invests in trade and production has an interest in rules protecting investment; an elite that lives on taxes and royal gifts has an interest in discretion. This did not make the English elite just, but its interests partly coincided with those of everyone who built something.

It also had purely rent-seeking habits. Beginning in 1651, the Navigation Acts required goods to be carried to England and its colonies on English ships. Under pressure from woolen manufacturers, the Calico Acts of 1700 and 1721 first prohibited imports of printed Indian textiles and then the wearing of calico altogether. This was classic protectionism benefiting one industry at the expense of consumers. The irony is that the ban on Indian calico cleared the market for domestic imitations, and half a century later cotton, not wool, would become the leading industry of English factories. A dual case: rent-seeking that produced a developmental side effect—but only because coal, craftsmen, and machines were available alongside it.

### Toleration and Expulsion

Another decision of government proved no less important to the economy than taxation. In 1685, Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes, which for nearly a century had guaranteed French Protestants freedom of worship. Huguenots were forced to convert to Catholicism, and their churches were demolished. By common estimates, between one hundred and fifty and two hundred thousand people fled—to Holland, England, Prussia, and Switzerland. Craftsmen, merchants, silk weavers, watchmakers, and printers were disproportionately numerous among them. London’s Spitalfields district became a center of silk weaving largely because of them; the Elector of Brandenburg issued a special edict inviting them to settle in Berlin. With its own hands, France expelled part of its human capital to its rivals—a model case of destruction committed for religious uniformity. Historian Warren Scoville did show that the damage to France as a whole was smaller than legend suggests: particular cities and crafts suffered, not the entire economy. The gain for receiving countries, however, is visible in the figures: economist Erik Hornung calculated that Prussian manufactories in places settled by Huguenots still operated at noticeably higher productivity a century later.

England was no model of toleration. After 1689, the Toleration Act allowed Protestant dissenters outside the Church of England—Quakers, Baptists, and Presbyterians—to worship freely, but they were still barred from public office and from universities, at least from taking degrees. Catholics faced still harsher restrictions. And here something unexpected happened. Shut out of careers as officials and clergy, dissenters entered trade, banking, and industry and created their own educational institutions—“dissenting academies”—where mathematics, natural science, and modern languages were taught alongside theology. Abraham Darby was a Quaker; so were the founders of banks that later grew into Barclays and Lloyds. Dissenters appeared among the engineers and manufacturers of the next generation far more often than their share of the population would suggest. Denial of privilege channeled their abilities into fields where permission from above was unnecessary.

### Institutions of Knowledge

Finally, government in this era began to support knowledge as a permanent occupation rather than the whim of an individual patron. The Royal Society received a charter, although it saw almost no money from the crown and lived on members’ subscriptions. In France, Minister Colbert established the Academy of Sciences in 1666; its members received royal salaries and carried out commissions for the state. The Paris Observatory (1667) and Greenwich Observatory (1675) were created for navigation. The scholars of a Europe divided by war corresponded, exchanged books and journals, and argued across borders. This community was called the “Republic of Letters,” and its rules differed from those of courts: authority was earned through publication and verification, not birth.

In *A Culture of Growth*, Mokyr sees this as one of the keys to the entire era. Europe was fragmented into dozens of states, and a thinker persecuted in one could move to another. Descartes lived in Holland for around twenty years, most of his adult life; Locke, exiled there as well, completed *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*. No ruler could ban an idea across the entire continent as an emperor could in a unified China, discussed in Chapter 6. Political fragmentation, which had fed wars for so many centuries, simultaneously created a market for ideas with competing patrons.

Where, then, did the surplus of power—its ability to gather and direct resources—go? In England after 1688, a substantial share still went to war and to supporting the privileges of a narrow circle of property owners. But the rules changed in one crucial respect: extraction became predictable and constrained by law, while creating something new increasingly brought the reward once conferred by proximity to the throne. This was not justice. It was protection from arbitrary power—and, as the experience of previous chapters shows, that alone was enough for a noticeable share of surplus to begin settling not in palaces, but in workshops.

## What Fostered Development

In 1759, engineer John Smeaton, the son of a Yorkshire lawyer and a former maker of scientific instruments, presented a paper on waterwheels to the Royal Society. People had built mill wheels for more than two thousand years, and every miller knew how to install one—by custom, his father’s advice, and eye. Smeaton proceeded differently. For several years, he built small model wheels in his workshop, fed them water from a tank with a known head, and measured what load each wheel could lift in a given time. He discovered that, all else being equal, a wheel struck by water from above produced roughly twice as much work as one pushed by water from below. The Society awarded him the Copley Medal, its highest honor, and millers and manufacturers across the country began rebuilding their wheels. Smeaton himself began calling himself a “civil engineer,” in contrast to a military one, and in 1771 assembled the first society of people in this new profession.

This episode reveals the era’s principal developmental discovery. Not a single invention, but a method for producing inventions—turning the question “what works best?” from a matter of taste and custom into one of experiment and measurement. Let us separate this method into its parts.

### Knowledge That Is Easy to Obtain

Joel Mokyr suggests thinking of knowledge as a commodity with a cost of access. If a craft secret is kept in one master’s head and passed only to his son, the cost of access is enormous: to learn it, one must be born into the right family. If it is described in a book available in any shop, the cost falls almost to zero. Nearly everything discussed in the preceding sections—journals, correspondence within the “Republic of Letters,” public lectures, the publication of patents—reduced that cost.

In 1695, the English Parliament did not renew the Licensing Act, effectively ending prior censorship of books and newspapers in the country. London’s first daily newspaper appeared in 1702. In France between 1751 and 1772, Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d’Alembert published the *Encyclopédie*—seventeen volumes of text and eleven volumes of engravings showing in meticulous, step-by-step detail the workshops of the pin maker, glassblower, weaver, and printer. What had been a guild secret for centuries became a picture any literate person could examine. And literacy was growing: Roger Schofield’s estimates, based on signatures in parish marriage registers, suggest that by the mid-eighteenth century around sixty percent of English men and between one-third and two-fifths of women could sign their names. This is a rough measure—a signature is not yet reading—but the trend is clear.

Knowledge has a property grain and gold do not: it can be given away without being lost. Bread eaten by one person cannot feed another, while Smeaton’s table is equally useful to a thousand millers. Every reduction in the cost of access therefore multiplied the return from a discovery already made. This is the very mechanism we called the accumulation and transmission of knowledge in the introduction—but for the first time it operated not at the scale of a monastery or guild, but of a continent.

### The Bridge Between Scholar and Craftsman

Beginning in the mid-1760s, Birmingham’s Lunar Society met once a month on the Monday nearest the full moon, so there would be light for the journey home. Around one table sat the industrialist Matthew Boulton, James Watt, pottery manufacturer Josiah Wedgwood, the physician and poet Erasmus Darwin—Charles’s grandfather—and the chemist Joseph Priestley. Historian Jenny Uglow described this circle in *The Lunar Men*. They discussed everything at once: gases and glazes, canals and steam pumps, electricity and the education of children. Wedgwood, who struggled with kilns that alternately overheated and underfired his wares, invented the pyrometer—an instrument for measuring very high temperatures through the shrinkage of clay bars—and was elected to the Royal Society in 1783.

Similar circles arose across Britain—in Manchester, Edinburgh, and Newcastle—while in London the Society for the Encouragement of Arts, Manufactures and Commerce, founded in 1754, offered prizes for specific improvements: a new dye, a new plow, a way to preserve grain. This extended the principle seen in the longitude prize: a reward for solving a problem, not for proximity to power.

Here we must acknowledge a debate honestly. Many historians—Robert Allen, and to some extent Kelly, Mokyr, and Ó Gráda themselves—emphasize that most eighteenth-century inventions were made by practitioners rather than scientists: Newcomen, Darby, and the weavers and clockmakers who devised spinning machines. Science in the narrow sense, with laws and formulas, did not often help industry directly; its principal fruits—dye chemistry and electrical engineering—would come only in the nineteenth century. Mokyr does not dispute this. His argument is different: what mattered was not so much scientific theory as the scientific habit—measuring, comparing, recording, and publishing. It was adopted even by those who had never read Newton. Smeaton with his model wheels is precisely such a case.

### Roads, Canals, and Insurance

The third mechanism was whatever reduced the costs of exchange, the fifth of our measures. In the eighteenth century, hundreds of turnpike trusts appeared in England—associations of local landowners and merchants that, with Parliament’s authorization, took charge of a stretch of road, repaired and paved it, and collected tolls at gates. A road that had previously belonged to everyone and was therefore properly maintained by no one acquired an owner with a stake in its condition. Over the course of the century, travel times between major cities fell severalfold.

In 1761, the Duke of Bridgewater’s canal opened, built by the self-taught engineer James Brindley from the duke’s coal mines at Worsley to Manchester. Contemporary accounts suggest that the price of coal in Manchester fell by roughly half as a result. A single horse towing a barge through calm water could move dozens of times more freight than the same horse pulling a cart along a rutted road. Over the next half-century, a network of canals spread across England, carrying coal, clay, grain, and iron to where they were needed most.

The price of risk was falling as well. After the Great Fire of London in 1666 destroyed most of the City, the first fire-insurance offices appeared in the 1680s. At Edward Lloyd’s coffeehouse, documented from 1688 onward, shipowners and merchants arranged insurance for ships and cargoes—from it would grow the famous Lloyd’s. Insurance is an inconspicuous but powerful developmental technology: it makes it possible to undertake a risky venture without staking one’s entire property. In this respect it is akin to the Poor Law discussed above—only for the merchant rather than the pauper.

### Lives That Grew Longer

The final mechanism concerned people themselves. In 1721, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, the wife of the British ambassador in Constantinople, had seen the local practice of inoculating healthy people with matter from the pustules of someone suffering a mild case of smallpox. In London, in the presence of royal physicians, she had her daughter inoculated. The practice itself was old and had come from Asia and Africa, but in England it began to be tested: physicians collected evidence and compared mortality among the inoculated with mortality among those infected naturally, and by the end of the century inoculation had become commonplace. In 1796, the country doctor Edward Jenner took the next step—vaccination with cowpox, which was much safer. The pattern was the same: knowledge from elsewhere, tested with numbers and published, became common property.

### Where the Surplus Went

Put the mechanisms together. Knowledge became cheaper to obtain, scholars and craftspeople began talking to one another, transport and risk grew less expensive, and rewards increasingly went to whoever solved a problem. None of these mechanisms was entirely new: writing, roads, marine insurance, and prizes had existed before—in Mesopotamia, Rome, China, and Venice. What was new was that they came together at the same time and reinforced one another. Cheap coal made steam pumps profitable; pumps required precise cylinders; cylinders required skilled craftspeople and machine tools; machine tools required new knowledge of metal; and canals carried coal more cheaply still.

This is the answer to the chapter’s question—at least on its developmental side. A noticeable share of the surplus that had once gone into population growth, palaces, and wars now flowed into things of a special kind—things that themselves produced new surplus: knowledge that does not wear out, canals that serve for centuries, craftspeople who train apprentices. But a qualification is needed at once: this share was still modest. As we have seen, most of the money collected by the British state went to the navy and army, while a considerable part of landowners’ incomes went to estates and parks. Development overtook destruction not because destruction had become slight, but because developmental investments for the first time began to yield returns comparable to it—and to multiply themselves.

## What Destroyed

In late November 1781, the Liverpool slave ship *Zong* was sailing from the coast of West Africa to Jamaica. According to documents from the subsequent court case, more than four hundred people had been crammed into its holds—twice as many as a ship of that size normally carried. Captain Luke Collingwood made an error in his reckoning and sailed past Jamaica; people in the holds were falling ill and dying; and, as the crew later claimed, little water remained. The captain then made what he regarded as a business decision. Under the rules of marine insurance at the time, the death of “cargo” from disease was the owner’s loss, while cargo thrown overboard to save the ship was covered by the insurer. Over several days, the crew threw more than 130 living Africans into the sea.

In 1783, the ship’s owners went to court—not as defendants, but as plaintiffs: they demanded payment from the insurers for those who had died. The jury initially found in their favor; Lord Chief Justice Mansfield then ordered a retrial, but not because the case involved murder—because he doubted that the ship had been short of water. No member of the crew was ever tried. The abolitionist Granville Sharp and the formerly enslaved Olaudah Equiano learned of the case, and it became one of the catalysts for the British movement to abolish the slave trade.

We have just called insurance a developmental technology—and it was. The story of the *Zong* shows that the same technology, the same courts, contracts, and account books that reduced the costs of honest exchange reduced the costs of trafficking in human beings just as efficiently. The institutions of the eighteenth century were open within a narrow circle and merciless beyond it. This era’s destruction cannot be understood by looking only at London and Manchester.

### The Atlantic Machine at Its Zenith

In the previous chapter, we saw how the plantation system took shape. In the eighteenth century it reached its zenith. According to the Slave Voyages database, roughly half of all Africans ever carried across the Atlantic were transported during this century alone. Over the entire history of the trade, British ships carried about three million people, according to the same database, and most of those voyages took place in the eighteenth century. Liverpool, still a modest port in 1700, had become Europe’s leading slave-trading harbor by the end of the century.

Destruction proceeded along several lines at once. People were destroyed: during the ocean crossing, the so-called Middle Passage, an average of one in seven died according to the same database, and by the end of the century roughly one in ten; mortality on the Caribbean sugar plantations was so high that the enslaved population could be maintained only through fresh arrivals. Trust within Africa itself was destroyed: the slave trade turned neighboring communities into hunters and prey. In a 2008 paper, the economist Nathan Nunn showed that the regions of Africa from which the most people had been taken are, on average, poorer even today; in a 2011 study with Leonard Wantchekon, he showed that their inhabitants still place markedly less trust in neighbors and even relatives. These are statistical estimates and remain contested, but the direction of the conclusion is difficult to dispute: trade built on abduction destroyed our fifth measure—the capacity of strangers to trust one another.

How much of Britain’s prosperity was paid for by this destruction? In his 1944 book *Capitalism and Slavery*, the Trinidadian historian Eric Williams argued that profits from the slave trade and the plantations provided one source of capital for the Industrial Revolution. The claim has been much debated. Stanley Engerman and Patrick O’Brien calculated that profits from the slave trade made up only a small share of all British investment at the time—not enough to build factories on. Joseph Inikori countered that profits were not the point; markets were. The Atlantic system—African buyers of cloth and iron, and planters who needed tools and clothing for enslaved workers—created the demand on which English workshops grew. Many historians today reach a cautious conclusion: without slavery, the Industrial Revolution would probably still have happened, but it would have unfolded differently. For our question, something else matters more. The surplus created on the plantations did not belong to those who created it. It went to owners, merchants, and the treasury—and some of it became Liverpool docks and Bristol mansions. In one place on the map it looked like development; in another, it was pure extraction.

### A Company That Became a State

On the other side of the world, a mechanism born of the same joint-stock companies discussed in the previous chapter was at work. On June 23, 1757, the forces of the British East India Company under Robert Clive defeated the Nawab of Bengal’s army at Plassey—largely because some of his commanders had been bribed in advance. In 1765, the Company received the *diwani* from the Mughal emperor—the right to collect taxes in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa, among the richest and most populous regions in the world.

This transformation of merchant into tax collector deserves a close look. The Company had previously bought Indian textiles with silver brought from Europe. Now it could buy them with money collected from the Bengalis themselves. The goods went to London, while almost nothing returned to Bengal in exchange. Company servants, modestly salaried, traded privately under the protection of its power and returned home with immense fortunes. In England they were nicknamed “nabobs.”

In 1769–1770, drought struck Bengal, followed by famine. The Company did not reduce the land revenue; contemporary accounts suggest that tax receipts scarcely fell during those years. No one knows exactly how many people died. Warren Hastings, soon to become governor-general, wrote of one-third of the population; modern historians regard that figure as unreliable, and their estimates range from one or two million to ten million deaths, depending on how they estimate Bengal’s population before the famine and the contraction in cultivated acreage. The Company did not cause the drought. But a power that collected taxes without answering to those who paid them turned a failed harvest into a catastrophe. The famine provoked a scandal, parliamentary inquiries, and the Regulating Act of 1773, which placed the Company under partial government control. The destruction was noticed—but it was noticed in London and discussed above all as a question of British politics and British money.

Here we see an unpleasant symmetry with the story of the Glorious Revolution. English landowners had ensured that the king could not take their money without their consent. Bengali peasants had no such parliament, and to them the Company was precisely the kind of arbitrary extraction from which the English had protected themselves in 1689.

### Wars That Fed on Debt

Let us return to Europe. Between 1689 and 1815, England and France spent scarcely fewer years at war with each other than at peace: the Nine Years’ War, the wars of the Spanish and Austrian Succession, the Seven Years’ War, the American War of Independence—and each cost more than the last. Eighteenth-century armies were more disciplined than those of the Thirty Years’ War and pillaged indiscriminately less often, but destruction could also be methodical. In the winter of 1688–1689, French troops retreating from the Palatinate burned towns and villages on orders from Versailles to leave the enemy a wasteland: Heidelberg, Mannheim, Speyer, and Worms suffered. This was not soldiers running riot, but military policy—the destruction of another country’s capital as a means of defense.

Debt proved the costliest destruction of all. As we have seen, Britain knew how to borrow cheaply and honor its obligations; France did not. Its treasury borrowed at higher interest, tax farmers collected a substantial share of its taxes and kept a handsome portion for themselves, and the nobility and clergy were exempt from many levies. Every war added to the debt, and by 1788, according to widely cited estimates, roughly half the French crown’s expenditure went to interest payments. The king could not raise taxes without the consent of the estates, so he was forced to summon the Estates-General—for the first time since 1614. What followed in 1789 is well known. Debt was not the Revolution’s only cause; beside it stood the failed harvest of 1788 and expensive bread. But it was debt that forced the king to call the estates. A monarchy that, unlike the English one, had failed to bind itself with rules ultimately lost everything. The wars destroyed not only soldiers and cities but the very regime that waged them—and Europe would pay for this with new wars over the next quarter-century.

### An Internal Colony

Finally, there was destruction carried out by laws rather than cannon. After William of Orange’s victory at the River Boyne in 1690, Ireland came under the rule of a Protestant minority. A series of so-called Penal Laws restricted Catholics—the majority of the Irish population—in their right to buy land, take long leases, receive an education, hold office, and bear arms. According to widely cited estimates, by the end of the eighteenth century Catholics owned only a small fraction of the island’s land—a few percent. The Wool Act of 1699, passed under pressure from English clothmakers, forbade the export of Irish woolens anywhere except England, where they faced tariffs.

These were the same instruments we encountered in the section on power: property rights and protectionism. But here they were directed not toward protecting investment, but toward protecting against competition and entrenching someone else’s rents. A person who can neither buy land nor sell his goods loses the incentive to invest in either. The surplus of the Irish countryside flowed out as rent to landowners, many of whom lived in England—and this prepared the poverty that would culminate in the Great Famine in the mid-nineteenth century.

### Where the Surplus Went

Let us sum up. The destruction of this era did not resemble the sack of Rome by barbarians or the devastation of Europe by the Black Death. It was organized, lawful, and sometimes insured. It was carried out by the same joint-stock companies, the same courts, and the same parliaments that appeared as heroes in the developmental part of our story. The difference lay not in the instruments, but in whom their rules covered.

This leads to a conclusion that will prove useful more than once. Institutions that protect against arbitrary extraction protect first and foremost those represented within them. The wider that circle, the more people can invest with confidence that they will keep the fruits. The narrower it is, the easier it becomes for the same state and the same companies to turn neighbors, colonies, and their own peripheries into sources of extraction. In eighteenth-century England, the circle expanded slowly—from the king to a parliament of landowners, then to merchants and manufacturers. Outside it remained the English poor, Irish peasants, Bengali weavers, and Africans in the holds of the *Zong*. Part of the surplus on which the new economy grew was created by their labor and taken from them by force. An honest answer to the chapter’s question must include this side of the ledger as well.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

In Dorset, in southern England, stands the village of Milton Abbas: two rows of identical whitewashed, thatched cottages lining a broad street, with lawns and chestnut trees between them. It is often photographed as the model of an old English village. In fact, it is younger than many London streets. In 1752, the wealthy landowner Joseph Damer, later Lord Milton, bought the local estate with its ancient abbey; beside the abbey stood a genuine market town, with breweries, shops, a school, and several hundred inhabitants. The town spoiled the view from his windows. During the 1770s and 1780s, Damer gradually bought the houses, waited for their leases to expire, and demolished them; according to local historians, one obstinate owner was driven out when construction of a pond flooded his house. The residents were resettled in a neat new village hidden in a valley beyond the hill, and a park was laid out where the town had stood. Lancelot “Capability” Brown, the most fashionable landscape gardener of the age, was involved in the work on both park and village.

Here is wasteful spending in its purest form. Nothing was built for production, nothing was invented; immense labor—demolishing, filling in, digging ponds, planting trees—was spent so that the owner could look from his window upon hills unmarked by anyone else’s life. Destruction was involved as well, however: the town, with its crafts and market, disappeared.

### Palaces and Parks

Milton Abbas was an extreme case, but not an exception. In England, the eighteenth century was the age of the country palace. The greatest of them, Blenheim Palace in Oxfordshire, was begun in 1705 as a gift from the nation to the Duke of Marlborough for his victory over the French and Bavarians at Blenheim. Queen Anne granted the land, the Treasury promised to pay, and the architect John Vanbrugh conceived not a house but a monument: nearly two hundred rooms, ceremonial courtyards, and colonnades. Treasury funds stopped around 1712, when the duke fell from favor; construction dragged on intermittently for another decade, while Duchess Sarah sued the contractors and quarreled so bitterly with the architect that, according to a well-known account, Vanbrugh was refused admission in 1725 when he came to see his own finished creation. The palace cost hundreds of thousands of pounds—an immense sum by the standards of the time.

Smaller but still lavish were Prime Minister Robert Walpole’s Houghton Hall, Stowe, and Kedleston. Around them, Brown and his imitators created “natural” landscapes: digging lakes, raising hills, transplanting mature trees, and demolishing hedges and villages if they obstructed the view. On the Continent, every German prince built his own little Versailles: Ludwigsburg in Württemberg, the Würzburg Residence, Frederick II’s Sanssouci in Potsdam.

An honest qualification is needed here—the same one we made for pyramids and cathedrals. Palaces gave work to stonemasons, carpenters, plasterers, and gardeners; skills honed on them flowed into ordinary construction; some of the collections brought together in these houses later became the foundations of public museums. Many of them now earn income from tourism. But by our measures, none of these considerations makes a palace a developmental investment: output per hour of labor did not rise because of it, knowledge did not accumulate, and the broad majority did not come to live better. It was a transfer of surplus from the countryside to a narrow circle, transformed into stone and lawn.

### Sinecures and Elections

The second channel was what contemporaries called “influence” and the next generation, following the polemicist William Cobbett, dubbed “Old Corruption.” The word *sinecure* comes from the Latin for “without care”: it meant a post that brought an income but required no work. The eighteenth-century British state was stuffed with them. The prime minister’s son Horace Walpole, a writer and connoisseur of art, received several Treasury posts through his father while still a young man; hired deputies performed the duties, and he lived on the income for the rest of his life. Such positions were distributed to relatives, supporters in Parliament, and their sons and widows.

This was rent in the sense defined in the introduction: income received for possessing a right rather than creating value. The mechanism was simple. A minister needed a majority in the House of Commons; the majority was bought with offices and pensions; members of Parliament, in turn, spent money to enter the House. The Oxfordshire election of 1754 became legendary: both parties spent enormous sums on bribes, feasts, and transporting voters, while William Hogarth painted *The Humours of an Election*, a series that portrayed drunken voters, bribery, and brawls with merciless humor. Money spent on such a campaign decided only one thing: whose clients would receive the next round of sinecures.

But here we can also see what distinguished this era from many that preceded it. Wasteful spending began to be measured and publicly debated. In 1780, Edmund Burke delivered a major speech in Parliament on “economical reform,” demanding the abolition of useless court and government posts, and in 1782 some of them were indeed eliminated. The reform was modest; it took another half-century to root out the sinecures. But the fact itself matters: the open Parliament and free press discussed above made it possible to call waste by its name. Under an absolute monarchy, such a conversation was almost unthinkable.

### Servants, Soldiers, and Lawyers

Most interestingly, in this era the attempt to draw a line between useful and useless expenditure became a subject of scholarship. In 1776, the Scottish professor Adam Smith published *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*—the book conventionally taken as the starting point of economic theory. In its second part, he divided all labor into “productive” and “unproductive.” Productive labor was that of a craftsperson or manufacturing worker: it was embodied in an object that could be sold, with the proceeds then used to hire another worker. Unproductive labor disappeared at the moment it was performed; such, in Smith’s view, was the labor of a domestic servant. A man grows rich by employing many artisans, Smith reasoned, and poor by maintaining many servants.

Smith then ventured a claim that sounds harsh even today. Among the unproductive he placed not only footmen, but also the sovereign and all public officials, the army and navy, as well as priests, lawyers, physicians, musicians, and actors. He did not call them useless: defense, justice, and medical care are necessary to society. His point was different. All are supported from the surplus created by productive labor, and return nothing to it that can be put to work again. A country’s wealth therefore depends, Smith argued, on what share of its annual product supports productive hands and what share supports unproductive ones.

The reader has probably noticed how close this is to the question of our book. In his own way, Smith was asking the same thing: where does the surplus go—where it will grow, or where it will simply be consumed? The picture in his own time gave cause for concern: domestic servants were one of the largest groups of working people in London, while military expenditure and interest on war debts, as we have seen, consumed the lion’s share of the state budget.

But Smith’s division also reveals its weakness, and this should be acknowledged honestly. Later economists abandoned it: a physician who prolongs a craftsperson’s life, or a teacher who teaches his child to read, creates no less than a weaver, even though the result of that labor cannot be placed in a warehouse. In our framework, the boundary lies elsewhere: not between goods and services, but between what increases society’s capacity to produce, know, and live better, and what merely sustains someone’s status. By this measure, a physician and a teacher are more likely developmental, while a Treasury sinecure and a liveried footman at the entrance to Blenheim are wasteful. Smith drew the boundary imprecisely, but he was among the first to attempt it systematically.

### The Navy: A Double-Edged Case Once Again

Britain’s largest item of expenditure—the navy—deserves separate mention. We examined the arms race in the previous chapter, and in the eighteenth century it continued at sea: ships of the line grew ever larger, and building one great ship such as *Victory*, launched in 1765, consumed thousands of trees, most of them oaks. For the navy itself, this was a contest in which a rival’s strength diminished the value of one’s own—a classic wasteful race.

Yet it left a developmental by-product that cannot be dismissed. The royal dockyards at Portsmouth, Plymouth, and Chatham were the country’s largest manufacturing enterprises, where thousands of workers labored to a common schedule long before the factories. The navy ordered copper sheathing for hulls, rope, anchors, chronometers, and charts; it needed provisions that would keep for months and remedies for scurvy. It also protected the trade routes without which there would have been neither Atlantic commerce nor cheap imports—historians Patrick O’Brien and Nicholas Rodger regard this protection as one of the preconditions of British growth. Even so, a substantial share of this expenditure remained spending for the sake of rivalry—but part of it became skills, organization, and knowledge that later served the peacetime economy.

### Where the Surplus Went

Add together the parks, palaces, sinecures, bought elections, armies of servants, and ships of the line, and it becomes clear that wasteful spending had by no means disappeared from eighteenth-century Britain. In absolute terms, it most likely even grew: the country now had something to spend. What distinguished it from Spain with its Escorial or France with its Versailles was not the virtue of the English lords, but proportion and feedback. Proportion: alongside waste arose a powerful stream of investment that yielded returns—canals, mines, workshops, knowledge. Feedback: waste could be seen, named, and, however slowly, cut back—through parliamentary criticism, through newspapers, through Smith’s book, which quickly ran through several editions.

This observation will be useful later as well. A society need not rid itself of wasteful spending in order to grow—no society in history has done so. It is enough for the developmental share of the surplus to grow larger and multiply itself more rapidly, and for the society to acquire tools that allow it to detect and reduce what is useless. In eighteenth-century England, the first process was only beginning; the second had appeared almost for the first time.

## The Lesson of the Era

Little more than a century passed between that evening at Gresham College and the first Watt engines in the mines of Cornwall. During that time England became neither just nor peaceful: it waged wars, trafficked in human beings, and demolished villages for the sake of a view. Yet it was here that average income first ceased returning to its former level after every increase in the number of mouths. Let us reduce the argument to five propositions.

**First: the way out of the Malthusian trap lies not through new land, but through new knowledge and new energy.** Every previous era had run up against the same ceiling—the acreage on which grain, hay, and timber could grow. Coal removed part of that ceiling: it supplied heat without taking hectares from the fields. But coal also lay beneath China and the Ruhr, while atmospheric pressure had been discovered by Italians. The decisive fact was that knowledge began to increase faster than people. Previously, every improvement had been consumed by additional mouths within one or two generations; now a new improvement arrived before the last one could be consumed. This is what escape from the trap consists of: not a single leap, but a stream of inventions that no longer ran dry.

**Second: protection from arbitrary power is cheaper than any treasury.** Eighteenth-century Britain was richer than France in neither land nor people. But it honored its debts, did not debase its coinage, and did not seize its subjects’ property by decree. Its credit therefore cost about three percent, while French credit was markedly more expensive—and this decided the outcome of more than one war. The same principle operated below: a mine owner who installed a Newcomen engine could expect to reap the fruits himself rather than surrender them to a royal favorite. But recall Sussman and Yafeh’s qualification: trust did not appear on the day the Bill of Rights was signed. It was earned through decades of punctual payments. Institutions cannot be switched on like a lamp; they must be cultivated, and they are far easier to destroy.

**Third: the breakthrough came from a combination, not a single cause.** Here we should gather the competing explanations encountered throughout the chapter and add those not yet mentioned. Robert Allen gives pride of place to prices: expensive labor and cheap coal made machinery profitable specifically in England, whereas the same inventions would not have paid in India or France. Joel Mokyr replies that prices explain why people searched, but not why they found: a culture of verifiable knowledge and a bridge between scholar and craftsperson were also needed. North, Weingast, Acemoglu, and Robinson point to institutions that protected investment. In *The Great Divergence* (2000), Kenneth Pomeranz showed that as late as the mid-eighteenth century, the richest regions of China, such as the Yangtze Delta, matched England by many measures, and that two strokes of luck decided the outcome: coal located close to cities, and colonies that gave Europe cotton, sugar, and timber—harvests from land it did not itself possess. In *Bourgeois Dignity* (2010), Deirdre McCloskey argues that the decisive change was in attitudes toward merchants and inventors: their occupations ceased to be despised, and gifted people were drawn toward pursuits that previously attracted only those with no other choice. In *A Farewell to Alms* (2007), Gregory Clark proposed the most controversial account: according to his data, more children survived in prosperous English families, and middle-class habits—thrift, literacy, patience—slowly spread through society. Each of these explanations faces powerful objections, and none explains everything. But together they fit well: coal and expensive labor provided the motive; a culture of knowledge, the method; institutions, confidence that the fruits would not be seized; colonies, additional resources; and a newfound respect for enterprise, people willing to take risks.

**Fourth: rules limiting extraction worked only within the circle.** The same courts, contracts, and insurance policies that protected a London merchant allowed people aboard the *Zong* to be thrown overboard and an insurance claim filed for them. The same Parliament that forbade the king to levy taxes without consent passed enclosure acts and Penal Laws against Irish Catholics. In the East India Company, a Bengali peasant confronted precisely the arbitrary power from which the English had protected themselves in 1689. The conclusion of this chapter extends beyond its era: the developmental power of institutions is proportional to the breadth of the circle they cover. Everything outside that circle becomes not a participant in growth, but a source of extraction.

**Fifth: a society need not eliminate wasteful spending in order to grow.** Blenheim, Milton Abbas, sinecures, and bought elections did not disappear—there were probably more of them than before. What changed was the proportion: alongside waste arose a stream of investments that multiplied themselves. Feedback changed as well: for the first time, waste was publicly called waste—in Burke’s speech, Hogarth’s engravings, and Smith’s book. A society capable of noticing useless expenditure has not yet rid itself of it, but it can already cut it back.

Now for the answer to our recurring question. Where did the surplus of this era go? If it could be counted, the greater part, as before, would have gone to war and interest on war debts, to palaces and parks, and to supporting a narrow circle. A substantial share was created in Africa, the Caribbean, and Bengal and extracted from those who created it. But for the first time, a noticeable share went into things that did not wear out and themselves generated new surplus: published knowledge, canals and roads, craftspeople and their apprentices, machines that replaced hands and muscle. And for the first time, this share yielded returns faster than the number of people grew. The proportion was modest, but it broke a balance thousands of years old.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. The “escape from the trap” is clearly visible only in hindsight: a contemporary of Malthus looking at the bread riots of 1795 might have seen no escape at all. Growth in income per person during the eighteenth century was very slow—fractions of a percent per year by most estimates—and historians still debate its magnitude. Many of the era’s fruits reached the broad majority only generations later, while the decades immediately ahead would bring child labor in factories and the slums of Manchester. Finally, we are telling the story of the victors: Holland, France, and the Yangtze Delta shared many of the same advantages, and chance probably played a larger role than any tidy theory would like.

How many people lived in England and neighboring countries, how income, wages, and life expectancy changed, and how far northwestern Europe had pulled ahead of the rest of the world by 1800—all this appears in the section of figures.

## The Numbers

The seventeenth and eighteenth centuries were the first era in which numbers began to be collected deliberately. William Petty and Gregory King counted England’s incomes and social orders, the Treasury kept accounts of the debt, and parishes recorded baptisms and burials. Yet even these figures, apart from the first census in 1801, remain reconstructions, and disagreements among historians are noted.

**Population.** According to Wrigley and Schofield, England had about five million inhabitants around 1700 and about eight and a half million by 1801. Growth began in the mid-eighteenth century and never stopped again. France had four times England’s population and three times that of Great Britain as a whole: just over twenty million people around 1700 and about twenty-eight million by 1789. London, which Wrigley estimates had already overtaken Paris around 1700, grew from roughly 570,000 inhabitants to 960,000 in the census of 1801 and remained Europe’s largest city. Nearly one English person in ten lived in the capital.

**Income per person.** In Angus Maddison’s estimates, Britain had an income of about 1,250 “1990 international dollars” per person in 1700 and about 1,700 in 1820. Over the same period, the Netherlands slipped from roughly 2,100 to 1,800: the former leader had yielded first place. France rose from about 900 to 1,100; China and India, according to Maddison, stood still at about 600 and 550. Britain’s growth rate was modest: in the revised calculations of Nicholas Crafts and Nick Harley, income per person increased by only about one-third of one percent per year during the eighteenth century. This was less than historians had believed a century ago—hence the dispute over whether the “revolution” was truly a revolution. The novelty lay elsewhere: the increase was no longer consumed by population growth. Stephen Broadberry and his coauthors confirm this picture with new evidence in *British Economic Growth, 1270–1870* (2015). Estimates for Asia remain the most contested: Pomeranz believes the Yangtze Delta kept pace with England until the mid-eighteenth century, while Broadberry, Guan, and Li (2018) argue that China as a whole had fallen behind earlier.

**Energy.** According to Michael Flinn’s calculations, British coal production rose from roughly three million tons around 1700 to fifteen million by 1800. Wrigley converted this into land: to obtain the same amount of heat from firewood by 1800 would have required planting between one-third and nearly half of all England with forest—more than its entire arable acreage. It was this “underground grove” that made it possible to feed and heat a growing population without running into the limit of land.

**Knowledge.** From signatures in marriage registers, Roger Schofield estimated that in the second half of the eighteenth century roughly six Englishmen in ten and between one-third and two-fifths of Englishwomen could sign their names. This was not full literacy, but it was very high for a preindustrial society. Fewer than ten patents a year were normally granted in the first half of the century; in the 1790s, according to Christine MacLeod and Patent Office summaries, the average exceeded sixty. Patent counts do not measure inventiveness directly—many innovations were never patented—but a severalfold increase in half a century speaks for itself.

**Debt and war.** Britain’s national debt was less than twenty million pounds in 1697, about 130 million after the Seven Years’ War, and about 240 million after the American War of Independence. According to John Brewer’s calculations, the army, navy, and interest on war loans usually consumed three-quarters or more of government expenditure during the eighteenth century. This is perhaps the best measure of where the surplus collected by the Treasury went: only a small share of it found its way into development. Yet loans cost about three percent a year—half as much as they had half a century earlier—and this, too, is a figure of the era.

**Length of life.** In Wrigley and Schofield’s reconstruction, life expectancy at birth in England fell to roughly thirty-two years during the worst decades of the late seventeenth century and had risen to 36–40 years by the beginning of the nineteenth, depending on the version of the reconstruction—Wrigley and Schofield’s 1981 work or Wrigley and his coauthors’ 1997 revision. The increase was small and unstable; its largest component came from declining epidemic mortality rather than wealth as such. Infant mortality remained enormous: roughly one child in six or seven did not survive the first year.

**People outside the circle.** According to the Slave Voyages database, about six and a half million people were taken from Africa during the eighteenth century—slightly more than half of all victims of the Atlantic slave trade over four centuries—and British ships carried more than those of any other country. This figure belongs alongside the rise in income and coal production: it was part of the same economy.

The chapter’s numerical conclusion is simple. By 1800, England was not yet an industrial country: most of its people worked on the land and in workshops, and average income grew more slowly than in any subsequent era. But for the first time, the population curve and the income curve were rising together. How this slow ascent became a collapse of the old world—with steam, cotton, factories, and children at the machines—is the story of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; errors by a factor of several are possible. The assumptions from previous chapters: a ten-hour day; a working life of around seventy thousand hours. In eighteenth-century England, labor was already bought for money, so I count in the days of a day laborer: by my estimate from Robert Allen’s wage series, he earned one to one and a half shillings a day, and more in London—this requires verification. A pound therefore equaled thirteen to twenty working days. For reference, a fifty-kilogram sack of wheat cost seven to nine shillings—this requires verification.

### Blenheim

**Input data.** The usual figure is around three hundred thousand pounds: according to the palace itself, the Treasury provided two hundred and forty thousand and the Marlboroughs added sixty thousand. Under pressure, Wren conceded that the building alone would cost two hundred and eighty-seven thousand.

**Labor, grain, lives.** That is four to six million working days, forty to sixty million hours—five hundred and fifty to eight hundred and fifty working lives, around seven hundred: roughly the same as Henry III’s Westminster Abbey. In grain, it is seven hundred to eight hundred and fifty thousand sacks, a year’s bread for more than one hundred thousand people.

**Alternative.** One appears in this same chapter. The Duke of Bridgewater spent, by different estimates, one hundred and seventy to two hundred and twenty thousand pounds on his canals, and the first of them halved the price of coal in Manchester. Blenheim equaled one and a half to two such canals.

### The Seven Years’ War

**Input data.** Britain’s debt rose from seventy-two to seventy-five million pounds in 1756 to one hundred and thirty to one hundred and thirty-six million in 1763—an increase of roughly sixty million. Together with taxes, the war cost, by my estimate, eighty to one hundred million—this requires verification—and interest on the new debt was then paid for decades.

**Labor, grain, lives.** Ninety million pounds is roughly one and a half billion working days, ten to twenty billion hours: one hundred and fifty to two hundred and ninety thousand working lives, around two hundred thousand, or two to three hundred Blenheims. In grain, it is two hundred to two hundred and fifty million sacks—by my estimate, enough bread for all of England for five to seven years. Literal lives require a separate accounting: according to a frequently cited summary from the period, of 185,000 naval sailors, around 1,500 were killed in battle, while more than 130,000 died of disease or deserted—this requires verification. I will not attempt to count soldiers or the inhabitants of devastated lands.

**Alternative.** The same money could have built four hundred to five hundred Bridgewater canals; as far as can be judged, Britain’s entire canal network over the following half-century cost several times less—this requires verification. The war brought Canada and consolidated Britain’s position in India—but we have seen what the latter cost Bengal.

**Conclusion.** For the first time, we can compare alternatives within a single country and a single generation. About three million working days for a canal—and coal became half as expensive. Five million for a palace—and it stands as a museum. One and a half billion for a war—and a debt that led to the loss of the American colonies twenty years later. Britain spent no less wastefully than Spain, but for the first time a cheap developmental sector was growing alongside the waste, and its returns compounded.
