# Chapter 3. Temple and Palace Economies

## Portrait of the Era

Around 3200 BCE in the city of Uruk, in what is now southern Iraq, someone took a lump of wet clay, smoothed it into a tablet the size of a palm, and pressed several signs into it with a reed stylus. The signs recorded the receipt of some twenty-nine thousand measures of barley over thirty-seven months, alongside a word that scholars read as “Kushim.” The tablet dried, lay in the earth for more than five thousand years, and is now held in a private collection. In *Sapiens*, Yuval Noah Harari called Kushim the first person in history whose name is known to us—or at least one of the first, since early Egyptian royal names also compete for that distinction. Historians debate whether it is a name at all: “Kushim” may have designated an office or institution. But in any event, something else is remarkable: the first words humanity considered worth recording were not a prayer, a poem, or a king's heroic deed, but an account of barley.

Uruk was then the largest city in the world. Archaeologists estimate that between twenty-five and fifty thousand people lived within its walls, and perhaps more by the beginning of the third millennium BCE, when the walls stretched for nearly ten kilometers. In a world where, just a few thousand years earlier, the largest village had housed only a few thousand people, this was a leap on the order of a town becoming a metropolis. At the city's center stood two sacred precincts with enormous temple buildings raised on platforms of mud brick; workshops, storehouses, and residential quarters crowded around them, while beyond the city, fields divided by canals extended for dozens of kilometers.

Walking through this city, the first thing one would have noticed was not luxury but the scale of the economy. At Uruk and other cities of the period, archaeologists find hundreds of thousands of identical, coarse clay vessels with beveled rims. They were made rapidly in molds, plainly mass-produced, and discarded by the thousand. The most common explanation is that they were ration bowls: each held a worker's daily grain allowance. Whether this interpretation is correct remains disputed, but if it is, these are history's first disposable containers and the earliest traces of a mass provisioning system for people who did not grow their own food.

This picture captures the essence of the era. The surplus that the Neolithic had learned to produce and store was, for the first time, collected in enormous quantities in a single place, recorded, redistributed, and spent according to plan. It was gathered by temples—estates belonging to the city's god and administered by priests—and later by royal palaces. Such systems are therefore known as temple and palace economies: at their center stood not the market or the individual household, but a large institution that received harvests, kept herds, fed craftspeople and laborers, and kept accounts of it all. We should not exaggerate: ordinary families with fields and gardens of their own always lived alongside the temples, while merchants and private transactions existed as well. But the largest flows of grain, wool, and labor passed through institutions.

Mesopotamia—Greek for “the land between rivers,” the Tigris and Euphrates—was not the only such world. At almost the same time, around 3100 BCE, a unified Egyptian kingdom emerged in the Nile Valley. There, all life followed the river's flood: each year the water deposited fertile silt on the fields, and the state, headed by a pharaoh regarded as a living god, measured the height of the flood to predict the harvest and determine how much tax could be taken. Several centuries later, from about 2600 BCE, the cities of Harappa and Mohenjo-daro rose in the Indus Valley, in what is now Pakistan and northwestern India, with straight streets, brick sewers, and standardized weights. The Harappan script has still not been deciphered, and we know far less about this civilization than the other two. Yet the same features are visible there as well: large settlements, storage facilities, uniform measures, and a complex system of exchange.

Three river valleys, three different paths—and one common task. In all three places, the rivers yielded enormous harvests, but only for those who could work together to build and clean canals, allocate water, store grain against a hungry year, and distribute it among thousands of people. In other words, the wealth of these lands could not be obtained alone: it demanded a degree of coordination unknown to any Neolithic village. And with coordination came all the new phenomena that fill this chapter: writing, born as bookkeeping; allocation norms and worker rations; interest-bearing debts and kings who periodically canceled them; armies, walls, and wars between neighboring cities; and finally monuments—ziggurats and pyramids—that still prompt the question whether they were senseless expenditure or a school in which humanity learned to organize great undertakings.

The scale of time should also be kept in mind. The era discussed here lasted about two thousand years—from the emergence of Uruk's first cities around the middle of the fourth millennium BCE to the middle of the second millennium, when the old order gave way to Bronze Age empires with chariots and international trade. That is roughly as long as the time separating us from the Roman Empire. Over such a span, civilizations arose, flourished, destroyed their own fields through salinization, endured conquest—and left behind inventions we still use today: writing, the calendar, standardized weights and lengths, contracts, written law, and the interest rate.

Here, for the first time, the book's recurring question can be asked not through conjecture based on bones and pits, but through documents. For the first time, surplus was recorded—and we can read where it went. What share went to canals, seeds, and reserves for hard times; what share to workers' rations; and what share to temple festivals, palace luxury, armies, and gigantic tombs? The scribes of Uruk, Lagash, and the Egyptian nomes—the administrative regions of Egypt—left us an answer, incomplete though it is, on thousands of tablets and papyri. Let us try to read it.

## Energy and Technology

If we ask what powered the economies of Uruk or Memphis, the answer is nearly the same as for a Neolithic village: muscle. The chief engine remained the person with a hoe, basket, and sickle; the second was the ox or donkey. Wind filled the sails of boats on the Nile and Euphrates, and fire baked pots and smelted copper, but waterwheels and mills did not yet exist: grain was ground by hand, stone against stone, work that took women several hours each day. In *Energy and Civilization*, Vaclav Smil emphasizes that the first civilizations discovered no new source of energy. They learned something else—to gather and direct known forms of energy on a scale never seen before.

The most important of these abilities was irrigation: the artificial watering of fields. Mesopotamia received too little rain to grow grain; in the south, annual precipitation was less than two hundred millimeters. The rivers, meanwhile, carried abundant water—but at the wrong time: the Tigris and Euphrates flooded in spring, when the crop was almost ripe, and a violent flood could wash away the fields. The Sumerians therefore had to construct an entire system: levees against floods, main canals to divert water from the river, networks of smaller channels to distribute it across fields, and gates to regulate the flow. The canals silted up and had to be cleared every year—endless work for thousands of people. The returns, however, were extraordinary. Administrative texts from the third millennium BCE report barley harvests twenty or more times the amount sown. Historians debate whether these figures reflect average years rather than the best ones, and whether scribes inflated them when calculating plan targets. Yet even cautious estimates rank southern Mesopotamia among the most productive lands of the ancient world. For comparison, medieval Europe generally harvested three to five grains for every one sown, according to Dutch historian Bernard Slicher van Bath.

Irrigation in Egypt was simpler and organized differently. The Nile flooded in summer, on a convenient schedule, and Egyptians used what is known as basin irrigation: earthen embankments divided the fields into large basins; during the flood, water was admitted and held for several weeks while it saturated the soil and deposited a layer of silt, then released back into the river. Seed was sown in the wet earth, sometimes simply by driving a herd of sheep or pigs over it to tread in the grain. This system required no constant watering and caused almost no salinization, because each flood washed the soil anew. That is why Egyptian agriculture remained productive for millennia, while the fields of southern Mesopotamia, as we will see in the section on destruction, gradually turned white with salt.

The first measuring instruments grew up around water as well. Egyptians installed nilometers on the Nile—wells or staircases marked with scales that recorded the height of the flood. A low flood meant crop failure; one too high meant washed-away levees and villages. These readings allowed officials to estimate the coming harvest in advance, and therefore the amount of tax that could be collected. This was not yet science in the modern sense, but it was systematic observation tied to an economic decision: knowledge transformed into administration.

Tools changed too. Mesopotamians plowed with an ard, a simple implement that did not turn the soil but merely opened a furrow. They devised an attachment depicted on seals and described in texts: a funnel seeder through which grain fell directly into the furrow. It conserved seed—and with harvests of “twentyfold,” every grain saved mattered. Oxen pulled the plow, and administrative accounts treated a team of several animals, together with a plowman, driver, and sower, as a unit assigned to a plot. Later, in the second half of the third millennium, the shaduf appeared in Mesopotamia: a counterweighted lever that lifted water from a canal to a higher field; it spread to Egypt still later. This simple lever allowed one person to raise in a day the amount of water that had previously required several.

The wheel appeared around the middle of the fourth millennium BCE—first, probably, as the potter's wheel, and then on carts. The earliest images of wheeled vehicles are known from Mesopotamia and, at about the same time, from Central and Eastern Europe, so precisely where the wheel was first invented remains uncertain. More importantly, for a long time it was less a transport revolution than an expensive novelty. Heavy solid wheels made from planks, slow oxen, and the absence of roads made carts useful over short distances. Rivers and canals remained the true “highways”: by water, one boat could carry as much grain as dozens of donkeys. The potter's wheel, by contrast, transformed the craft at once: pots could be made faster and more uniformly, and the potter became a professional who lived by the skill.

Metal was another technology of the age. Copper had been known earlier, but cities began smelting and casting it in large quantities, while as early as the fourth millennium people learned to make bronze—first an alloy of copper and arsenic, and in the third millennium an alloy with tin, harder and easier to cast. The river valleys themselves had no metals, hard stone, or construction timber: only clay, reeds, date palms, and water. Copper came from Oman and Anatolia, tin from still more distant places, perhaps Central Asia, wood from Lebanon, and precious lapis lazuli from Afghanistan. The technology of metal thus made the economy dependent on long-distance trade. That theme will become central in the next chapter, but its roots lie here: the valley's wealth had little value without the things for which it could be exchanged.

The era's most important technology, however, was not irrigation or bronze, but recording. Writing grew out of accounting. As early as the Neolithic, people in the Middle East used small clay tokens in different shapes—cones, spheres, disks—each signifying a particular quantity of goods. French-American scholar Denise Schmandt-Besserat proposed that the earliest marks on tablets developed from these tokens, which were sealed in clay “envelopes” and represented by impressions on the outside. Not every detail of her hypothesis is accepted, but the broad trajectory is beyond dispute: by Assyriologist Robert Englund's estimate, roughly nine-tenths of the earliest Uruk texts are administrative records of grain, livestock, textiles, and workers. Literature, laws, and letters appeared later—centuries later.

Such accounting required mathematics as well. The Sumerians used a numerical system based on sixty—from it, we inherited sixty minutes in an hour and 360 degrees in a circle. Scribes could calculate the area of fields, the volume of earth to be removed when digging a canal, and the number of workers and rations required for that volume. Egyptians solved similar problems in their own system and with their own textbooks: the surviving Rhind Mathematical Papyrus, copied around 1550 BCE from an earlier text, is full of problems about dividing bread and beer among workers or calculating the capacity of a granary.

In sum, the era's sources of energy remained the same, but their organization increased dramatically. The same muscles of people and oxen, channeled through canals, recorded on tablets, and supported by calculated rations, produced harvests beyond anything a Neolithic village could have imagined. Here we can see an idea that will run throughout this book: technologies of coordination—recording, calculation, standardized measures—can matter as much as technologies of production. But this powerful machine also posed a question that technology itself could not answer: who would decide where the surplus it gathered should go? The answer depended not on tools, but on how labor was organized and who held power.

## Organization of Labor

Who dug these canals, harvested the barley, and wove the wool? The best answers come from the records of antiquity's most bureaucratic state: the so-called Third Dynasty of Ur, which ruled Mesopotamia from roughly 2112 to 2004 BCE. In the space of a single century, its scribes left behind, by Assyriologists' count, about one hundred thousand published administrative tablets—more than any other period in Mesopotamian history. From them we can trace how many days a particular overseer's crew worked, how much grain each weaver received, and how many sheep died in the royal pens over the course of a month.

The foundation of the entire system was the rationed worker. The Sumerian word for such a worker was *gurush*, literally “man” or “worker,” and in accounting lists it served as a unit of labor. A *gurush* received a monthly barley ration from the temple or palace, wool or cloth once a year, and oil, beer, and fish on holidays. The allowances were standardized: an adult man was typically entitled to about sixty *sila* of barley a month—roughly sixty liters by modern estimates—a woman about half as much, and a child still less. That came to about two liters of grain a day: enough to live on, with almost nothing left over once one considers that everything else had to be bartered for out of the same ration. The ration was not a wage in our sense, money to be spent as one chose, but upkeep—like that provided to a soldier or a monastic novice.

The accounting of working time is especially striking. Assyriologist Robert Englund, who studied the archives of the city of Umma, showed that crew overseers kept a kind of balance sheet. At the beginning of the year, an overseer was charged with a certain number of labor-days that his people were required to work. Every task had a quota: how much earth to dig in a day, how much land to plow, how many sheaves to carry. At the end of the year, a scribe totaled the accounts: if the crew had failed to meet its target, the difference was carried forward as the overseer's debt for the following year. A woman's workday counted for less than a man's in these calculations—roughly in proportion to her ration, which was half as large. Here was a planned economy with production quotas, reporting, and managers held personally liable for shortfalls—four thousand years before Gosplan. Englund observed that some overseers were never able to make good their accumulated deficits, in which case their property became liable for the debt.

Not everyone worked for institutions year-round. A large share of the population lived in village households, held plots of their own or allotted by a temple, and surrendered part of the harvest. At certain seasons, however, they were called up for public works—to dig and clear canals, build walls, and harvest temple fields. This obligation to work for the state without pay, receiving only subsistence, is known as corvée labor. It was the era's principal tax: in a world where money scarcely existed, the state took not people's coins but their time.

The enormous workshops formed a category of their own. At Girsu, one of the centers of the Ur state, weaving establishments brought together thousands of workers, according to the tablets—German Assyriologist Helmut Waetzoldt counted some six thousand women and children at Guabba alone. Textiles were Mesopotamia's principal export, exchanged for metal, timber, and stone. The workers were women and children, often widows, orphans, captives, and their descendants. Their lives were harsh: the lists include notations of deaths and escapes. The word “slavery” applies only with qualifications: many were not the property of a particular owner, but neither were they free to leave. Historians call such people dependent workers. Between a free farmer and a slave lay a long spectrum of dependency, and most people of the period fell somewhere in between.

Slaves in the strict sense—people who could be bought and sold—also existed. Captives and debtors became slaves: someone unable to repay a loan could consign his wife, his children, or himself to bondage. But slaves were comparatively few in early Mesopotamia, and they worked more often in households than in fields. Dependent and semi-free peasants bore the main burden of agriculture. This matters for what follows: an economy built on mass slavery was a later phenomenon of classical antiquity, and its consequences will be discussed in the chapter on Greece and Rome.

Egypt left us the most famous spectacle of organized labor: the building of the pyramids. For a long time, people believed they had been raised by crowds of slaves under the lash, an image derived from Greek travelers' accounts and from the cinema. Excavations carried out at Giza since the late 1980s by American archaeologist Mark Lehner and Egyptian archaeologist Zahi Hawass painted a different picture. Beside the pyramids they found a builders' settlement with bakeries, storehouses, and the bones of numerous cattle, as well as a workers' cemetery. The skeletons reveal both hard labor—worn spines and injuries—and evidence of medical care: healed fractures and even amputations after which the patient lived for years. The builders were organized into crews with names of their own, preserved in inscriptions on the blocks—for example, “The Friends of Khufu.” Crews were divided into units, and units into groups of several dozen men. Permanent craftsmen formed the core, while the majority, most researchers believe, were peasants who came in rotation for a few months while the floodwaters covered their fields.

This does not mean that the pyramid builders were enthusiastic wage laborers. Their work remained an obligation, and they could not refuse it. But the image of the slave is no more accurate: this was a mobilized peasant labor force that the state fed—and, judging by the cattle bones, fed well—while it worked for the king. In other words, the pyramid was a means of turning a farmer's seasonal idleness into stone. We will return to this when deciding whether the pyramids should count as wasteful expenditure.

Another Egyptian scene compels us to view dependent workers differently. At the village of Deir el-Medina, where, during the New Kingdom, the craftsmen who cut and painted the pharaohs' tombs in the Valley of the Kings lived, thousands of records survive on potsherds and papyrus: lists of absences and their causes—illness, a relative's funeral, a drinking bout, a scorpion sting—disputes over debts, and transactions involving donkeys. In the twenty-ninth year of Ramesses III's reign, around 1157 BCE, when their rations were delayed, the craftsmen abandoned their work and staged a sit-in at the mortuary temples, demanding grain. It is the first strike known to us from written records, and their rations were issued, although shortages and protests recurred for months afterward. Even in a royal economy where everything was allocated from above, workers retained leverage: their skill was difficult to replace.

Above them stood new professions unknown to the Neolithic. A scribe trained for years: Mesopotamia had schools known as “tablet houses,” where boys, and occasionally girls, from prosperous families learned hundreds of signs, arithmetic, and the copying of model texts. There were priests, administrators, overseers, surveyors, potters, smiths, seal cutters, brewers, and merchants. The division of labor reached the point where a significant share of city dwellers never touched the soil at all. This was the surplus's principal developmental effect: it fed people who accumulated knowledge and skill.

Yet the same organization also reveals the other side. Records, quotas, and rations cut both ways: they made it possible not only to feed workers, but also to know precisely how much could be extracted from them. A rationed worker received an almost unchanging amount of grain, regardless of how well he worked or how abundant the harvest was. Every gain—from a good year, a new canal, or a better seeder—remained with the institution. Those who created it did not decide where it went next. This divide between the people who produce a surplus and those who control it first enters the documentary record here—and it will become one of the central threads running through this book.

## Exchange and Money

Imagine a brewer in Nippur around 1800 BCE. He needs a new copper cauldron, and the smith has plenty of cauldrons, but no use for beer: he wants wool and oil. What can they do? It was here, in the economies of temples and palaces, that humanity first created something capable of solving this problem without endless chains of barter: a common measure of value. But it arose in a way quite unlike the story usually told in school textbooks.

The conventional account runs as follows: people first exchanged goods directly, then chose a convenient commodity as an intermediary, and only later did coins and credit grow from it. In *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, anthropologist David Graeber, drawing on the work of Assyriologists, argues that Mesopotamian records present the reverse picture. First came a unit of account used by temple and palace scribes, then debts recorded in that unit, and only last a physical means of payment that passed from hand to hand. Economists and some historians dispute his view: they argue that institutional units of account and a market using commodity money existed side by side, and that private trade was significant in the Old Babylonian period. The debate, then, concerns sequence and proportion more than whether only one system existed at all. Coins would not appear here for more than another thousand years, and the era's money was above all a means of keeping accounts.

There were two such units, both originating in institutional administration. The first was barley, measured in *sila* (about a liter) and *gur* (three hundred *sila* in the Ur period). Rations were issued in barley, harvests counted in barley, and labor valued in barley. The second was silver, measured by weight: the shekel (about eight grams), the mina (sixty shekels), and the talent (sixty minas). The base-sixty system discussed above found its way here as well. Temple scribes established a fixed ratio between the two measures: one shekel of silver equaled one *gur* of barley. This ratio appears, for example, in the very first provision of the Laws of Eshnunna, compiled around the eighteenth century BCE, and persisted for a long time as the official standard, even though in practice the price of barley could differ severalfold immediately after the harvest and just before it. The ratio enabled a scribe to convert everything—sheep, cloth, oil, labor-days—into a single measure and balance the accounts of an enormous estate.

It is important to understand that silver itself rarely changed hands. Most of it lay in temple treasuries, while everyday transactions relied on written accounts and barley. A farmer whom a tavern keeper served beer on credit paid her in grain after the harvest. When silver was used, it was weighed on scales: archaeologists find coils, rings, and chopped pieces of silver wire from which the required amount was pinched off. This is the origin of the biblical expression “to weigh out silver,” while the word *shekel* itself is related to a verb meaning “to weigh.”

Egypt followed its own path. For a long time, it managed without any single intermediary at all: the state collected grain and issued rations, while private exchange was conducted by valuation. During the New Kingdom, the *deben*—a unit of copper weighing about ninety grams—served as the standard for such valuations. Documents from Deir el-Medina, the same village of tomb builders, are full of transactions in which a donkey, bed, or coffin is valued in deben but paid for with an assortment of goods: so many sacks of grain, a pair of sandals, a jar of oil. No copper at all may have changed hands—the deben was a ruler, not a coin.

Less is known about money in the Indus Valley, but one discovery speaks volumes: standardized cubical stone weights found from Harappa to the coast of Gujarat. Their weights form a strict progression, each one twice as heavy as the last, after which the sequence continues in multiples of ten. Identical weights across a vast territory imply a common standard and probably common oversight of it. Harappan-style seals have also been found in Mesopotamian cities, while Sumerian texts mention a distant country called Meluhha, which supplied carnelian, ivory, and timber; most researchers identify it with the Indus civilization.

Merchants lived alongside the institutions. In Mesopotamia they were called *tamkarum*. At first, they were agents of temples and palaces, entrusted with exchanging surplus wool and cloth for metal, timber, and stone, but over time they increasingly traded at their own risk and for their own profit. The most vivid example is that of the Assyrian merchants from the city of Ashur who, around 1900 BCE, carried tin and textiles by donkey caravan to Kanesh, in what is now central Turkey. Their archives, discovered at the mound of Kültepe, have yielded more than twenty thousand tablets: business letters, contracts, and calculations of partnership shares. The next chapter will discuss this trade in greater detail. What matters here is one point: long-distance exchange required trust between strangers, and written contracts, seals, and witnesses supplied it.

Where there is a unit of account and a contract, debt inevitably appears, followed by interest. Interest probably originated with the same institutions: a temple lent silver to a merchant or seed grain to a farmer and expected to receive more than it had given, just as a herd produces offspring over the course of a year. The Sumerian word for interest, *mash*, literally meant “kid.” Rates were standardized: under Mesopotamian law, including the laws of Hammurabi (around 1750 BCE), lenders could charge about twenty percent a year on silver and up to one third on grain. These rates persisted stubbornly for centuries, regardless of the return an investment actually produced.

For a merchant, twenty percent might be a reasonable price for risk. For a farmer, the situation was very different. A failed harvest, an illness, the death of an ox—and the family borrowed grain that had to be repaid with an additional third. Another bad year made the debt impossible to bear. Then the last assets came into play: first the field, then the freedom of the children and wife, and finally the debtor's own. The records are full of such transactions—for a free inhabitant of Mesopotamia, debt was the main road into slavery. Here, for the first time in history, we can see a mechanism that will recur again and again: interest on debt grows faster than harvests, and surplus gradually flows from those who produce it to those who lend.

Mesopotamian rulers understood the danger. A farmer who became a creditor's slave ceased to serve in the king's army and report for public works. Kings therefore periodically issued decrees “restoring justice”: they canceled consumer debts, returned land sold for debt, and released debt slaves to their families. One of the earliest rulers to take such a step was Urukagina of Lagash, around 2350 BCE. His inscription contains the word *amargi*, translated as “freedom”: literally, it means “return to the mother,” or sending freed debtors home. Such decrees later became almost customary: a new king of Babylon would often proclaim a cancellation in the first year of his reign. The best-preserved example is the decree of King Ammisaduqa, issued around 1646 BCE. It carefully distinguishes between debts: consumer loans to farmers were forgiven, while commercial loans to merchants were not.

Historians assess these cancellations differently. American economist Michael Hudson, on whose work Graeber also draws, sees them as a deliberate policy that preserved a free peasantry and prevented creditors from turning it into a dependent labor force. Skeptics point out that the decrees were not enforced everywhere, that creditors devised ways around them, and that the very repetition of the decrees shows the problem was never solved. Both sides are right in their own way. But the fact itself is remarkable: four thousand years ago, political authority recognized that debts accumulated according to the rules could destroy a society and judged it wise to reset the accounts from time to time.

The answer to the question “where did the surplus go?” is therefore twofold in the sphere of exchange. On the one hand, standardized measures, written contracts, and credit reduced the cost of exchange and made it possible to connect the barley fields of Sumer with the copper of Oman and the lapis lazuli of Afghanistan; this was developmental. On the other hand, the same credit, with fixed rates and fickle harvests, worked like a pump, moving land and people from the hands of producers into those of creditors and institutions; this was extraction. The decision as to which side would prevail was made not in the market, but in the palace. The next step, therefore, is to examine how power was organized.

## Power and Institutions

Who decided how much grain to reserve for seed, how much to distribute as rations, and how much to devote to a new temple or a campaign against a neighboring city? In the early cities of Sumer, judging by the oldest texts, the temple stood at the head of the economy, and at the head of the temple stood a ruler called an *ensi* or *en*: priest, administrator, and military commander at once. Alongside him, at least in some cities and periods, councils of elders and assemblies of free men also operated. In the 1940s, Danish Assyriologist Thorkild Jacobsen proposed that early Mesopotamia possessed a kind of “primitive democracy”: important decisions, such as whether to go to war, were made by an assembly. He based this idea largely on myths and epics—for example, the story of Gilgamesh, in which the ruler of Uruk consults both the elders and the young warriors before marching against Kish. Many historians consider the picture too bold: a literary plot is not the minutes of a meeting. Yet traces of collective bodies—city assemblies, councils of elders, and the court of “the city”—do appear in the records, and they survived into the Babylonian period.

Over time, a palace arose beside the temple. The word *lugal* literally meant “great man” and probably referred at first to a military leader elevated during a time of danger. But wars among the city-states—Lagash and Umma, Uruk and Kish—continued for centuries, and the temporary leader became permanent. Around 2334 BCE, Sargon of Akkad became the first to unite all of Mesopotamia under his rule and created what is often called the first empire. One of his inscriptions says that 5,400 people ate before him every day—probably a reference to a standing army and court maintained by the palace. The figure may be exaggerated, but the point is clear: for the first time, a substantial share of the surplus went to support a professional army that served not the city, but the king.

The Third Dynasty of Ur, discussed above, took this system to its limit. King Shulgi, who ruled for roughly half a century in the twenty-first century BCE, introduced standardized measures of weight and volume, a unified calendar, a standing army, and a network of roads with way stations and couriers. The provinces took turns, month by month, delivering a prescribed share of livestock, grain, and other products to the center. This arrangement is known as the *bala* system, meaning “rotation” or “turn.” A special redistribution center, Puzrish-Dagan, was built near Nippur. According to the tablets, tens of thousands of sheep and goats passed through it every year: they were received, recorded, and sent on to temples, the palace, ambassadors, and officials. This was no longer merely a temple economy but a state economy in the fullest sense—by the classic assessment, one of the most centralized in history before the twentieth century. Today, some Assyriologists, including Steven Garfinkle, argue that the archives exaggerate the reach of the state: private households and entrepreneurs also played a significant role, but their affairs appeared less often in royal records.

Power was organized differently in Egypt. There was no succession of rival cities; from the unification of the country around 3100 BCE, it was headed by the pharaoh, regarded as a god on earth. His primary duty was to uphold *maat*—order, justice, and the proper course of the world, including the timely flooding of the Nile. In practice, the country was administered by the vizier, or chief minister, and a network of officials: scribes, tax collectors, managers of royal storehouses, and nomarchs, the governors of the regional nomes. The durability of this order depended on whether the rulers fulfilled their side of the bargain. When, at the end of the third millennium BCE, a series of low floods coincided with a weakening of the central government, the nomarchs began to rule as independent princes, while later texts described the period as an age of famine and turmoil. Egyptologists call it the First Intermediate Period. Scholars debate how much the texts exaggerated the suffering for didactic purposes, but the connection is visible: a power that collects the surplus but cannot protect people from a failed harvest loses its right to collect it.

The era's most famous document is the laws of Hammurabi, king of Babylon, carved around 1750 BCE onto a basalt stele more than two meters tall; it now stands in the Louvre. It was not the first such collection: fragments survive of the laws of Ur-Nammu, founder of the Third Dynasty of Ur, compiled about three and a half centuries earlier. But Hammurabi's stele is the most complete text: some 280 provisions concerning theft, the leasing of fields, wages, debts, marriage, inheritance, a builder's responsibility for a collapsed house, and a physician's responsibility for a failed operation. In the prologue, the king explains that the gods appointed him so that the strong would not oppress the weak. Hammurabi's laws are known for their severity—the principle of “an eye for an eye” and punishments involving mutilation—and for their unequal justice: injuring a full citizen, an *awilum*, carried one penalty; injuring a dependent *mushkenum*, a smaller fine; injuring a slave, compensation to the owner.

Yet for the economy, what matters most about the stele is not its cruelty but the fact that the rules of transactions were written down and displayed publicly. The laws set interest rates, wages for hired workers and boatmen, rental terms, and a farmer's liability for failing to reinforce a dike and thereby flooding a neighbor's field. Historians debate whether courts applied these provisions literally: direct references to the stele are almost absent from the thousands of surviving court records, and many scholars regard it more as a proclamation of royal justice than as a code in the modern sense. But even as a proclamation, it established expectations. Merchants, tenants, and creditors knew what they could count on—and predictability, as we shall see in Chapter 9, is one of the principal conditions for people to invest effort in the future rather than hide their property.

The Indus Valley poses an awkward question for this picture. Archaeologists have found large cities, standardized weights, common standards for bricks—their proportions are the same from one end of the civilization to the other—sewers, and public structures such as the Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro. But they have found no indisputable palace, no lavish royal tombs, and no images of victorious kings trampling their enemies. In *The Dawn of Everything*, David Graeber and David Wengrow cite this as an example of how a complex urban society could function without a conspicuous monarchy. Other archaeologists object that absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and that because the Indus script remains undeciphered, we simply do not know who ruled there. Only one conclusion can be stated honestly: someone maintained common standards across a vast territory, and that someone apparently did not spend the surplus glorifying itself in stone the way the pharaohs and kings of Akkad did.

Taken together, the era's ruling powers performed three functions, and the balance among them determined where the surplus went. The first was coordination: canals, measures, calendars, reserves for years of famine, and courts operating under written rules. This was developmental, and without it the river valleys could not have fed their cities. The second was defense and attack: walls, armies, and campaigns. It could be both necessary and destructive, and it is often difficult to say where one ended and the other began. The third was self-glorification: palaces, tombs, lavish festivals, and retinues. A narrow circle—the king, priests, and senior officials—decided how these three functions would be balanced. Those who dug the canals and harvested the barley had almost no influence on the decision, apart from rare strikes or flight. Debt-cancellation decrees show that sensible rulers understood that by exhausting the producers, political power undermined its own foundation. But that understanding depended on the wisdom of an individual king, not on the design of institutions. Let us now consider what, in all of this, genuinely advanced society.

## What Drove Development

Seen through the eyes of their contemporaries, the wealth of temple and palace economies was measured in heaps of grain in the granaries, herds in the royal pens, and silver in the treasury. Almost none of it has survived: the grain was eaten, the sheep slaughtered, and the silver melted down or carried away by conquerors. Yet some of the era's investments are still at work today. These are what deserve to be called developmental: the things that increased people's capacity to produce, know, and reach agreements—and continued to yield returns after both the kings and the temples that paid for them had disappeared.

The first and most important was recording. Writing was born as a way not to lose count of barley, but it quickly became clear that signs were useful for more than sacks. As early as Uruk's first centuries, scribes compiled so-called lexical lists—sets of words grouped by subject: professions, vessels, trees, fish, cities. They were teaching aids and were copied with almost no changes for hundreds of years. Assyriologists see them as the first attempts to organize the world in words—the germ of the reference work and the classification system. Letters, contracts, laws, hymns, and the Epic of Gilgamesh followed. Cuneiform endured for some three thousand years and served more than a dozen languages, from Sumerian and Akkadian to Hittite, Hurrian, and Urartian; the Persian kings even modeled a script of their own on it. No army of the period remained in service for so long. Under the criteria established in Chapter 0, this contributed in several categories at once: the accumulation of knowledge, which could now be preserved outside the memory of an individual, and lower coordination costs, because a written promise is harder to dispute than an oral one.

The second was mathematics and measurement. A school exercise asking how many workers are needed to dig a canal of a certain length in a given number of days looks dull, but exercises like this produced one of antiquity's strongest mathematical traditions. Babylonian scribes of the early second millennium BCE used positional notation, in which the value of a sign depends on its place, just as our digit “2” can mean twenty or two hundred. They could solve problems that we would express today as quadratic equations, and they compiled tables of reciprocals and squares to speed up their calculations. The famous tablet Plimpton 322, written around 1800 BCE, records a series of numbers related by the same relationship as the sides of a right triangle in the theorem later named after Pythagoras. Scholars still debate why it was compiled—for teaching or calculation—but it is clear that this knowledge grew out of administrative practice and then took on a life of its own. The Egyptians, for their part, created a civil calendar of 365 days: twelve months of thirty days and five additional days. It was needed for taxation and for predicting the flood, but through Alexandria and Julius Caesar, its descendant became the calendar by which the entire world lives.

The third was infrastructure: structures used by the entire economy at once—canals, dikes, storehouses, roads, and quays. A canal is capital in its purest form: labor invested once by thousands of people continues for decades to yield harvests from every field the water reaches. Crucially, a single family cannot build such a thing: the benefits of a canal are distributed among everyone downstream, while the cost is too great for any one household. Here, a large institution—the temple or palace—did what a market of small farms would not have done, and did it with returns visible in the harvests. This is the strongest argument in favor of the first states, and honesty requires that we acknowledge it: without centralized coordination, the fields of Sumer and Egypt could not have fed their cities.

The fourth was reserves. Temple and palace granaries acted as insurance: grain collected in a good year was distributed in a bad one. In Egypt, with its capricious Nile, this was a matter of survival, and the collapse of political authority during the First Intermediate Period shows what happens when the insurance ceases to function. By the criterion of resilience to shocks, this was developmental: a hunter-gatherer could move away from crop failure, a Neolithic farmer could only endure it with his own reserves, while a subject of the pharaoh, at least in good times, could rely on the common granary.

The fifth was common measures, contracts, and predictable rules. The standard shekel, *sila*, and *gur*; the identical weights of the Indus Valley; the recorded terms of leases and loans—all reduced the cost of a transaction, what economists call transaction costs: the time and effort spent not on production, but on reaching agreement, checking compliance, and avoiding deception. A merchant from Ashur could send tin a thousand kilometers to Kanesh and entrust it to strangers because the cargo was sealed and the terms written down. This was trust between strangers, which in Chapter 1 we saw only in embryonic form in the exchange of obsidian and shells.

The sixth was specialization. The surplus collected by institutions fed people who did not plow the land: scribes, potters, coppersmiths, weavers, seal cutters, surveyors, and physicians. A professional craftsperson who spends a lifetime doing the same thing works faster and better than an amateur and passes the skill on to apprentices. The schools known as “tablet houses” turned knowledge into something that could be reproduced from one generation to the next. For the first time, a social stratum appeared whose labor was knowledge itself—and this, too, was an investment of surplus rather than its loss.

Finally, the debt cancellations discussed above should also be counted as developmental. By themselves, they produced nothing. But they prevented the debt pump from turning free farmers into dependents, thereby preserving the foundation on which the harvests, the army, and public works all rested. It is a rare historical example of political power deliberately repairing a mechanism that it had itself created.

All these achievements share one qualification, however, and omitting it would make the picture dishonest. Almost every developmental investment of the era served the interests of the institution: writing allowed it to collect more accurately, mathematics to calculate quotas more precisely, and canals to bring more grain into temple granaries. Knowledge was the monopoly of a narrow circle: historians estimate that no more than a few percent of the population was literate, and schooling was available mainly to the children of scribes and officials. A rationed worker received his two liters of barley in a failed harvest and in a record harvest alike; almost none of the gains from higher productivity reached him. In other words, surplus really was invested in tools, knowledge, and trust—but the benefits of those investments were appropriated at the top, while the broad majority received above all protection from starvation. That is not a small thing. Yet by our third criterion—the quality of life of the majority—the era looks far less impressive than it does by the first two. And the fact that its principal inventions outlived their creators says less about the generosity of kings than about the nature of knowledge itself: once written down, it escapes its owner and begins to serve everyone.

## What Destroyed

Around 2450 BCE, Eannatum, ruler of Lagash, erected a stone stele on his city's border. It depicts a god trapping enemies in a giant net, while birds of prey circle above the battlefield and tear apart the bodies of the dead. It is therefore known as the Stele of the Vultures; its fragments are held in the Louvre. The cause of the war is recorded with an accountant's precision: a disputed strip of fertile land called Gu'edena and the canals that irrigated it. Lagash and neighboring Umma contested it for more than a century—making treaties and breaking them, erecting and removing boundary stones, diverting water. In Lagash's inscriptions, the victor demands that the defeated city repay a “debt” of barley accumulated over years of using the disputed fields—an amount so vast that repayment was impossible. This is the first war known to us from written records, and it was fought not for faith or glory, but for fields and water: for the source of the surplus itself.

War was the first of the era's destructive forces. Neolithic villages also fought, but a city-state fought differently: it had a surplus with which to feed an army, and walls, granaries, and temples worth capturing. The Sumerian cities waged such wars almost continuously for several centuries. Every one of them destroyed what our framework classifies as capital: dikes collapsed, canals filled with silt, fields were trampled, workers carried off as captives, and storehouses burned. Worse still, water bound neighbors together more tightly than they wished: a city upstream could divert a canal and leave a downstream city without a harvest. Irrigation, which required coordination, also placed a weapon in the hands of those who refused to coordinate.

Unifying the country under a single power might seem as though it should have ended this. But the era's empires proved fragile. Sargon of Akkad's state endured for roughly a century and a half and disintegrated by the end of the twenty-second century BCE under pressure from Gutian highlanders and internal rebellions. The later Mesopotamian poem *The Curse of Akkad* describes its end as divine punishment: the fields ceased to yield, the canals became overgrown, and grain prices soared. Some researchers, including American archaeologist Harvey Weiss, connect this decline to a severe arid period around 2200 BCE, traces of which appear in lake and cave deposits from the Mediterranean to India; climatologists call it the “4.2-kiloyear event.” Others regard drought as an overly convenient explanation for a collapse with ample political causes. Most likely, the forces combined: a state that extracted surplus to the limit had no margin of safety when the harvest failed.

The fate of the Third Dynasty of Ur—the model bureaucratic state with its quotas and reports—is even more revealing. It collapsed within roughly one generation. A correspondence survives—though only in school copies made centuries later, and its accuracy is debated, notably by American Assyriologist Piotr Michalowski—between the last king, Ibbi-Sin, and his commander Ishbi-Erra. The latter reports that he has purchased grain but cannot deliver it to the capital because Amorite nomads have cut the roads, and asks for more silver. Yet even apart from the letters, administrative records from the dynasty's final years show that the price of grain in the capital rose many times over. One province after another ceased sending its *bala* “turn” to the center, and Ishbi-Erra himself soon became an independent ruler. Around 2004 BCE, the Elamites captured Ur and took the king prisoner. *The Lament for Ur*—one of antiquity's most poignant poems—depicts a city where the dead lie in the streets and the granaries contain no grain. The lesson is harsh: a system in which all surplus converged on the center was highly efficient while communication between center and provinces functioned, and defenseless when that connection broke. Extreme centralization economizes on coordination but concentrates every risk in a single point.

The second destroyer was the land itself—or, more precisely, what people did to it. River water, even fresh water, contains small quantities of dissolved salts. When irrigation water evaporates, the salt remains. In Egypt, the annual flood washed it away; in southern Mesopotamia, with its poor drainage and high water table, the salt rose toward the surface year after year. In “Salt and Silt in Ancient Mesopotamian Agriculture,” published in *Science* in 1958, Thorkild Jacobsen and American archaeologist Robert Adams traced two troubling trends through temple tablets. First, wheat, which tolerates salt poorly, gradually vanished from southern fields. By their estimate, it had been sown in roughly equal proportion to barley at the end of the fourth millennium BCE; by the end of the third millennium, its share had fallen to a few percent, and later wheat was scarcely mentioned in the south at all. Barley is hardier, and farmers sowed more and more of it. Second, yields declined by their calculations: from very high levels in the middle of the third millennium to markedly more modest ones by its end, and lower still afterward. The records even mention fields explicitly described as “white”—covered in salt.

This reconstruction is not accepted without qualifications. In the 1980s, American Assyriologist Marvin Powell pointed out that the shift to barley could also be explained by taste and economic calculation, while yield figures from planning documents cannot be compared directly across periods. Today, most specialists believe salinization was real and serious, but that it proceeded unevenly, place by place, rather than in a single collapse. At the same time, the center of Mesopotamian civilization did gradually shift away from the far south toward Babylon and later Assyria, while the old Sumerian cities, once the largest in the world, emptied over time. The mechanism matters throughout this book. Salinization is the destruction of capital that no one intended: every individual decision—to irrigate a field once more, not to leave it fallow, to obtain a larger harvest in order to meet a quota—was rational for the person making it, yet over centuries the sum of those decisions ruined the soil. We may suspect that planning pressure accelerated the process: an overseer whose property was liable for a shortfall was in no position to let a field rest.

The third destructive force is already familiar from the section on exchange: the debt pump. Credit itself advanced trade, but at interest rates of twenty and thirty-three percent, with harvest failures occurring regularly, it forced free farmers into dependency. A debtor who became a bonded laborer did not disappear, but he lost any reason to invest effort in land that no longer belonged to him. Debt-cancellation decrees repaired the mechanism, but only periodically; between them, the pump continued to operate.

Finally, some forms of destruction annihilated knowledge. Around 1761 BCE, Hammurabi captured the city of Mari on the middle Euphrates and soon afterward destroyed and burned its palace, one of the richest in the region. In an irony of history, the fire baked the clay tablets and preserved some twenty thousand documents from the palace archive. But it could not restore the city itself, its workshops, trade connections, or people: Mari never regained its former importance. The Indus Valley civilization declined after roughly 1900 BCE: its great cities emptied, its standardized weights and seals fell out of use, and its writing was forgotten. The causes remain disputed—shifting and drying rivers, weakening monsoons, declining trade with Mesopotamia—and archaeologists have found no evidence of mass violence there. But the result for knowledge was the same as after a military defeat: the Indus script vanished together with the people who mastered it and remains undeciphered to this day. Knowledge held by a narrow circle of specialists died with that circle.

In sum, the surplus the era destroyed flowed in three directions: into wars over the source of surplus itself—land and water; into soil exhausted to meet today's quota; and into the hands of creditors who turned producers into dependents. All three shared a short time horizon. A ruler thought about the next campaign, an overseer about the annual report, and a creditor about the coming harvest. The cost was paid by generations that inherited saline fields and ruined cities. But the era had another category of expenditure—one that destroyed nothing, yet created nothing except magnificence. That is the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasted

In the 1920s, British archaeologist Leonard Woolley excavated a cemetery at Ur that he called the Royal Cemetery. Among nearly two thousand ordinary burials, he found about sixteen exceptional tombs dating from the middle of the third millennium BCE. One contained a woman named Puabi, whose name was preserved on a cylinder seal. She wore a headdress of gold leaves and necklaces of lapis lazuli and carnelian; the lapis had been brought from the mountains of present-day Afghanistan, more than two thousand kilometers away. Beside the tombs, Woolley found pits filled with the bodies of servants, guards, musicians with lyres, and women in elaborate dress. The largest, which he named the Great Death Pit, contained seventy-four people. Woolley imagined a quiet, voluntary death by poison. A new examination of the skulls by CT scan in the late 2000s, published in 2011, revealed a harsher picture: at least some of the people had been killed by blows to the head, after which their bodies were treated and arranged in rows.

This is the extreme case of wasteful expenditure—and a useful place to begin because there is no ambiguity. Gold placed in the earth ceased to participate in exchange. Lapis lazuli, for which caravans had crossed half a continent, no longer served anyone. And people trained in crafts, music, and service—bearers of accumulated skill—were simply destroyed. The custom of mass human sacrifice did not endure long in Mesopotamia, but the idea that surplus could and should follow a ruler into the afterlife proved far more persistent.

The pyramids are more complicated, and the question posed at the beginning of the chapter demands an honest answer. The Great Pyramid of Khufu, built around 2560 BCE, stood about 146 meters high and, by the conventional estimate, was composed of more than two million stone blocks. Its construction took roughly twenty years. Mark Lehner and Zahi Hawass estimate that twenty to twenty-five thousand people worked on it at any one time, including peasants serving in rotation—far fewer than the hundred thousand described by Herodotus. In 2013, French Egyptologist Pierre Tallet discovered the oldest known papyri on the Red Sea coast at Wadi al-Jarf: the diary of an official named Merer. Day by day, he recorded how his crew of several dozen men shipped limestone from the Tura quarries along the Nile and canals to Giza. It is perhaps our most detailed picture of what Egypt's surplus became: boats, grain for the rowers, canals leading to the building site, and endless journeys carrying stone.

There are three arguments for treating the pyramid as a developmental investment. First, the construction project served as a school of organization. Feeding, housing, treating, and directing tens of thousands of people over many years required recordkeeping, logistics, and the skills of engineers and surveyors—all of which the Old Kingdom state acquired through the pyramids. Second, construction took place during the flood, when the fields lay underwater and peasants could not work anyway, meaning that seasonal idleness rather than harvest was turned into stone. The third and most modern argument invokes Keynes. In *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* (1936), he remarked with clear irony that even digging holes or building pyramids at public expense was better than unemployment, because the workers' wages became demand for everyone else. In the same passage, half in jest, he wrote that ancient Egypt was doubly fortunate in having both pyramids and the search for precious metals. Sometimes this irony is taken seriously and the pharaohs are cast as the first Keynesians.

None of these arguments survives scrutiny in full. Organizational skills did indeed accumulate, but after the first enormous pyramids, the return from each additional one declined rapidly: one would have been enough to learn how to build. Yet Khufu's father Sneferu built three large pyramids—one at Meidum and two at Dahshur—which, by Egyptologists' calculations, together consumed more stone than the Great Pyramid. The same experience could have been acquired by building canals, dikes, and storehouses that would have continued to yield harvests for centuries. The argument from “seasonal idleness” is weaker than it appears as well: the core workforce labored year-round, while mobilized peasants still had to be fed with grain harvested by everyone else. The free time created by the flood could have been invested in something that increased the following year's harvest; instead, it was invested in something that increased nothing. As for Keynes, his irony concerned an economy with unemployed people and idle factories, where money sat unused. In a world where almost everyone was occupied in obtaining food, there were no spare resources: every worker at a pyramid was a person fed by someone else's labor.

The most revealing detail, however, lies not in the pyramids themselves, but in what followed them. Every pyramid acquired a mortuary temple, priests, and a settlement allotted land so that the cult of the dead king could continue forever. The so-called Dahshur Decree of Pharaoh Pepi I, who ruled roughly three centuries after Sneferu, has survived: it exempts the settlements attached to Sneferu's pyramids from labor obligations. In other words, three hundred years after the pharaoh's death, the state continued to support an estate whose sole purpose was to commemorate him—and exempted it from common duties as well. Every new king added more such estates. Some Egyptologists see the accumulation of these tax-exempt holdings, together with the increasing power of local nobles, as one cause of the Old Kingdom's weakening. This is a hypothesis, not an established fact, but the mechanism is clear: an increasing share of the living population's surplus went to support the dead. This is rent in its purest form—continuous extraction unrelated to any current contribution.

Mesopotamia had variants of the same phenomenon. Ziggurats—stepped temple towers such as the one Ur-Nammu raised at Ur around the twenty-first century BCE—were smaller than the pyramids but consumed enormous quantities of mud brick and labor, and required constant repair because rain eroded the unfired bricks. Gods in the temples received meals several times a day—the most detailed descriptions are admittedly late, from the first millennium BCE. Temple texts show, however, that what the god “ate” was then distributed to priests and attendants, making this less a pure loss than a redistribution in favor of temple staff. Palaces grew along with royal ambition, while inscriptions such as Sargon's reference to 5,400 table companions evoke a court that fed from the common granary simply because it surrounded the king.

Finally, there was a less visible kind of wasteful expenditure: excessive control. The archives of the Third Dynasty of Ur contain tens of thousands of tablets recording every lamb, every work shift, and every sack of grain. Accounting, as we have seen, was the era's greatest invention. But there is a point beyond which it ceases to aid production and begins to serve only itself: when an overseer is personally liable for a shortfall and a scribe writes a report about a report, some of the surplus goes not into grain but into checking the grain. The ancient records do not allow us to determine exactly where this point lay. Yet the fact that the Ur state collapsed within one generation despite immaculate reporting demonstrates that perfect accounting is no substitute for resilience.

What share of the surplus went into these categories cannot honestly be stated—no reliable calculations exist. But comparison with the Indus Valley, where, as a reminder, archaeologists have found no lavish tombs, palaces, or monuments to victory, suggests that this share was not a law of nature. It was determined by who controlled the surplus and to whom they were accountable. The measure proposed by this chapter is simple: did the investment leave behind a capability that others could use? A canal did, and so did a tablet. The Great Death Pit at Ur and Sneferu's third pyramid did not.

## The Lesson of the Era

The first states endured in the river valleys for about two thousand years—roughly as long as the span separating us from Julius Caesar. Dozens of dynasties came and went during that time; cities rose and emptied, yet one pattern recurred with the force of a law: grain was collected in one place, recorded, distributed—and contested. The scribe in Umma reducing man-days to an annual report and the Egyptian worker at Deir el-Medina who laid down his tools because his rations were late were, in essence, speaking about the same thing. The argument can be condensed into five propositions.

**First: when surplus is concentrated in a few hands, the question “where should it be invested?” becomes political.** In the Neolithic, the increment was dispersed among thousands of family granaries, and each family decided for itself. In Uruk, Ur, and Memphis, decisions were made by a narrow circle—the king, the priests, and senior officials. This made possible canals that no single village could have dug and reserves that saved a country in a year of famine. But the same concentration made it possible, within a single generation, to commit the labor of tens of thousands of people to a pyramid or a campaign against a neighboring city. Concentrated surplus is neither good nor evil; it is an amplifier, magnifying both the benefit of a sound decision and the harm of a bad one.

**Second: the era's most durable investments were those that escaped their owners.** Writing was invented to collect barley more accurately, mathematics to calculate quotas more accurately, and the calendar to collect taxes on time. The institutions that paid for these inventions vanished long ago, but positional notation, the reckoning of time, and the very idea of a written contract serve everyone. This gives us a measure we will continue to apply: an investment advances development when it leaves behind a capability available to others. The wider the circle able to use it, the longer its returns endure.

**Third: debt without a safeguard turns a producer into a debtor, and a debtor into a worker with no stake in the work.** Credit enabled Assyrian merchants to trade across a thousand kilometers, and that was development. But at interest rates of twenty and thirty-three percent, amid regular harvest failures, the same mechanism transferred land and freedom from the many to the few, year after year. Mesopotamian kings understood the danger and periodically proclaimed debt cancellations—the earliest known example of a government repairing a mechanism that threatened its own foundations. But the repair depended on the will of each successive ruler, not on the design of the rules. As Michael Hudson observes, the question of who may cancel debts, and when, would remain one of economics' central questions for millennia; we will return to it in Rome, medieval Europe, and the crisis of 2008.

**Fourth: centralization economizes on coordination but concentrates all risk at a single point.** The state of the Third Dynasty of Ur, with its quotas, reports, and distribution center at Puzrish-Dagan, was probably the most exact economic mechanism in the ancient world—yet it collapsed within roughly one generation when the roads between the capital and the provinces were severed. The Akkadian Empire did not survive a century of aridity; Egypt's Old Kingdom did not survive a succession of low floods combined with a weakening center. The saline fields of southern Mesopotamia reveal the same problem from another side: every overseer met his annual quota, and no one was responsible for the soil a hundred years later. A system in which every decision flows upward sees clearly what can be counted within a year and poorly what accumulates over centuries.

**Fifth: the share lost to wasteful expenditure is determined not by wealth but by accountability.** The Great Death Pit at Ur, Sneferu's three pyramids, and mortuary estates exempted from obligations for centuries were not the unavoidable price of civilization. The Indus Valley, whose large cities, standardized weights, and sewer systems were accompanied by neither lavish tombs nor monuments glorifying kings, reminds us that a complex society could spend its surplus differently. We do not know who ruled there or why—the Indus script remains undeciphered, and any conclusions must be cautious. But the comparison suggests that the scale of prestige spending depended on whom the managers of surplus had to answer to. Where they answered to no one but the gods, the gods received the largest share.

Now for the answer to the book's recurring question. Where did the surplus of the first states go? Most still went to feeding a growing population: rations, seed, and reserves against crop failure. A significant share went into development: canals and dikes, writing, scribal schools, measures and weights, written law, and crafts that could now support specialists year-round. Another share went into destruction: wars over land and water, soil salinization, and debt bondage. And for the first time in history, an enormous share—measurable in millions of stone blocks—went into prestige and into supporting those who controlled the rest: tombs, ziggurats, palaces, mortuary cults, and retinues. Compared with the Neolithic, it was not so much the nature of the choice that had changed as its scale: one person could now decide what the labor of an entire country would become for twenty years.

The limits of these conclusions should also be stated honestly. We see the era through the eyes of its institutions: the tablets were written by temple and palace scribes, and they tell us what those institutions considered worth recording. The life of a peasant family outside the temple economy, markets for which no reports were compiled, and the views of those who dug the canals all remain in shadow. Estimates of yields, urban populations, and the roles of climate and salinization remain contested, and Assyriologists and Egyptologists will revise them more than once. Yet the central framework is robust: the state was born as a machine for collecting and distributing surplus, and its earliest forms already reveal all three destinations to which surplus can flow—tools and knowledge, destruction, and prestige. In the next chapter, metals, long-distance trade, and coinage enter this picture, and for the first time not only temple and palace but merchant too will be able to decide how surplus is used.

## Figures

For the first states, numbers can for the first time be drawn not only from the ground but from documents: scribes themselves counted people, rations, and harvests. But the tablets survive selectively, while archaeological estimates still provide only orders of magnitude. Everything below is an estimate, and disagreements among specialists are noted.

**World population.** Historical demographers Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones estimate that roughly fourteen million people lived on Earth around 3000 BCE and about fifty million by 1000 BCE. Other syntheses put the population at the beginning of the third millennium at more than forty million, but all agree on the central point: growth accelerated, though it was still measured in tenths of a percent per year.

**Cities and countries.** Around 3100 BCE, Uruk covered approximately 250 hectares; its population is estimated at 25,000–50,000 (Guillermo Algaze, Marc van de Mieroop), making it probably the largest city of its time. Jonathan Kenoyer estimates that Mohenjo-daro in the Indus Valley had several tens of thousands of inhabitants. In 1976, geographer Karl Butzer estimated the total population of Old Kingdom Egypt at roughly 1.5 million—one of the most frequently cited figures, but far from undisputed.

**Rations.** Mesopotamian tablets reveal something unavailable for any earlier era: a consumption standard. The typical monthly ration for an adult male temple or palace worker was about sixty liters of barley; a woman received about thirty, and children less, supplemented by oil, wool, or cloth. Sixty “liters” a month is about two liters, or roughly 1–1.4 kilograms of grain per day; on paper, that amounts to 3,500–4,500 kilocalories. But after losses from hulling and milling, barley used for beer or exchanged for oil, fish, and clothing, and often the shares of dependents as well, it was a modest subsistence allowance, not plenty (calculations by British Assyriologist Rosemary Ellison).

**Harvest and soil.** Economic texts from southern Mesopotamia report yields high by ancient standards: in a number of records from the middle of the third millennium, harvests amounted to twenty or thirty times the seed sown (compared with the usual three to five times in medieval Europe, according to Slicher van Bath). In 1958, Thorkild Jacobsen and Robert Adams argued that by the eighteenth century BCE yields in southern Sumer had fallen almost threefold—from roughly 2,500 to 900 liters per hectare—and wheat had nearly disappeared from the fields, displaced by more salt-tolerant barley. Marvin Powell and others dispute these calculations; many accept the direction of change—salinization and declining returns—while its scale remains contested.

**Construction.** The Great Pyramid of Khufu, built around 2560 BCE and originally about 146 meters high, is conventionally estimated to contain approximately 2.3 million blocks. Mark Lehner and Zahi Hawass, who excavated the workers' settlement, estimate a labor force of 20,000–25,000 at any one time, including peasants serving in rotation—not the hundred thousand slaves described by Herodotus. For a country of roughly 1.5 million people, that was still several percent of its adult men diverted from the fields for two decades.

**Debt.** The Laws of Hammurabi (around 1750 BCE) cap interest at twenty percent per year on loans of silver and thirty-three and one-third percent on loans of grain. For comparison, if the harvest halved in a bad year, a peasant who borrowed one sack of barley had to return that sack plus another third a year later—from a granary that was already empty.

**Literacy.** Egyptologists John Baines and Christopher Eyre estimated literacy in Old Kingdom Egypt at roughly one percent of the population. No reliable estimate exists for Mesopotamia, but writing there too remained the craft of a narrow circle of scribes. The archives are nevertheless vast: Assyriologists have counted roughly one hundred thousand economic tablets from the Third Dynasty of Ur alone.

**Life expectancy and income.** Life expectancy at birth remained between twenty and thirty years, still determined above all by infant mortality. Income per person, measured in the manner of Angus Maddison, remained close to subsistence: his series begins only in the first year CE, but even then he estimated average world income at roughly $450–470 in 1990 international dollars—about the level of the poorest countries today. The surplus of the first states was enormous in aggregate and almost invisible per person: it resided in canals, archives, and pyramids, not in the peasant's bowl.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author's estimate. For the first time in this book, the calculation has documentary support: what we know about pyramid builders and rations comes from excavations and tablets, not only conjecture. But every link still rests on assumptions, and errors by a factor of several remain possible.

The price of a sack. Agriculture in the Nile Valley was among the most productive in the ancient world: the flood itself fertilized and flushed the fields, and yields were high. I therefore use the lower end of the range established in the introduction—fifteen to thirty hours of peasant labor for a fifty-kilogram sack. A working life is about seventy thousand hours.

### The Great Pyramid of Khufu

**The object.** The tomb of one king, built around 2560 BCE. As we have seen, it produced secondary benefits—experience in organization and logistics—but that experience could have been acquired more cheaply, so I count the pyramid as wasteful expenditure.

**Labor.** Mark Lehner and Zahi Hawass estimate a workforce of 20,000–25,000 at any one time, including those who quarried and transported the stone; construction continued for about twenty years. At 300–360 working days a year, that gives 120–180 million person-days. At ten hours a day, the total is 1.2–1.8 billion hours.

**Grain.** People had to be fed every day. At one kilogram of grain per day—and the Egyptian ration of bread and beer was no smaller, judging from the documents—20,000–25,000 people over twenty years consumed 150–180 million kilograms: about three to three and a half million sacks. I do not count the meat, fish, and onions that Lehner's excavations show were also brought to the workers' settlement.

**Hours.** At fifteen to thirty hours per sack, this represents 45–110 million hours of peasant labor.

**Lives.** Together with the builders' own hours, the total is 1.25–1.9 billion hours, or 18,000–27,000 working lives; if a working life is assumed to last 60,000–90,000 hours, the range widens to approximately 15,000–30,000. The Pyramid of Khufu cost Egypt the equivalent of about twenty thousand adults laboring from youth to old age for a single burial. By comparison, Silbury Hill from the previous chapter cost about 250 lives; the difference is nearly a hundredfold. At the scale of the whole country, this amounted to roughly five to seven percent of all adult labor in Egypt every year for two decades—if Karl Butzer's population estimate of 1.5 million is accepted.

One qualification in the pyramid's favor: some peasants worked during the flood season, when their fields were underwater anyway. If their hours are counted as free, the price falls but does not disappear: the grain they ate had been grown and did not enter the granary.

**The alternative.** More than three million sacks would have fed all of Egypt for roughly four to five months—enough to survive one catastrophically low flood with almost no famine. Such floods occurred in Egyptian history, and according to one interpretation it was a series of failed harvests that undermined the Old Kingdom three centuries after Khufu. The builders' hours could also have moved an enormous mass of earth. In field experiments conducted by American anthropologist Charles Erasmus in the 1960s, one person using primitive tools excavated roughly two to three cubic meters of soil per day. At that rate, 120–180 million person-days amount to hundreds of millions of cubic meters: dikes, flood-retention basins, and thousands of kilometers of canals—many times more than the entire Egyptian valley required. The figure is crude, but the scale is clear: the labor spent on one tomb could have rebuilt the irrigation system of an entire country.

### Sneferu's Pyramids

Khufu's father built three large pyramids, which together, by Egyptologists' calculations, consumed more stone than the Great Pyramid. Construction periods and workforce figures are less certain here, so I estimate crudely by volume: if cost was proportional to the amount of stone, they represented 20,000–40,000 working lives. By this estimate, father and son together spent 40,000–60,000 working lives on four tombs over half a century, in a country of roughly 1.5 million people.

### The Great Death Pit at Ur

Here the accounting is not in hours. The seventy-four people in the Great Death Pit were lives in the literal sense, as the introduction warned: I do not convert them into sacks. But the loss is substantial in hours as well: if they were adult craftspeople, musicians, and servants who were, on average, in the middle of their working lives, approximately fifteen hundred years of unrealized labor died with them—along with skills no one had time to inherit.

**The result of the calculation.** The Neolithic spent hundreds of lives on prestige; the first states spent tens of thousands. The nature of the choice did not change. Its scale did—and for the first time, that scale was determined by one person's decision.
