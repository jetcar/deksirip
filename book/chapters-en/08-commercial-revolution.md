# Chapter 8. The Commercial Revolution and the Age of Discovery

## Portrait of the Era

May 1498, the Malabar Coast of India. Three battered Portuguese ships drop anchor off Calicut, one of the richest ports in the Indian Ocean. Vasco da Gama commands them. Nearly a year of sailing lies behind them: around Africa, through storms off the Cape of Good Hope, and along the East African coast, where Arab and Swahili merchants met the strangers warily. A pilot hired in Malindi, who knew the monsoons as well as Lisbon sailors knew their river, guided the Portuguese across the ocean.

Calicut had not been waiting for Europeans and had no need of them. Ships from Arabia, Persia, Egypt, Gujarat, Malacca, and China converged here; pepper, ginger, cinnamon, cotton cloth, and precious stones changed hands under the watch of the ruler whom the Portuguese called the Zamorin. When da Gama prepared to present him with gifts, courtiers merely laughed, according to an anonymous participant in the voyage: a few pieces of striped cloth, hats, washbasins, coral, sugar, and honey. Even the poorest merchant from Mecca would offer such things, they said. Europe had nothing to offer India—apart from silver and, as soon became clear, cannon.

Less than fifty years passed, and the picture was reversed. In 1545, according to tradition, an Indigenous man named Diego Gualpa found a vein of silver on the slope of a mountain high in the Andes, in what is now Bolivia. The mountain was named Cerro Rico—the “Rich Mountain”—and the city that grew at its foot, Potosí. At an elevation of around four thousand meters, where the thin air makes it hard to breathe even while standing still, within a few decades there lived, historians estimate, roughly one hundred thousand people, and by the boldest contemporary counts as many as one hundred and sixty thousand—as many as in Seville, Spain’s largest city. Indigenous people were driven into the mines under a system of compulsory labor that the Spanish borrowed from the Inca and called the *mita*. The silver was smelted, cast into ingots, and minted into coin, then carried by mules and llamas to the Pacific, shipped from there to Panama, hauled across the isthmus to the Atlantic, and finally escorted to Seville by the “silver fleet.” There it was weighed and registered by officials of the Casa de Contratación, the House of Trade, established in 1503; the institution oversaw all trade and navigation with the Indies, but its chief concern was ensuring that not an ounce escaped the royal treasury.

But the silver did not remain in Seville. It went to Genoa and Antwerp—to bankers who had lent the king money against future fleets; to the Netherlands, Italy, and Germany—to pay for armies, cloth, and weapons; through the Baltic and the Levant, and later through Manila, reached from Acapulco by Spanish galleons beginning in 1571—to China, where silver was valued more highly than in Europe and exchanged for silk and porcelain. The Spanish coin, the “piece of eight,” became the first truly global money: it was known in London, Istanbul, Gujarat, and Canton.

And at the end of this chain comes a third scene. Amsterdam, 1611. A new exchange building opens in the square by the River Amstel: a rectangular courtyard with a colonnade, where merchants, brokers, and speculators gather during appointed hours, from noon until two. They trade not only Baltic grain and herring, but something entirely new—shares in the Dutch East India Company, the VOC, founded nine years earlier. A person who had never seen the sea could buy a small piece of future voyages to Java and the Moluccas, then sell it to a neighbor without waiting for the ships to return. Nearby, in the town hall, the Amsterdam Exchange Bank had operated since 1609: it accepted coins from every country, checked their weight and fineness, and kept accounts through which merchants settled payments without hauling sacks of metal.

Three scenes—three ways of handling wealth. The Portuguese entered someone else’s trading system with cannon and tried to exact tribute from it. The Spanish found a mountain of silver and turned it into wars and debts. The Dutch, who had neither silver mountains, nor spices, nor a large army, built a mechanism of trust: an exchange, a bank, and a company that made it possible to gather the savings of thousands of people into a single enterprise. It was this mechanism, not silver, that would become the foundation of the centuries ahead.

This chapter covers roughly 1450–1700—from the first Portuguese voyages along Africa to the time when Amsterdam yielded primacy to London. Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones estimate that Europe’s population grew during this period from around sixty million to well over one hundred million, while Europe became for the first time the center of a system linking every inhabited continent. But this system had another side, one that must not be discussed in a whisper. After the Europeans arrived, the population of the Americas fell—depending on the starting estimates—severalfold, perhaps even tenfold: through epidemics, wars, forced labor, and famine. According to the Slave Voyages database, around twelve and a half million Africans were carried across the Atlantic over the entire history of the trade, with a substantial part of that traffic occurring in the following century. The Commercial Revolution was also a revolution in plunder.

The chapter’s question is therefore the same as before, only on a different scale. For the first time, surplus was gathered not from a single valley or empire, but from the entire world. Where did it go? Into ships, maps, and navigational tables, into banks and exchanges—in other words, into tools and trust? Or into palaces, the Habsburgs’ endless wars, and coffers that filled and emptied without leaving behind workshops or knowledge? The answer, as we shall see, depended not on how much silver passed through a country, but on who controlled it and on what terms.

## Energy and Technology

If we asked what powered the economy of 1500, the answer would differ little from that for 1300: human and animal muscle, firewood, water, and wind. The Age of Discovery found no new source of energy. Its breakthrough lay elsewhere—in learning to use old sources farther afield and with greater precision. Above all, the wind.

A medieval European ship was either an oared galley suited to the calm Mediterranean, or a broad-bellied cog with a single square sail that performed well only before the wind. By the mid-fifteenth century, Portuguese and Spanish shipwrights had combined the northern tradition of a sturdy hull with the Mediterranean lateen sail, which allowed a vessel to sail at an angle to the wind. Thus appeared the caravel—a small, nimble vessel that could explore an unfamiliar coast and return against the wind. It was followed by the carrack, or *nao*: a large ship with three or four masts and a mixed sail plan, capable of carrying cargo, cannon, and crew across an ocean. Columbus’s *Santa María* was such a *nao*, while the *Niña* and *Pinta* were caravels.

A ship alone did not yet make the ocean navigable. Sailors had to know where they were. Longitude on the open sea could not be determined reliably until the marine chronometers of the eighteenth century, but latitude—the distance north or south of the equator—could be calculated from the altitude of the North Star, and farther south, where it fell below the horizon, from the altitude of the noonday sun. This required simple angle-measuring instruments—the quadrant and the mariner’s astrolabe—together with tables giving the sun’s declination for every day of the year. Such tables were compiled by the astronomer Abraham Zacuto, who worked in Salamanca under the patronage of the local bishop and later in Portugal; his *Almanac* was printed in 1496. A year later Zacuto, like all Jews in Portugal, faced the threat of forced conversion—a detail to which we will return in the section on destruction.

It is important to understand that this was not merely a collection of inventions, but a system for accumulating knowledge. The Portuguese crown kept a master chart in Lisbon, updating it with information from every returning voyage; in Seville the Padrón Real at the House of Trade served the same purpose, and pilots were examined there. Every voyage became experience, and experience became a shared resource. In 1569, the Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator published a world map in a projection where a line of constant bearing appears straight—a sailor could plot it with a ruler. Knowledge of the world began to be produced and reproduced almost like a commodity.

Reproduced in the literal sense. Around 1450 in Mainz, Johannes Gutenberg combined movable metal type, oil-based ink, and a press resembling a wine press. Within half a century, printing shops had appeared in hundreds of European cities. Eltjo Buringh and Jan Luiten van Zanden calculate that in the second half of the fifteenth century Europe printed around twelve million books—more than, by their own estimate, scribes had produced over the preceding centuries. The price of a book fell severalfold. For the economy this meant highly practical things: textbooks of commercial arithmetic, manuals on double-entry bookkeeping—Luca Pacioli’s treatise appeared in Venice in 1494—merchant handbooks, price lists, sailing directions, and maps. Printing made not texts in general, but applicable knowledge cheaper.

The era’s third technology—gunpowder and cannon—proved the most double-edged. A ship capable of carrying heavy artillery along its sides gave Europeans in the Indian Ocean an advantage not in trade, but in violence: the Portuguese could not offer Calicut better goods, but they could bombard it from the sea, as da Gama did on his second voyage. Cannon-inspired changes in fortification—low, thick walls with angular bastions, the so-called *trace italienne*—made wars longer and more expensive. This knowledge advanced engineering and metallurgy, but the lion’s share of the spending associated with it went into destruction. We will return to that as well.

The most interesting developments from the standpoint of this book’s argument occurred where there were neither silver mountains nor imperial fleets. The Dutch Republic suffered an acute shortage of firewood and had no coal of its own, but it stood on peat. In *The First Modern Economy*, economic historians Jan de Vries and Ad van der Woude showed how peat, extracted in enormous quantities and distributed through a network of canals, became fuel for breweries, brickworks, dyehouses, sugar refineries, saltworks, and domestic hearths. By their estimate, the seventeenth-century Netherlands consumed noticeably more energy per person than most of its neighbors. In their view, it was one of the first European economies, alongside England, which was shifting to coal during the same decades, to rely systematically on a fossil fuel—even if a young, marsh-born one. Some historians, Richard Unger among them, consider peat’s contribution more modest.

Wind on land became a second source. In 1594, miller Cornelis Corneliszoon of Uitgeest received a patent for a wind-powered sawmill whose crankshaft converted the rotation of the sails into the stroke of a saw. Cutting a log, which had previously required many days of hard manual labor, became, by rough estimates, several times or even an order of magnitude faster. Hundreds of windmills arose along the River Zaan north of Amsterdam, sawing timber, pressing oil, grinding pigments and tobacco, and making paper. Windmills also pumped water from lakes, turning them into polders—new agricultural land created by labor and engineering rather than conquest.

Those sawmills built the *fluyt*, the Dutch merchant vessel that appeared in Hoorn in the 1590s. The *fluyt* was essentially a floating warehouse: a long hull, narrow deck, simple rigging, and almost no cannon. Maritime historians such as Richard Unger estimate that it required one-third to one-half as many sailors per ton of cargo as comparable vessels belonging to neighboring countries. The Dutch built them in series, from standard designs and with mechanical saws, so *fluyts* were cheaper to build as well. Cheap freight made Amsterdam the intermediary in trade in Baltic grain and timber, Norwegian lumber, and French wine. This was a victory not of force, but of lower costs.

Finally, Potosí. Even here, at the edge of the world and within a system of coerced labor, technology delivered the decisive leap. By the 1570s the rich surface ore had been exhausted, and output fell. Amalgamation provided the answer—a method for extracting silver from low-grade ore with mercury, refined in Mexico by Bartolomé de Medina in the mid-1550s. In the 1570s, Viceroy Francisco de Toledo introduced it in Potosí together with the mass *mita*, and silver output rose severalfold. Mercury came from the Huancavelica mine in Peru and from Almadén in Spain. Both sides of the process—mining cinnabar and heating the amalgam—poisoned people with mercury vapor. The technology genuinely increased output per worker, but its fruits went to the royal treasury and the mine owners’ pockets, while Indigenous workers paid the price with their lungs and nerves.

Here, then, is the chapter’s first fork in the road. The era’s technological possibilities—the ocean-going ship, navigation, the printing press, peat, the windmill, and amalgamation—became available to many European countries at almost the same time. What differed was their use. Portugal used the ship and cannon to exact tribute from someone else’s trade. Spain used them to carry away silver. The Netherlands used them to reduce the cost of transport, sawing, drainage, and accounting. The first path brought rapid returns and required constant spending on fortresses and fleets. The second produced an enormous stream of metal that, as we shall see, flowed into other countries. The third offered few dramatic scenes, but made life a little cheaper every year—and it was the one that grew longest.

## Organization of Labor

Who, then, worked in this expanding world? Viewed as a whole, the era presents a striking paradox. The same trading system made labor freer in some places and less free in others. In Amsterdam and London, growing numbers of people lived on cash wages and decided for themselves whom to work for. In the Andes, Brazil, and the Caribbean, by contrast, forms of coercion emerged that Europe had not known since the Roman latifundia. These are not two different stories, but two sides of one chain: sugar grown by slaves in Barbados sweetened a London apprentice’s tea.

Let us begin with the Americas. Immediately after the conquest, the Spanish crown granted conquistadors *encomiendas*—the right to receive tribute and labor from particular Indigenous communities in return for an obligation to “protect and Christianize” them. In practice, it was the right to squeeze. The Dominican Bartolomé de las Casas, himself a former holder of an *encomienda*, renounced it and spent decades arguing at court that the system was exterminating people. He was heard in part: the New Laws of 1542 restricted the *encomienda*. But the demand for labor remained and was met in another way—the *mita* already mentioned. Under Viceroy Toledo’s reform, each community across a large area of the Andes had to send roughly one-seventh of its adult men to the mines of Potosí every year. They were paid, but their wages covered neither a journey hundreds of kilometers long nor life in a high-altitude city. Many communities preferred to buy their way out with money, while people fled their native villages simply to avoid the rolls. The surplus of this system was measured in ingots, but its costs in emptied settlements and workers poisoned by mercury.

Where epidemics had mown down Indigenous people and plantations required hard, continuous labor, Europeans turned to Africa. Sugarcane had to be cut at the right moment, then crushed and boiled immediately before the juice spoiled; a plantation with its own mill and boiling house operated like a brutal open-air factory long before the factories of Manchester. The Portuguese developed this model on São Tomé and Madeira, then transferred it to Brazil, which by the beginning of the seventeenth century had become the world’s largest sugar producer. In the 1640s, English colonists in Barbados, having learned the technology from Brazilians and Dutchmen, shifted from tobacco and European indentured servants to sugar and slaves. Many historians call this the “Sugar Revolution”; others, following Russell Menard, stress that the transition took decades, not years, and that tobacco, cotton, and indentured servants long coexisted with sugar and slaves. Either way, by the end of the century the island was one of the empire’s richest colonies—and one of the most terrible places on earth for those who worked there. Mortality on the plantations was so high that slaves were not so much born as continually purchased.

From the standpoint of our framework, caution is needed here. The plantation genuinely increased sugar output per worker compared with smallholdings—in a narrow accounting sense, it was a “productive” organization. But the book’s criteria require us to look beyond tons of output. The quality of life of most of those who produced the sugar was destroyed; knowledge and skills were not accumulated, but deliberately withheld—slaves were generally not taught to read, and later many colonies explicitly prohibited it by law; resilience was nonexistent, sustained by constant violence and the shipment of new people. The surplus born on plantations crossed the Atlantic to owners, merchants, and the treasury—while leaving behind in the colonies a society built on fear. We will return to this when discussing destruction.

Now to Europe. Here the principal change was inconspicuous but profound: work began to escape the authority of the guilds. The urban guilds discussed in the previous chapter restricted the number of masters and set rules for production. Merchants found a way around them. They distributed raw materials—wool, flax, yarn—to peasant families in villages where guild rules did not apply, then collected the finished yarn or cloth and paid for the work. The German tradition calls this the *Verlagssystem*, the English the “putting-out system.” In 1972, historian Franklin Mendels proposed the term “proto-industrialization” for this phenomenon: in Flanders, England, Silesia, and Switzerland, entire regions spent winter evenings spinning and weaving for markets hundreds of kilometers away. For a peasant family it was extra income in the dead season; for a merchant, cheap and flexible labor; for the economy, the first experience of mass production for distant markets. This freedom had a price as well: the outworker depended on the merchant who supplied the materials and dictated the rates, and when demand fell was left without an income.

Within the Dutch Republic, an almost modern labor market took shape. Leiden was one of Europe’s largest clothmaking centers, and workers streamed there from across Flanders, many of them Protestant refugees from the south. The fleet, fisheries, and trade required tens of thousands of sailors; a substantial share were foreigners—Germans and Scandinavians. During its nearly two centuries of existence, the East India Company sent roughly a million people to Asia, according to historians Jaap Bruijn and Femme Gaastra. Many never returned from distant voyages: scurvy, tropical diseases, and shipwrecks took an enormous share of crews. A VOC soldier or sailor signed a contract years in advance, received an advance that often went to recruiters, and could find himself thousands of miles from home without the right to leave. Free hiring and harsh dependence coexisted here as well.

Yet it was in northwestern Europe that what Jan de Vries called the “industrious revolution” occurred. In the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, households in Holland and England began working more hours, while more family members—wives and adolescent children—sold their labor or its products on the market. Why? Not because they were forced, but because the market now offered things worth buying: calico, tea, sugar, tobacco, earthenware, clocks, mirrors, and printed pictures. Families traded some leisure and household production for money in order to buy goods that had previously been out of reach. Using price and wage data, economist Robert Allen showed that real wages in London and Amsterdam remained relatively high during this period, while in most of Europe—from Florence to Vienna and Kraków—they fell noticeably after 1500. What distinguished the northwest was not that its workers lived in luxury, but that their labor was expensive—and this, as we shall see in later chapters, would create an incentive to replace it with machines.

This picture also has a dark side, one that bears directly on Keynes’s question. The industrious revolution meant that the surplus created by trade and new technology went not into free time, but into new wants. People began working more in order to consume more. Some of these new goods genuinely improved life—cheap dishes that were easier to clean, cotton underwear, books. Some were pure status and habit—tobacco, sugar, fashionable fabrics needed only to avoid falling behind one’s neighbor. And almost all colonial “pleasures” were grown by slave labor. Thus three strands that will run through the rest of this book became intertwined in a single era: rising productivity, rising consumption for prestige, and the price paid for others’ consumption by people who had no voice.

## Exchange and Money

Imagine a Spanish peasant from near Toledo who lived a long life—say, from 1520 to 1590. In his youth, he sold a *fanega* of wheat—a measure of grain weighing roughly fifty kilograms—for one price; in old age, he demanded several times more for the same *fanega*. This did not make him richer: cloth, iron, wine, and taxes had all risen in price as well. He did not understand why this was happening and blamed middlemen, usurers, and foreigners. The answer lay across the ocean, in Potosí and Mexico’s Zacatecas.

In 1934, after combing through the accounts of Spanish hospitals, monasteries, and town councils, American economist Earl Hamilton constructed the first long price series and showed that prices in Spain rose roughly three- to fourfold over the sixteenth century. The increase was slower in other European countries, but occurred everywhere from England to Poland. Historians called it the “Price Revolution.” Hamilton’s figures have since been refined, and the causes remain disputed: population recovery after the plague-era collapse, coin debasement, and faster monetary circulation all played a part. Using Dutch newspapers, Michel Morineau showed that more silver arrived in the seventeenth century than Seville’s records report, while Jack Goldstone considers population growth the principal driver of prices. Yet few deny the connection to the influx of American silver. By annual standards, inflation was modest—around one to two percent on average—but over the course of a generation it reshaped incomes: those who sold goods and borrowed gained, while those who lived on fixed rents or salaries lost.

The most interesting point is that contemporaries already understood the mechanism. In 1556, the theologian Martín de Azpilcueta, from the circle of the University of Salamanca, observed that in Spain, where money was plentiful, everything was more expensive than in France, where money was scarce—simply because something abundant was valued less. Twelve years later, the Frenchman Jean Bodin, arguing with a royal official who blamed everything on coin debasement, reached the same conclusion: the chief cause of high prices was the abundance of gold and silver. This was one of the first formulations of the idea later called the quantity theory of money: if the quantity of money grows faster than the quantity of goods, each coin buys less.

From this follows a simple conclusion, though it was not obvious at the time, worth pausing over. Silver does not in itself make a country richer. It is merely a claim on things produced by others. If a country gains no more grain, cloth, or ships, but only more metal, the metal loses value and real goods flow to those who produce them. Spain found itself in the position of a man who had discovered a purse but lived in a house where almost nothing could be made for sale. The silver paid for what others produced: Flemish cloth, Italian silk, German weapons, Baltic grain, mercenary armies. Later economists would call this effect the “resource curse” and, in the twentieth century after the discovery of an enormous gas field in Groningen, the “Dutch disease”: easy money from natural wealth drives up domestic prices and wages, makes local production uncompetitive, and discourages the building of workshops when finished goods can simply be bought. Historians dispute how severely this damaged Castilian crafts—Mauricio Drelichman has examined the thesis in detail for Spain—but the direction of the blow is not in doubt.

Where, then, did the silver ultimately settle? The answer was long sought in Europe and found in China. Historians Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez showed that global silver trade was born, in their phrase, “with a silver spoon in its mouth”—thanks to Chinese demand. In the sixteenth century, after unsuccessful experiments with paper money, the Ming Empire shifted taxes to silver: the Single Whip reform consolidated numerous taxes and labor obligations into a single payment in metal. An enormous economy needed an enormous amount of silver, which it did not possess. In China, silver exchanged for gold at a rate roughly twice as favorable to the silver seller as in Europe. Metal therefore flowed there from everywhere—from Japan, which was also experiencing a silver boom, from the Americas through Manila, and from Europe through India and the Levant. A substantial share of Andean silver ultimately ended up in China—between a third and half of global output by Flynn and Giráldez’s estimates, though Richard von Glahn considers these shares exaggerated—while silk, porcelain, and later tea spread around the world in return. For the first time, the same coin connected a miner in Potosí, a banker in Genoa, and a taxpayer in the Yangtze Delta.

Now let us return to the Spanish king. Despite the flood of silver, Philip II suspended payments on his debts four times: in 1557, 1560, 1575, and 1596; his son Philip III did so in 1607. How was this possible? The crown’s share amounted to only about one-fifth of the metal mined—the so-called royal fifth—while wars in the Netherlands and against the Ottomans, France, and England cost more. The crown borrowed short-term from Genoese and German bankers through *asientos*, secured against future silver fleets. Using bills of exchange, the bankers delivered money wherever the armies stood—in Flanders or Milan. When a fleet was late or a war dragged on, the debts were converted into long-term bonds called *juros* at lower interest, effectively forcing creditors to share the loss. In *Lending to the Borrower from Hell*, economists Mauricio Drelichman and Hans-Joachim Voth argue that this was not an insane but a perfectly rational system: bankers priced the risk into the interest rate and, on average, still came out ahead. But something else matters for our question. The crown’s share of silver had been pledged to war before it even reached Seville, while most military spending still fell on Castilian taxpayers: Drelichman and Voth calculate that America provided only about one-fifth to one-quarter of Philip II’s revenues. Private silver, meanwhile, passed through Spain in transit, turning into soldiers’ pay and interest for the Genoese.

It is worth explaining here what a bill of exchange is, because it—not silver—was the true engine of trade. A bill is a written order: “Pay a specified sum in Antwerp to this person on my account.” A merchant in Florence paid a local banker, who drew a bill on his partner in Bruges, and a few weeks later the partner counted out the money to the recipient. No metal traveled over dangerous roads, while offsetting debts were netted at fairs—in Lyon, later in Besançon and Piacenza—where bankers met several times a year and canceled enormous sums against one another through entries in their books. In time, a bill could also be used to pay a third party by endorsing it. Thus, long before paper money, Europe learned to create means of payment out of trust.

The Dutch took the next step—they turned this trust into a public institution. Dozens of types of coin circulated in the Republic, differing in weight and fineness, often worn and clipped; settling accounts in such currency was a lottery. The Amsterdam Exchange Bank, founded by the city in 1609, accepted coins, assessed them by their actual metal content, and credited the customer’s account in “bank guilders.” Municipal ordinance required large bills of exchange to be settled through the bank. Money in its accounts was more reliable than coins in a pocket—and therefore commanded a small premium called the *agio*. Adam Smith would later analyze the bank in detail in *The Wealth of Nations* as a model. The bank did not print money and officially made no loans; its product was certainty: a guilder in an account was exactly a guilder.

Beside the bank stood the exchange, and on the exchange traded shares of the East India Company. When it was founded in 1602, the VOC raised around six and a half million guilders from more than a thousand investors—from wealthy merchants to artisans and maidservants who invested small sums. The capital was locked into the company for the long term—initially ten years, and when the settlement with investors never took place in 1612, effectively forever: an investor could not withdraw his share, but could sell it to someone else. This arrangement—locked-in capital plus freely tradable shares—proved one of history’s most important social technologies. The enterprise could build shipyards and fortresses decades ahead, while an investor could leave the venture any day. Speculators appeared almost at once: as early as 1609, the merchant Isaac Le Maire, a former company director who had fallen out with its management, organized a short-selling campaign against its shares, and in 1610 the States General prohibited the sale of securities the seller did not own. The first stock-market regulation was born almost simultaneously with the market itself.

Nor can we pass over the famous “Tulip Mania” of 1636–1637, when prices for bulbs of rare varieties soared to absurd heights for several months and collapsed. It is often cited as an example of market madness. Historian Anne Goldgar, who studied notarial and court archives, showed that the scale of events was greatly exaggerated by later moralists: trading was conducted chiefly by prosperous enthusiasts, and there was no wave of bankruptcies or economic collapse. Bubbles do occur in new financial markets—and we will see them more than once—but to judge the Dutch system by tulips is to miss the main point.

And the main point was the price of money. In the seventeenth century, the province of Holland borrowed at interest rates that by mid-century had fallen to roughly four percent and later still lower; private borrowers with good reputations paid little more. During the same decades, the Spanish crown paid bankers noticeably more—and still periodically paid nothing at all. A low interest rate meant that projects with long horizons and modest returns could pay: draining a polder, building a *fluyt*, erecting a mill, or making a trading voyage to the Baltic. Cheap credit is, in essence, the price of trust: the less a borrower can take from a lender at whim, the more cheaply others will lend. Here, not in the quantity of silver, lay the difference between the two economies. Spain received metal and spent it in advance. Holland had almost no metal, but built institutions that allowed thousands of people to lend one another their savings and put them to work. What sustained this trust, and why some states could create it while others could not, is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

In June 1494, in the Castilian town of Tordesillas, the envoys of Spain and Portugal, negotiating directly and shifting the line drawn a year earlier by papal bull, drew a new line north to south on the map, 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde Islands. Everything discovered or yet to be discovered west of it belonged to Castile; everything east, to Portugal. Two kingdoms divided between them a world most of which none of the signatories had ever seen. The scene is revealing: at the beginning of the age, the ocean was considered the property of crowns and trade a royal privilege to be granted, sold, or withdrawn.

Portugal put this idea systematically into practice. Its Estado da Índia—a network of fortresses from Mozambique to Malacca, centered on Goa, seized in 1510—was less a trading company than a collector of tolls on other people’s trade. Asian merchants had to buy a Portuguese safe-conduct called a *cartaz*; a ship without one could be seized or sunk. Pepper traveled to Lisbon on royal ships, and the treasury controlled its sale. Crown revenues were impressive, but historian Niels Steensgaard estimates that almost all of them were consumed by garrisons, fleets, and officials’ salaries, while the officials also traded on their own account. Other historians, Sanjay Subrahmanyam among them, paint a less bleak picture: in its best decades, the treasury did make a profit. Portugal gained not a new economy but a new tax collected by cannon—and it lasted only until its rivals acquired cannon just as good.

The Spanish monarchy arranged matters differently, but according to the same logic. Trade with America passed only through Seville, later Cádiz, only in convoys, and only through merchants enrolled in Seville’s guild, the *consulado*. The crown received its fifth of the silver mined and customs duties, the guild received a monopoly, and all other subjects received the right to pay three times the price for colonial goods or buy them from smugglers. Something else, however, mattered more. The Castilian Cortes—the assembly of estates legally required to approve new taxes—lost its former power after the revolt of the Comuneros was suppressed in 1521. It continued to vote taxes, including from 1590 a large new levy called the *millones*, and, as historian I. A. A. Thompson showed, could bargain over terms; the Cortes did not truly weaken until the mid-seventeenth century. But American silver went directly to the king, bypassing any approval: this was revenue over which he did not have to negotiate with his subjects at all. It also made it convenient to sell offices, titles of nobility, monopoly rights, and tax exemptions. The famous Mesta, an association of sheep owners, enjoyed for centuries the privilege of driving flocks across other people’s fields because it paid the crown. A state that receives income without negotiating with anyone rarely thinks about making that income grow—it thinks about how to spend it.

The Dutch Republic was born from the opposite experience—from revolt against that very Spanish king. The Union of Utrecht of 1579 joined the northern provinces in an alliance, and in 1581 the States General formally renounced Philip II. The resulting state looked strange even to contemporaries: no king, no capital in the strict sense, seven provinces each with its own estates, and dozens of cities each with its own council. Decisions on taxes were made by assemblies in which sat representatives of the very merchants who paid those taxes. Power belonged to the so-called regents—a narrow circle of wealthy urban families—and this was not democracy in our sense: artisans and sailors had no part in government. But the regents were also creditors of the state. Thousands of inhabitants bought the province of Holland’s loans, and rulers who themselves held bonds had no interest in defaulting on them. Here lies the answer to the question that ended the previous section: trust in Dutch debt rested, roughly speaking, on the fact that borrower and creditor were the same people.

To this was added what historian Jonathan Israel calls practical religious toleration in *The Dutch Republic*. The Reformed Church was official, but Catholics, Lutherans, Mennonites, and Jews in the cities were generally left alone if they did not display their faith too openly. Protestants from Antwerp, captured by the Spanish in 1585, Sephardic Jews expelled from Spain and Portugal, and Huguenots from France came to Amsterdam. Each brought capital, connections, and a craft. Here politics passed directly into economics: a country that does not expel specialists receives them from neighbors that do.

A distinctive institution of the era was the chartered trading company. The English East India Company received a royal charter in 1600, and the Dutch VOC one from the States General in 1602. The VOC’s charter gave it a monopoly on trade east of the Cape of Good Hope and the right to conclude treaties, build fortresses, maintain troops, and wage war. It was a hybrid of private enterprise and state, and in Asia the company behaved like a state in the worst sense. In 1621, Governor-General Jan Pieterszoon Coen, seeking a monopoly on nutmeg, conquered the Banda Islands; most of the local population was killed, starved, or sold into slavery—historians estimate that of roughly fifteen thousand inhabitants only around one thousand remained—while the land was distributed to colonists who worked it with slave labor. The same company that created the first liquid market in shares in Amsterdam imposed a monopoly through massacre at the other end of the world. Institutions that restrained arbitrary power at home did nothing to restrain it overseas.

Above all this stood the era’s dominant economic doctrine, later called mercantilism. Its premise was simple: a country’s wealth was its stock of gold and silver, and trade was a game in which one side’s gain was the other’s loss. Hence its prescriptions: export more than was imported, prohibit the export of metal, impose tariffs on foreign goods, promote domestic manufactures, and seize colonies as closed markets. In 1651, England adopted the Navigation Act, prohibiting goods from being imported except on English ships or ships belonging to the producing country—a blow aimed directly at Dutch intermediation, followed by three Anglo-Dutch wars. In France, from the 1660s Louis XIV’s minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert built royal manufactories, regulated the width of cloth and the number of threads, and dreamed of driving the Dutch from the seas.

Mercantilism was not merely an error. Support for manufactures, the fleet, and skills sometimes did create production, and economic historians still debate how much English protectionism contributed to the later industrial leap. But its central idea—that wealth was metal rather than the ability to produce—was precisely the misconception Spain tested upon itself. If trade is understood as a war over a finite stock, it is logical to spend the surplus on fleets and wars for markets. If it is understood as mutual exchange from which both sides gain, it is logical to spend it on roads, ports, and lower costs. For an entire century, European states acted according to the first logic.

There is a well-known attempt to draw these observations into a single picture. In a 2005 article on the rise of Europe, economists Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, and James Robinson showed that Atlantic trade enriched above all those countries where monarchical power was already constrained to some degree—England and the Netherlands. There, the profits of the ocean went to a broad stratum of merchants, who then used them to constrain the crown still further and protect their property. In Spain and Portugal, the same trade was a royal monopoly and strengthened the crown—and with it everything that impeded growth. Not all historians accept this explanation: some emphasize war and geography, while others point out that England’s “constrained” monarchy of the sixteenth century was not nearly so constrained. But for the question of this book, the conclusion holds: the ocean gave surplus to everyone, while the rules governing whether and how power could take it determined what that surplus became.

## What Fostered Development

If we set aside fleets, fortresses, and silver caravans and ask what from this era continued to yield benefits a century or two later, the list looks surprisingly modest. It contains almost no resounding events. But it does contain the potato, a scheduled barge, a cheap book, and locked-in capital. Let us examine the mechanisms in turn.

The first, and perhaps most underestimated, was the exchange of living organisms between hemispheres. In 1972, historian Alfred Crosby called it the “Columbian Exchange.” From the Americas came potatoes, maize, sweet potatoes, beans, tomatoes, squashes, peanuts, peppers, and cacao; in the opposite direction went wheat, rice, sugarcane, horses, cattle, sheep, and pigs. No decree or company produced such an increase in food. On the same area of land, potatoes yielded noticeably more calories than rye or oats, grew in poor and cold soils, and did not require threshing. Maize and sweet potatoes in China allowed hillsides where rice would not grow to be cultivated. In a 2011 article, economists Nathan Nunn and Nancy Qian estimated that the spread of the potato explains roughly a quarter of the growth in Old World population and urbanization between 1700 and 1900. This is an estimate, and its precision can be debated, but the order of magnitude is impressive. It also matters who received the benefit: potatoes were food for the poor, did not need to be taken to market or surrendered to a lord, and grew in gardens beside the house. This is a rare case in which an era’s surplus went directly onto the table of the majority.

This exchange also had a terrible half: smallpox, measles, and influenza traveled to the Americas with horses and cattle, as the next section will discuss. But the plants that crossed the ocean continued to feed people long after the empires that had carried them collapsed.

The second mechanism was cheaper transport—the reduction of transaction costs in the most literal sense. We have already discussed the *fluyt*. But the Dutch also had a less famous invention: a network of passenger canals. Beginning in the 1630s, the cities of Holland dug straight canals one after another and launched the *trekschuit* on them—a covered barge towed by a horse walking along the bank. Jan de Vries, who studied this system in *Barges and Capitalism*, shows that the barges ran to published timetables, many times a day, in almost any weather, and for a fixed fare. From Amsterdam to Haarlem, one could depart at an appointed hour and arrive at an appointed hour. For us this is commonplace; for the seventeenth century, almost a miracle: regular public transport two hundred years before the railways. The network was built not by a king or conqueror, but by the cities themselves, which reached agreements with one another and invested loans at modest interest. No single investment brought extraordinary profit, but every day each saved time for thousands of people.

The third mechanism was specialization made possible by cheap transport. A Dutch farmer did not try to grow everything his family needed on wet soil. Grain came cheaply from the Baltic through Danzig, while local farms shifted to what worked best there: milk, cheese, and butter; fattening cattle; flax, hops, and madder for dyehouses; vegetables and flowers for the cities. De Vries and van der Woude estimate that by the mid-seventeenth century only a minority of the inhabitants of the province of Holland worked in agriculture, while its output per worker was among the highest in Europe. This is the gain from exchange that Adam Smith and David Ricardo would describe a century and a half later: each person does what he does relatively better and obtains the rest through exchange. Mercantilists saw imported grain as a leakage of money; in fact, it freed hands for more valuable work.

The polders belong here as well. From the late sixteenth to the mid-seventeenth century, dozens of lakes in North Holland were drained—the Beemster, Purmer, Wormer, and Schermer. The drainage of the Beemster, completed in 1612, was financed by Amsterdam merchants who invested in the project as a business venture: engineer Jan Adriaanszoon Leeghwater installed several dozen windmills around its perimeter, and within a few years cows grazed on the bed of the former lake. This was new land taken not from a neighbor, but created from water, capital, and engineering knowledge. Compare it with Spanish campaigns for land and tribute: the same thirst for land, two opposite ways to satisfy it.

The fourth mechanism was knowledge made cheap and widely available. We have already discussed the printing press; what matters here is how it was used. By the mid-seventeenth century, Amsterdam and Leiden had become one of Europe’s principal centers of publishing: works that were banned or that printers did not dare publish in other countries appeared here—from Descartes’s writings to maps and sailing directions. The Blaeu publishing house sold atlases across the continent, while the Elzevirs sold inexpensive pocket-sized books. Leiden University, founded in 1575 as a reward to the city for withstanding a Spanish siege, admitted students of different confessions and became one of Europe’s strongest. Historians of education estimate that literacy in the Dutch Republic was among the highest on the continent. Information about prices, exchange rates, ships, and cargoes appeared in regular exchange price lists and the first newspapers: one of the earliest known Dutch-language newspapers was published in Amsterdam in 1618. Information that in Venice had been a secret of merchant houses became in Amsterdam a commodity available to anyone willing to pay a few coins. And the more people know the prices, the harder it is for one of them to profit from another’s ignorance.

The fifth mechanism is what the preceding two sections have discussed, but it should now be named directly: social technologies of trust. A joint-stock company with locked-in capital and freely tradable shares; an exchange bank with a reliable unit of account; an exchange with public prices; marine insurance; the bill of exchange; public debt that is serviced regularly. None of these things produced grain or cloth. But each reduced the cost of cooperation among strangers: the cost of finding a partner, checking him, reaching an agreement, and making him keep his promise. When that cost falls, thousands of transactions and projects that previously did not pay become profitable. London, Paris, and the entire world would later adopt these institutions. The silver of Potosí has long since been melted down and spent; the joint-stock company and the exchange live on.

The sixth mechanism was openness to people. The section on power has already addressed it, but the contribution of migrants is worth illustrating with examples. Leiden’s clothmaking was transferred to a considerable degree by refugees from Hondschoote and other cities of the southern Netherlands; Amsterdam’s sugar and diamond trades, and its connections with Portugal and Brazil, rested largely on Sephardic families; Huguenots fleeing France after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 brought silk weaving, watchmaking, papermaking, and glassmaking to the Netherlands, England, and Prussia. In that age, knowledge passed above all from hand to hand, from master to apprentice. To expel a master was to give one’s neighbor what generations had accumulated.

Finally, it should be admitted honestly that even projects conceived for plunder contained a seed of development. Portuguese and Spanish voyages undertaken for tribute and gold nevertheless left the world maps, navigational tables, knowledge of monsoons and currents, and descriptions of plants and peoples. Amalgamation in Potosí was a genuine technological achievement. But this development arose as a by-product, and its fruits—open knowledge—were put to best use not by those who paid for it, but by those who knew how to reproduce it.

If we draw all this together and ask where the part of the surplus that genuinely fostered development went in this era, the answer is: into calories for the poor brought from across the ocean; into canals, mills, and polders built by cities and merchant partnerships; into printed knowledge and universities; into institutions that made it possible to trust a stranger. All share one feature: each investment made something once expensive and scarce cheaper and more accessible—food, transport, information, and credit. And none required taking anything from a neighbor. That is what distinguishes them from the subject of the next section.

## What Destroyed

November 1532, the city of Cajamarca in the northern Andes. Francisco Pizarro, with a force of fewer than two hundred men, lures Atahualpa—the ruler of the Inca Empire, fresh from victory in a civil war—into the square. An ambush, a cannon shot, a cavalry charge, several thousand people from his nearly unarmed retinue killed—and Atahualpa is captured. For his freedom, he promises to fill one room with gold to the height of his raised hand, and the two adjoining rooms with silver. Vessels, statues, and plates from temple walls are gathered across the empire. The ransom is delivered; Atahualpa is executed anyway in 1533, and the assembled treasures are melted into ingots to be divided among the expedition and to send the royal fifth to Spain. The work of several generations of goldsmiths became metal valued only by weight.

This scene is the short answer to the question of what the era destroyed. It annihilated not only people and property, but what our framework values most—the knowledge societies had accumulated and their ability to transmit it.

The first and greatest destruction was the demographic catastrophe of the Americas. No one knows exactly how many people lived in the New World before 1492: historians’ and demographers’ estimates range from fewer than ten million to more than one hundred million. In his synthesis, geographer William Denevan settled on just over fifty million; in a 2019 article, a group of researchers led by Alexander Koch estimated that by 1600 roughly fifty-six million of an original sixty million had died—around nine-tenths of the initial population. Even using cautious figures, this was the largest proportional population decline in history. The principal killers were not swords but diseases—smallpox, measles, influenza, and typhus—to which the inhabitants of the Americas had no immunity. Here it is important to avoid an oversimplification: epidemics were not an “accident of nature” separable from conquest. War, forced labor, the concentration of people in new settlements, and famine weakened communities, and disease finished off those who no longer had the strength to resist. Historians of the Caribbean believe that the Indigenous population of Hispaniola had almost vanished within a few decades of Columbus’s arrival.

Entire systems of knowledge disappeared with the people. Andean terraces and canals, which required collective maintenance every year, fell into neglect when no workers remained. The Inca storehouses discussed in Chapter 6 were plundered. Quipu—the knotted records on which the empire’s accounting rested—were associated with old rites and declared objects of idolatry: the Third Council of Lima in 1583 ordered their destruction. Accounting quipu survived for a long time in the colonial administration, but the tradition gradually faded. In July 1562, in the town of Maní in Yucatán, the Franciscan Diego de Landa staged an auto-da-fé at which Maya manuscript books were burned; only four codices survive today, and the authenticity of one was long disputed. Astronomical tables, calendars, history, agricultural knowledge—much of what a people had recorded for centuries burned that day, and other fires and time destroyed the rest. This is destruction in its purest form: the surplus was not taken but burned, and no one became richer.

The second destruction was the transatlantic slave trade. According to the Slave Voyages database, around twelve and a half million people were taken from Africa between 1501 and 1866, and about ten million seven hundred thousand reached the Americas alive—the rest died in the holds during the so-called Middle Passage across the ocean. An unknown but enormous number also died within Africa itself—in wars and raids waged to capture them, and on the road to the coast. The trade was only gathering momentum in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries and would peak in the eighteenth, but its mechanism took shape in this era. In a 2008 study, economist Nathan Nunn compared the number of enslaved people taken from different parts of Africa with their present-day income and found that the more people were taken from a region, the poorer it is today. His explanation, supported by his 2011 joint work with Leonard Wantchekon, is that the slave trade corroded trust. When a neighbor can sell a neighbor, and a chief his subjects, people cease to trust either kin or authority, while states are built around capturing people rather than producing. We have already seen that trust is one of an economy’s principal forms of capital. The slave trade destroyed it across an entire continent, and the data show that these losses have not been repaired to this day.

The third destruction was the expulsion of those with knowledge and skill. In 1492, the year of Columbus’s voyage, the Jews of Castile and Aragon were given a choice between baptism and exile; in 1497, most in Portugal were simply converted by force—and the astronomer Abraham Zacuto, whose tables guided Portuguese sailors around Africa, left the country and, via Tunis, ended his life in the Middle East, in Jerusalem or Damascus. How many left Spain is disputed: estimates range from several tens of thousands to one hundred and fifty thousand or more. Many converts remained under suspicion from the Inquisition, and even a century later their descendants were barred from many offices by rules of “purity of blood.” Between 1609 and 1614, Spain expelled the Moriscos—descendants of Muslims who had converted to Christianity—around three hundred thousand people. In Valencia and Aragon, they formed a substantial share of the rural population and possessed skills in irrigated agriculture; entire villages emptied, and landowners lost their tenants. Finally, the Index of Prohibited Books issued by the papacy in 1559 and the Spanish decree of the same year forbidding subjects to study at most foreign universities cut the country off from the part of European knowledge then growing fastest. We have seen what the Dutch gained from these expulsions. Spain lost it.

The fourth was the wars that silver not only paid for but inflamed. The fate of Antwerp is especially instructive. In the mid-sixteenth century, the city was Europe’s principal market: Portuguese spices, English cloth, German copper, and Spanish silver all came there. In November 1576, Spanish soldiers who had gone unpaid for months—the crown had suspended payments in 1575—mutinied and sacked the city. The “Spanish Fury,” as those days became known, killed several thousand townspeople by varying estimates and destroyed hundreds of houses. In 1585, Antwerp fell to the Spanish after a siege, while the northern provinces closed the mouth of the Scheldt; Protestants were given several years to leave, and they left—for Amsterdam, Leiden, and Middelburg. The chain is striking: unpaid debt became mutiny, mutiny became the devastation of Europe’s greatest trading city, and its capital and craftsmen became the foundation of a rival.

The Thirty Years’ War of 1618–1648, in which the Habsburgs fought almost all of Europe, devastated the German lands. Historian Peter Wilson and other researchers estimate the Holy Roman Empire’s population losses at around one-fifth or more, and in some regions at half the inhabitants or still higher; most died not in battle, but from famine, epidemics, and the plunder of armies that lived off the land. Contemporaries called this the principle that “war feeds war”: an army extracted the surplus itself from those whose lands it crossed and destroyed what it could not carry away. Villages, mills, livestock, seed grain—everything peasants had accumulated over decades vanished with a single passage of troops.

The fifth was the destruction of a country’s own money. When silver was no longer sufficient for its wars, Spain found a simple way out. From the beginning of the seventeenth century, the crown minted ever larger quantities of small change from pure copper, called *vellón*, and in 1603 reminted copper coins already in circulation, doubling their face value. Copper drove silver from circulation, prices in copper coin rose, and the crown alternately raised and slashed the face value of *vellón*—in 1628 it was cut in half at a stroke, and holders of copper lost half their money by decree. Every such step was a hidden tax and at the same time a blow to trust: a merchant who does not know what his money will be worth in a year prefers not to invest it in a long-term venture. We encountered this mistake in third-century Rome and will encounter it again in the twentieth century.

Taken together, all this reveals a common feature. The era’s destruction was not a side effect of trade—it was its alternative. Trade and production require both sides of a transaction to remain alive and want to meet again. Seizure, expulsion, and the corruption of money proceed from the belief that one person can gain only by taking from another. Mercantilist doctrine, which treated the world’s wealth as a fixed pie, supplied a theoretical justification. But the outcome leaves no room for doubt: what was taken from the Americas, Africa, the Moriscos, Antwerp, and German peasants did not become lasting wealth for the victors. Atahualpa’s gold was melted down and spent on wars within a generation. Where this stream of metal went besides war, and what it left behind, is the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

More than forty kilometers from Madrid, at the foot of the Guadarrama Mountains, stands an immense gray granite building: El Escorial. Philip II began its construction in 1563 and completed it in 1584. It is at once a monastery, palace, royal tomb, library, and basilica, arranged in a severe rectangle with hundreds of windows. The king personally supervised construction and spent the final years of his life there, in a small room from which he could see the altar through a window. El Escorial is a magnificent monument, and its library preserved rare manuscripts. But in economic terms it was what the pyramids were to Egypt and cathedrals to a medieval city: an immense investment of surplus in stone that produced nothing, taught no one a craft beyond the building site, and made not a single thing in the country cheaper. It was built during the very years when the crown again suspended payments on its debts in 1575.

Wasteful spending differs from destruction in that it destroys nothing: houses do not burn, people do not die. Resources simply flow somewhere from which they do not return as new possibilities. In this era they followed three principal channels—prestige, the arms race, and rent.

Let us begin with prestige. Before flowing away to Genoa and China, Spanish silver settled generously into gilding. The churches of Potosí, Lima, and Mexico City were covered with gilded *retablos*, silver revetments, and candlesticks. This was art of a high order, and today it attracts tourists. But for seventeenth-century Andean society, it was surplus extracted from *mita* workers and frozen in metal above the altar—while those same provinces built neither roads nor schools for Indigenous communities. Similar logic operated at court. Beginning in the 1660s, Louis XIV transformed his father’s hunting lodge at Versailles into a residence to which the court and government moved in 1682. Versailles was conceived as an instrument of power: nobles who lived beside the king and spent fortunes on clothes, balls, and gambling ceased to be dangerous in the provinces. Politically, this may have worked. Economically, it was a machine for converting income from land into status consumption.

The clearest symbol is the Machine de Marly. In the 1680s, engineers Arnold de Ville and Rennequin Sualem built a gigantic system on the Seine, with fourteen waterwheels and hundreds of pumps, that raised water up a hill more than one hundred and fifty meters high. By the standards of the age, it was an engineering marvel—one of the largest machines in the world. The water was intended chiefly for the fountains of Versailles and Marly. The same energy and knowledge could have drained mines or irrigated fields, but instead they were directed toward making jets rise higher. Compare this with the windmills of the Zaan and Beemster discussed above: technologically related things, economically opposites.

The second channel was the arms race. In the section on technology, we mentioned the new fortresses with angular bastions. In the 1950s, historian Michael Roberts called the changes in warfare the “Military Revolution,” and Geoffrey Parker developed the idea, making the new fortress its principal driver; some historians, Jeremy Black among them, believe the changes were gradual. Either way, fortresses became harder to capture, sieges longer, armies larger, and all of it increasingly expensive. The logic was the same as in later arms races. Every state had to build bastions because its neighbor did; each new fortification canceled the advantage of the previous cannon, reducing the gain from expenditure to zero while the expenditure remained. For years, Spain maintained the Army of Flanders—described by Parker in detail in *The Army of Flanders and the Spanish Road*—supplying it through an overland corridor across Italy, Savoy, and Franche-Comté. Some of this money, of course, went into the destruction discussed in the previous section. But an enormous share went merely into upkeep: garrisons, repairs to walls, wages paid so that nothing would happen. Portugal’s network of fortresses in the Indian Ocean belongs in the same category: by Steensgaard’s estimate, its cost consumed almost all the revenue for which it existed.

Here we need the same honest qualification we made for the pyramids. Military spending was not entirely barren: it stimulated metallurgy, casting, cartography, engineering, and the organization of supply. Parker and his followers argued that this arms race created strong European states with tax systems and bureaucracies that later proved useful for many other purposes. This is the classic dual case. But the scale is worth comparing: by my estimate—and this is a judgment, not a calculation—for every coin of incidental engineering benefit, many coins burned in gunpowder and stone. The Dutch fought as well—for eighty years against Spain, then against England and France—and military debts also ruined them, especially in the eighteenth century. The difference was that their budget found room for canals and polders alongside war.

The third channel was rent: income received not for what one creates, but for possessing a right or privilege. The era abounded in it. Seville’s monopoly on American trade, the Mesta’s privileges, the monopolies of chartered companies—all were ways to charge for access rather than for a product. In England under Elizabeth I and James I, the crown granted courtiers monopoly patents to produce and sell salt, soap, playing cards, starch, and dozens of other goods; prices rose, while the patent holders invented nothing. Parliament’s outrage led to the Statute of Monopolies of 1624, which limited such privileges and retained an exception only for genuinely new inventions—a role we will discuss in the next chapter.

But perhaps the most insidious kind of rent was the psychology created by silver. In Spain, purchasing a noble title, an office, or a government bond called a *juro* brought respect and reliable income, while trade and craft were considered unworthy of a *hidalgo*. Capital accumulated in commerce went not into workshops, but into land, titles, and interest on loans to the crown. The Spanish themselves noticed this. A group of publicists in the late sixteenth and early seventeenth centuries called the *arbitristas*—writers of proposed remedies for the king—described the country’s troubles with striking clarity. The most famous, the jurist Martín González de Cellorigo, argued in a treatise of 1600 that Spain had grown poor not from a lack of gold and silver, but because people had stopped working and begun living on rent, interest, and the labor of others—and that a country’s real wealth consisted of things produced, not metal. The idea Adam Smith would reach one hundred and seventy years later was expressed in Spain itself, but neither crown nor society listened.

Holland did not resist this temptation either, only succumbed later. In the eighteenth century, many regent families that had made fortunes in trade stopped taking risks and began living on interest from public debt and foreign loans, moving to country estates. Dutch historians wryly call this the “Age of Wigs.” The Republic changed from Europe’s workshop into its banker—wealthy, but steadily less inventive. This is an important warning for the whole book: once built, a mechanism of trust does not by itself guarantee that surplus will continue to flow into development. It can serve rent just as readily.

Let us sum up. In an age when more silver passed through Europe than apparently in all its previous history, a substantial share went neither into destruction nor development, but into gilding, bastions, court life, and interest. Such expenditures left behind no new skills, no cheap goods for the majority, and no resilience against future crises. Their common feature was that surplus was spent to preserve or display status, not to expand possibilities. And the more easily surplus was acquired—from a mine, a monopoly, or another people’s tribute—the more readily it was spent in precisely this way.

## The Lesson of the Era

Little more than a century separated the pepper that the Zamorin of Calicut refused to exchange for washbasins and hats from shares in the East India Company that an Amsterdam maidservant could sell to her neighbor without waiting for the ships. In that time, Europe circled the world, linked it with a single coin, and received potatoes and silver from across the ocean—and paid for them in other people’s lives on a scale history had never known. Let us reduce the story to five propositions.

**First: wealth found is not the same as wealth created.** Spain received the greatest flow of precious metal in European history and a century later found itself poorer than a country with neither mines nor spices. Silver is merely a claim on things produced by others. If a country cannot produce those things itself, the metal flows to those that can: Genoa, Antwerp, Amsterdam, China. But the curse also had a political side, perhaps the more important one. A king who receives the fifth from Potosí needs to negotiate less with the Cortes than he would without it, and therefore has less reason to think about making his subjects richer. A qualification is necessary: American silver supplied only one-fifth to one-quarter of the crown’s income, and the Cortes bargained over tax terms throughout the sixteenth century, as Thompson showed—so this was a shift in the balance, not complete freedom from negotiation. Easy revenue frees power from the need to bargain with those who create wealth. We will encounter this pattern again in the oil states of the twentieth century.

**Second: the price of money is the price of trust.** The Dutch province borrowed at four percent or less; the Spanish crown borrowed at higher rates and still repeatedly failed to pay. The difference lay not in the quantity of metal, but in whether the borrower could take the creditor’s money at will. Where rulers themselves hold bonds, decisions about taxes are made by those who pay them, and an exchange bank guarantees that a guilder in an account is a guilder, credit becomes cheaper—and slow, modest, long-term projects become profitable: a polder, a *fluyt*, a canal with a scheduled barge. A bank, exchange, and joint-stock company produced nothing themselves, but reduced the cost of cooperation among strangers. These institutions, not silver, outlived the era.

**Third: those who believe in a zero-sum world spend surplus on weapons.** Mercantilism treated the world’s wealth as a fixed pie that could only be divided anew. From this premise followed logically the Navigation Act, three Anglo-Dutch wars, bastions built because a neighbor was building them, and the Portuguese network of fortresses that, by Steensgaard’s estimate, consumed almost all the revenue for which it was maintained. Where trade was understood as mutual gain, surplus went into canals, mills, and printing presses. The era offers a good example of how a mistaken economic idea becomes a material force: it determines where a society invests its common surplus—and therefore, ultimately, whether that surplus fosters development, destroys, or burns away in waste.

**Fourth: institutions that restrain arbitrary power work only for those inside them.** The very East India Company that created the first liquid market in shares massacred the population of the Banda Islands for a monopoly on nutmeg. Slaves in Barbados sweetened the tea of a London apprentice. The plantation increased sugar output per worker, but accumulated neither knowledge, resilience, nor well-being for those who labored there—to measure it in tons alone is to discard almost every criterion of this book. Property rights, courts, parliament, low interest rates—all worked for the merchants of Amsterdam and London and did not work for Indigenous *mita* laborers or Africans in ships’ holds. Hence a question that will become central in the synthesis: can growth whose costs are shifted onto people without a voice be called development? By the standards of our framework, no: it is a transfer of surplus, not its creation.

**Fifth: even a good mechanism does not decide where surplus will go.** The industrious revolution showed that northwestern Europe’s new wealth went not into leisure but new wants: people worked more to buy sugar, tobacco, calico, and porcelain, some of which improved life and some of which served only status. And in the eighteenth century, the Dutch regents who inherited the legacy of those who drained the Beemster preferred to live on interest in country estates. Trust and cheap credit open the road to development, but can just as readily serve rent. An institution creates possibilities; what people do with them depends on who controls it and whether they have reasons to take risks.

Now for the answer to the book’s recurring question. Where did the surplus of the Age of Discovery go? For the first time in history it was gathered from the entire world, and most of it, if both documents and consequences are to be believed, did not go into development. Destruction absorbed an enormous share: the devastation of the Americas, the slave trade, the Thirty Years’ War, the sack of Antwerp, and the expulsion of Jews and Moriscos. An even larger share burned away in waste—in the gilding of *retablos* and El Escorial, the fountains of Versailles, the bastions of Flanders, interest paid to Genoese bankers, and purchased titles. The developmental share looked more modest but proved more durable: potatoes and maize in peasant gardens, the *fluyt* and the scheduled barge, polders, printed books and newspapers, Leiden University, the exchange bank, and the stock exchange. One quality distinguished it: it made food, transport, information, and credit cheaper for many without taking anything from a neighbor. And perhaps the chapter’s central conclusion is this: surplus went into development not where it was most abundant, but where many people controlled it and where it was hardest to seize by force.

Let us honestly state the limits of these conclusions. The comparison between Spain and Holland is too convenient to be the whole truth: Holland had a favorable location at the mouths of great rivers, the Baltic nearby, and cities inherited from the Middle Ages, while Spain had dry plateaus and the need to maintain an empire from Milan to Manila. Acemoglu, Johnson, and Robinson’s explanation is contested, and it probably understates the roles of war, geography, and chance. We cannot calculate precisely what share of surplus followed each channel: this is a judgment from the sources, not an accounting exercise. Finally, for all its institutions, Holland did not become the birthplace of the Industrial Revolution—primacy passed to England, where trust in credit was joined by cheap coal, expensive labor, and above all a new culture of testable knowledge. How this combination formed, and why precisely there, is the subject of the next chapter.

How many people lived in Europe and the Americas during these two and a half centuries, what a day’s labor was worth in London and Florence, and how much richer a Dutchman was than a Spaniard—all this is covered in the section on the numbers.

## The Numbers

The Age of Discovery left more numbers than any preceding era: Seville’s customs books, East India Company ship lists, prices from the Amsterdam exchange, and parish registers from English villages. Yet here, too, almost everything is a reconstruction, and large disagreements are noted.

**Europe’s population.** Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones calculated that Europe had around eighty million inhabitants in 1500, about one hundred million by 1600, and about one hundred and twenty million by 1700. Growth was slow: wars, years of famine, and plague, which returned to London as late as 1665, repeatedly set it back. Individual cities, however, grew rapidly. Amsterdam, which had around thirty thousand inhabitants in the 1570s, reached two hundred thousand by the 1670s according to conventional estimates. Jan de Vries calculated that roughly a third of the population in the Dutch provinces lived in towns—more than anywhere else in Europe.

**The population of the Americas.** The most disputed figure in the book. No one knows how many people lived in the two American continents in 1492: estimates over the past century have ranged from eight million to well over one hundred million. William Denevan’s synthesis (1992) gives around fifty-four million; the authors of a 2019 review—Koch, Brierley, Maslin, and Lewis—give around sixty million, with about fifty-six million deaths by 1600. But almost everyone agrees on the scale of the losses: by the beginning of the seventeenth century, the Indigenous population had fallen by roughly nine-tenths. Diseases brought from the Old World were the principal cause, but wars, forced labor, and famine caused by the destruction of the economy compounded them.

**People in the holds.** According to the Slave Voyages database compiled from ships’ documents, around twelve and a half million people were taken from Africa between 1501 and 1866; about ten million seven hundred thousand reached the other side of the Atlantic alive. More than half of all voyages occurred in the eighteenth century—after the end of this chapter’s period. The mechanism this era set in motion continued gathering speed for another century and a half.

**Silver and prices.** From Seville’s account books, Earl Hamilton calculated official imports in 1503–1660 at around sixteen thousand tons of silver and around one hundred and eighty tons of gold; smuggling, by general agreement, added a great deal. By his data, prices in Spain rose roughly three- to fourfold during the sixteenth century. Using Dutch newspapers, Michel Morineau puts seventeenth-century imports noticeably higher than Seville’s records do. This sounds catastrophic, but on an annual basis it is only around one and a half percent—modest by twentieth-century standards. Novelty made it a “revolution”: for centuries before, prices had scarcely changed, and their long, unstoppable rise came as a shock.

**Income per person.** In Angus Maddison’s estimates, average income in Western Europe rose from roughly 770 “1990 international dollars” in 1500 to one thousand in 1700. The average conceals a divergence. According to Maddison, the Netherlands had an income of around 1,400 dollars per person in 1600 and more than 2,000 by 1700: the richest country in the world. For all its silver, Spain remained at around 850 and did not advance over the century. Later recalculations by Carlos Álvarez-Nogal and Leandro Prados de la Escosura make sixteenth-century Spain richer and its seventeenth-century decline more pronounced; the gap with Holland in 1700 does not disappear. England lay between them: roughly one thousand in 1600 and 1,250 in 1700. Maddison places China at around six hundred dollars throughout those two centuries, although Kenneth Pomeranz and other historians believe that its most developed regions, the Yangtze Delta, still matched England in the eighteenth century. These calculations are estimates, but the direction of change is hardly in doubt.

**Payment for labor.** Robert Allen (2001) converted construction workers’ daily wages in two dozen European cities into “baskets” of goods. The result is known as the “Little Divergence.” From the fifteenth to the eighteenth century, real wages in London and Amsterdam remained at roughly the same level—a worker could buy bread, some meat, beer, candles, and cloth. In Florence, Vienna, Madrid, and Kraków, they fell by almost half over the same period, to a level at which a family lived on gruel alone. Silver did not raise the Spanish mason’s wage. Trade, urban growth, and high output in the northwest did.

**Credit.** By the mid-seventeenth century, the province of Holland borrowed at four percent a year. Under Philip II, the Spanish crown suspended payments four times—1557, 1560, 1575, and 1596—according to Mauricio Drelichman and Hans-Joachim Voth’s calculations, and still found lenders, but at higher rates. According to Bruijn, Gaastra, and Schöffer, the East India Company sent almost a million people to Asia between 1602 and 1795. Of every three, roughly one returned home.

**Length of life.** Edward Wrigley and Roger Schofield reconstructed England’s demography from 1541 onward using thousands of parish registers. In their estimates, life expectancy at birth ranged from just under thirty to a little over forty years, falling during epidemics and crop failures and rising in prosperous decades. The exchange, the potato, and silver had not yet shifted it. As in the Middle Ages, the average person’s life was not much longer than his great-grandfather’s. The new surplus gathered from across the world made some countries and some estates richer, but had not yet changed the principal limit set by hunger and disease. How that limit first began to be dismantled—with testable knowledge, a parliament controlling debt, and an inventor’s right to his invention—is the story of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; errors by a factor of several are possible. The assumptions from the previous chapter: a ten-hour day; thirty to fifty hours of peasant labor for a fifty-kilogram sack of grain; a working life of around seventy thousand hours, roughly two thousand a year. A ducat was eleven reales; an unskilled laborer in late-sixteenth-century Castile earned, by my estimate from Earl Hamilton’s data, two to three reales a day—this requires verification.

### El Escorial

**Input data.** Construction from 1563 to 1584, with one thousand to fifteen hundred people on the site. The total price is known only from tradition: five to six million ducats; I found no documented total from the accounts—this requires verification.

**Labor, grain, lives.** That is twenty to thirty million person-days—stonemasons, carters, foundrymen, artists—or two hundred to three hundred million hours. Feeding the workers required another half-million sacks, twenty to thirty million hours of peasant labor. Total: three to five thousand working lives, six to seven times Westminster Abbey. A bottom-up check: the site alone over twenty years amounts to around one thousand lives; the rest came from quarries, transport, bronze, and paintings.

**Alternative.** The Beemster cost, by a widely cited estimate, around one and a half million guilders and created around seven thousand hectares of new land—this requires verification. At an exchange rate of two and a half to three guilders per ducat—this requires verification—El Escorial equaled eight to twelve Beemsters, land for several thousand farms. There was also a Castilian alternative: in 1588, barges built by engineer Juan Bautista Antonelli traveled down the cleared Tagus from Toledo to Lisbon in fifteen days, but after the engineer’s death the project stalled.

### The Army of Flanders

**Input data.** From 1567 to 1659, Spain maintained an army in the Netherlands; according to Geoffrey Parker, at its peak in 1574 it numbered more than eighty thousand men. The average size is my estimate: forty to sixty thousand; excluding the truce of 1609–1621, that gives three to five million soldier-years.

**Labor, grain, lives.** A soldier-year meant two thousand hours taken from the economy: six to ten billion hours in all, or eighty-five to one hundred and forty-five thousand working lives. His bread amounted to around seven sacks a year, twenty to thirty-five million sacks, another ten to twenty-five thousand peasant lives. Total: one hundred to one hundred and seventy thousand working lives, twenty to forty Escorials. And this is a lower bound: it excludes bastions, gunpowder, wagon trains, and pay beyond bread. I will not attempt to count the literal lives—the people killed, those who died in camps, or the inhabitants of devastated cities.

**Alternative.** Thirty million sacks were a year’s bread for four to five million people, nearly all of Castile—this requires verification—where famine and plague killed hundreds of thousands around the turn of the seventeenth century. And the hours would have sufficed for hundreds of Beemsters.

**Conclusion.** El Escorial still stands, and manuscripts are read in its library. The Army of Flanders left debts, suspended payments, and an independent Holland it failed to hold. Both bills were paid from the silver of Potosí and the taxes of the Castilian village—and both were many times greater than what the Dutch paid for their polders, canals, and exchange.
