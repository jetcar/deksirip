# Chapter 6. Beyond Europe

## Portrait of the Era

In the winter of 1405, at the mouth of the Yangtze, beside the docks of Liujia Harbor not far from present-day Shanghai, a fleet was gathering unlike any the world had ever seen. According to the official history of the Ming dynasty, sixty-two great “treasure ships” and some twenty-eight thousand people—sailors, soldiers, interpreters, physicians, scribes, and astrologers—set out on the first voyage. Including supply vessels, water carriers, and escort junks, the fleet numbered, by varying estimates, between two hundred and three hundred ships. The dimensions of the largest treasure ships remain disputed: the figure in the chronicles—roughly 130 meters long—seems impossible for a wooden vessel to many engineers, and modern estimates are generally more modest. Yet even a modest estimate means that China’s largest ships were several times bigger than the caravels in which Vasco da Gama would round Africa ninety years later.

The fleet was commanded by Zheng He, a court eunuch of Muslim origin from Yunnan, captured as a child during the Ming conquest of the province. Over twenty-eight years, his squadrons made seven voyages: to Java and Sumatra, Ceylon, Calicut on India’s Malabar Coast, Hormuz at the entrance to the Persian Gulf, Aden, and the shores of present-day Somalia and Kenya. They carried silk, porcelain, and bronze, and returned with spices, precious stones, envoys from distant kingdoms—and, once, a giraffe that the court took for the auspicious unicorn-like *qilin*.

Then it all stopped. The seventh voyage returned in 1433, and there was no eighth. The court decided that distant expeditions were too expensive, that the threat came not from the sea but from the northern steppe, and that the empire’s proper concerns were land, grain, and the Great Wall, not foreign shores. Private maritime trade, banned by the dynasty’s founder, remained prohibited, while the building of large ships came under suspicion. It was later said that the official Liu Daxia destroyed or concealed the voyage records so that no one would think of repeating them. Historians consider the story less than entirely reliable, but the fact that people eagerly retold it is revealing in itself. Little more than sixty years after the last squadron returned, the Portuguese—with dozens of times fewer ships—entered the same Indian Ocean ports and remained there for centuries.

This scene is often told as a parable of how China “missed its chance to rule the world.” We will not rush to a moral. The Ming court’s decision was rational in its own way: the voyages brought no revenue, while the nomads in the north posed a real threat. But something else matters for this book. Here was a civilization that, at the beginning of the fifteenth century, could do more than any European country: build enormous ships, feed tens of thousands of people on campaign, navigate by compass, print books, and pay with paper money. And by its own choice, it abandoned some of what it knew how to do. This is the chapter’s central question: why did wealthy and inventive societies repeatedly come to a halt?

To answer it, we must look beyond a single scene. Until now, this book has followed the line European textbooks traditionally draw: Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome. But that line is deceptive. From roughly the fifth through the fifteenth century—while Western Europe was putting itself back together after Rome’s collapse—the richest, most literate, and technologically strongest societies in the world were not in Europe. We will visit four of them, each with its own answer to the question of where to direct the surplus.

The first is China. Picture Kaifeng, capital of the Song dynasty, around 1100. The famous scroll by the artist Zhang Zeduan, *Along the River During the Qingming Festival*, is generally thought to depict this world almost frame by frame: freight barges on the canal, shops with signboards, eating houses, physicians, fortune-tellers, porters, and a camel caravan at the city gate. The city, whose population historians estimate at anywhere from half a million to more than a million, lived on grain carried north from the south along the Grand Canal. Here people paid with paper money, printed books from wooden blocks, smelted cast iron on a scale England would not achieve until the eve of the Industrial Revolution, and recruited officials through written examinations.

The second is the Islamic world. Baghdad in the ninth century, the city of the Abbasid caliphs, was probably the largest city of its time outside China. Merchants from Spain and India met in its markets, and the caliphs’ court had a library known as the House of Wisdom. Caliphs, nobles, and physicians all commissioned Arabic translations of Aristotle, Euclid, and Ptolemy; historian Dimitri Gutas has shown that the legend of the House of Wisdom as a state academy of translators is greatly exaggerated. A merchant could send not a sack of silver from Cairo to Aden but a letter instructing someone to pay the money—and his partner in Aden would trust that letter. The documents of the Cairo Geniza, a repository of old papers attached to a synagogue in Fustat, preserved thousands of such letters, allowing historians to reconstruct the commercial life of the Mediterranean from the tenth through the thirteenth century in a level of detail unavailable for Europe at the time.

The third is India. For centuries, the weavers of Bengal, Gujarat, and the Coromandel Coast clothed half the world: Indian cotton fabric has been found in Egypt, Southeast Asia, and East Africa, while the words “calico” (from Calicut) and “chintz” (from the Hindi *chhint*) preserve in European languages the memory of where it came from. “Muslin,” admittedly, was named for the Iraqi city of Mosul, but over time the word came to refer above all to the finest fabric of Bengal. This was an economy of virtuosic craftsmanship and vast trading networks—and also an economy in which a person’s occupation was largely determined by birth.

The fourth is the Inca Empire in the Andes, which at the turn of the sixteenth century united, by various estimates, between six and twelve million people, perhaps even more, across a territory stretching from present-day Ecuador to Chile. It had no money, no markets in our sense, and no writing in the conventional sense—only knotted cords called *quipu*, on which accountants recorded numbers. Yet it built tens of thousands of kilometers of roads, maintained warehouses of grain, clothing, and weapons throughout the country, and could feed armies and victims of crop failure. It is the largest economy known to us that functioned without money—and therefore the purest experiment in our book in distributing surplus by plan rather than by exchange.

Four worlds, four answers. China relied on a state of scholar-officials, canals, and grain. The Islamic world relied on long-distance trade, trust among merchants, and translated knowledge. India relied on craftsmanship embedded in hereditary communities. The Incas relied on labor levies and storehouses. For a time, each of these answers made its society richer than its neighbors. And each, as we will see, eventually reached its own ceiling.

The question of that ceiling is one of the fiercest in economic history. In *The Great Divergence*, American historian Kenneth Pomeranz argued that as late as around 1750, China’s most developed regions, such as the Yangtze Delta, had living standards comparable to those of Europe’s most developed regions, and that their paths diverged only afterward—thanks to English coal and American colonies. His opponents, among them Robert Allen and Stephen Broadberry and his coauthors, use wage and price data to conclude that Northwestern Europe pulled ahead earlier—in the seventeenth century, or by some estimates even the sixteenth. We will not resolve this dispute. But it suggests how to read this chapter: not as a story about a “backward East,” but as the history of societies that remained in the lead for a long time—and of what they spent their surplus on while they were ahead.

## Energy and Technology

Let us begin with the simple question economic historians ask of every society before the nineteenth century: where did its energy come from? The answer was the same everywhere—from sunlight captured by plants. Grain fed people, grass and straw fed animals, and forests supplied fuel and building material. British historian Edward Wrigley called this kind of system an “organic economy”: everything it could do ultimately depended on the area of land available for fields and forests. Neither China, Baghdad, Bengal, nor Cusco broke out of this circle. Yet within it they achieved astonishing things—and that is precisely why their example matters. They show how far a society could go while remaining within the limits of muscle, water, wind, and firewood.

### China: Rice, Canals, and Cast Iron

Medieval China’s main energy revolution happened not in a workshop but in a rice field. In the early eleventh century, the Song court ordered that farmers in the lower Yangtze be given an early-ripening, drought-resistant rice imported from Champa, a kingdom in the south of present-day Vietnam. It made it possible to harvest twice a year where there had once been only one crop, or to grow rice on slopes that water had scarcely reached before. Together with terracing, treadle-operated waterwheels, transplanting seedlings, and fertilizing fields with urban waste, it turned the Yangtze Valley into one of the most densely populated and productive regions on the planet. Under the Song, China’s population is estimated to have exceeded one hundred million for the first time.

A rice field is not only a source of food but a particular organization of labor. Rice demands an immense amount of work by hand: leveling plots, building and repairing bunds, monitoring water levels, transplanting every seedling. Output per hectare is very high; output per hour of labor is modest. Remember this distinction: we will return to it when we ask why China did not take the path of machinery.

The grain still had to be delivered to the officials, soldiers, and townspeople who consumed it. This was the purpose of the Grand Canal, a system of artificial waterways that connected the Yellow and Yangtze Rivers under the Sui dynasty in the early seventh century and was later extended to Beijing. It was one of the greatest engineering works of the preindustrial world, eventually reaching about 1,800 kilometers in length. In 984, according to Song sources, the official Qiao Weiyue built a pound lock with two gates on the canal—a chamber in which a barge could be raised or lowered without wasting water. Similar chamber locks would not appear in Europe until the late Middle Ages. The canal was the artery through which the south’s surplus flowed north. Where it went next—into the treasury, to the army on the frontier, or to the capital’s markets—is a question of power, and we will ask it in due course.

The most remarkable thing about Song China was metal. After studying tax records, American historian Robert Hartwell estimated in the 1960s that northern China produced roughly 125,000 tons of iron a year around 1078. The figure has often been challenged—historian of metallurgy Donald Wagner, for example, considers it too high—and should be treated as an order of magnitude rather than a precise number. Yet even skeptics agree that output was enormous: comparable to all of Europe around 1700 and greater than England’s output as late as 1788. Chinese craftspeople had long known how to produce cast iron—molten metal that could be poured into molds—and turn it into steel. When the forests around the metallurgical regions thinned, furnaces began switching to mineral coal. Sources from the period mention coal being sold in Kaifeng as an ordinary household fuel.

At this point, one wants to stop and ask: was this not the beginning of an industrial revolution? Coal, cast iron, mass production—the same words that would be heard in England seven hundred years later. Many historians have asked this question, and we will return to it when discussing ceilings. For now, note one thing: by the fourteenth century, iron output in the northern regions had apparently fallen sharply. Wars with the Jurchens and Mongols, the devastation of the north, and the shift of the economy’s center to the south, where coal and ore were not found close together, all interrupted the expansion. The knowledge did not vanish, but the demand and capital did.

China’s other inventions are familiar from schoolbook lists. Paper: it had been made since the Han period, and the court official Cai Lun improved the process around 105. Woodblock printing: the oldest precisely dated printed book is the *Diamond Sutra* of 868, found in the Dunhuang caves. Movable type made of fired clay, invented in the 1040s by the artisan Bi Sheng, according to the scholar Shen Kuo. The magnetic compass, used by mariners no later than the turn of the twelfth century. Gunpowder—first a material for incendiary projectiles, and from the twelfth and thirteenth centuries for true firearms. The English biochemist Joseph Needham devoted a multivolume history to Chinese science and formulated the question that now bears his name: with such a legacy, why did the scientific and industrial revolutions not happen in China?

Notice where these inventions went. Printing served above all to disseminate the classical texts, Buddhist sutras, calendars, and examination manuals—that is, knowledge the state considered proper. For Chinese script, with its thousands of characters, movable type was less advantageous than it was in Europe, with its two or three dozen letters, and woodblock printing remained dominant for centuries. Gunpowder served the army. The compass served trade and the fleet. There was no less knowledge here than in Europe, but it was directed differently.

### The Islamic World: Water, Paper, and Translated Science

The lands of the Abbasid Caliphate—from Spain to Central Asia—were mostly dry. The greatest technical achievements of this world were therefore connected to water and its scarcity.

The *qanat*, or *karez*, an underground aqueduct, had been invented in ancient Iran. At the foot of mountains, where groundwater lies closer to the surface, people dug a chain of wells and connected them with a sloping gallery through which water flowed by gravity for many kilometers to the fields. Underground, it did not evaporate in the heat. Such systems were built and maintained for centuries and spread across North Africa and Spain. Alongside them worked *norias*, enormous water-lifting wheels turned by the river itself; several ancient wheels still stand on the Orontes River in the Syrian city of Hama.

In the tenth century, geographers described windmills in Sistan, on the present-day border of Iran and Afghanistan. They had vertical axes adapted to the region’s constant strong winds. This is among the earliest reliable evidence of windmills anywhere in the world.

In the 1970s and 1980s, American historian Andrew Watson proposed the idea of an “Arab Agricultural Revolution.” In his view, the first centuries of Islam saw sugar cane, cotton, rice, citrus fruit, eggplant, and spinach spread across the caliphate, along with irrigation and summer watering that made harvests possible during the hot months. Many later specialists, Michael Decker among them, concluded that Watson had exaggerated: some of these crops had been known long before Islam, and the scale of the change is difficult to measure. Yet the broader picture is beyond doubt—a single political and commercial space through which plants and techniques spread rapidly. A realm stretching from the Atlantic to the Indus functioned as a vast conduit of exchange.

Perhaps the most important development was the spread of paper. In the traditional account, its secret reached the Arabs after the Battle of Talas in 751, when Chinese craftspeople were taken prisoner. Historians, including Jonathan Bloom, consider this story more legend than fact: paper was probably known in Central Asia earlier. But from the late eighth century onward, paper workshops appeared in Samarkand and then Baghdad. Paper was far cheaper than parchment, and unlike papyrus, which grew only in Egypt, it could be made anywhere from linen rags. A cheap medium meant cheap books, cheap accounts, and cheap letters. Without it, there would have been neither the libraries of Baghdad and Córdoba nor the thousands of merchants’ letters preserved in the Cairo Geniza.

This paper was used for copying and translation. In the ninth century, especially under Caliph al-Ma'mun, translators—many of them Syriac Christians, such as the renowned physician Hunayn ibn Ishaq—translated into Arabic most of the available Greek works on medicine, philosophy, mathematics, and astronomy. But they did more than preserve them. Around the 820s, Muhammad al-Khwarizmi wrote a treatise on solving equations whose title contained the term *al-jabr*, meaning restoration; from it came the word “algebra,” while a Latin corruption of the author’s name gave us “algorithm.” His book on calculation with Indian numerals eventually introduced Europe to positional notation and zero. In early-eleventh-century Cairo, Ibn al-Haytham conducted experiments with light and wrote the *Book of Optics*, which was later translated and read in Europe. Ibn Sina compiled *The Canon of Medicine*, still used for instruction at European universities in the seventeenth century.

This knowledge is a textbook example of developmental investment in the terms of our framework: it was not consumed but accumulated, and it could be passed on. Who paid is revealing as well. Translations were funded by caliphs, courtiers, wealthy families such as the Banu Musa brothers, and physicians who needed textbooks. As long as patrons had both surplus and interest, knowledge grew.

### India: Cotton and Steel

Indian technology is less conspicuous because its greatest achievement was not a machine but craftsmanship refined to the utmost delicacy. India was the first to learn to grow, spin, and weave cotton and, most importantly, to dye it with lasting colors. This required knowledge of mordants—metal salts that bind dye to fiber so that a pattern does not wash out. Indian dyers mastered the art so thoroughly that printed cottons from the Coromandel Coast remained the most coveted commodity in the Indian Ocean for centuries. According to travelers’ accounts, the finest Bengali muslin could be drawn through a signet ring.

The *charkhi*, a device with two rotating rollers, separated cotton fibers from their seeds, while a spinning wheel, apparently widespread in India by the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, spun them into thread. All these devices were operated by hand. India’s cotton industry was immense, but dispersed among thousands of villages and artisanal quarters rather than concentrated in workshops with a common power source.

Indian metallurgists smelted *wootz*, a high-carbon steel produced in small sealed crucibles. Wootz ingots were exported to the Middle East, where smiths forged them into blades with patterned surfaces. Europeans would call these blades Damascus steel, though the steel itself came from India or Central Asia. The Iron Pillar of Delhi, made according to the generally accepted dating around the fifth century, has stood outdoors for fifteen hundred years with almost no rust, thanks to the metal’s composition and the protective film that forms on its surface.

Finally, India, like every land around the Indian Ocean, lived to the rhythm of the monsoons. Sailors knew that in summer the wind blew from sea to land and in winter in the opposite direction, and all trade was organized around this schedule. Knowing the season, one could plan a voyage from Gujarat to Aden or from Malabar to Malacca with precision measured in months. Wind was the energy that carried cloth, pepper, and rice across the entire ocean—free of charge, but strictly by the calendar.

### The Incas: Without the Wheel, Iron, or Draft Animals

The technology of the Inca Empire is the hardest to imagine because it lacked what we are accustomed to regarding as fundamental: wheels for transport, iron, horses, and oxen. Its only pack animal was the llama, which according to varying estimates carries about thirty kilograms and cannot pull a plow. The land was worked with a foot plow, the *chaquitaclla*, a sharpened pole with a footrest. Everything built, transported, and cultivated was built, transported, and cultivated by human hands.

The result is all the more impressive. Long before the Incas, Andean farmers turned steep slopes into stairways of terraces with stone retaining walls and irrigation canals. The terraces held soil and water and made it possible to grow different things at different elevations: maize below, potatoes and quinoa higher up, and llamas and alpacas grazing higher still. Andean peoples developed hundreds of potato varieties. They knew how to make *chuño*: potatoes were left to freeze in the high-altitude cold for several nights, then trampled by day to squeeze out their water and dried in the sun. The result was a lightweight food that could be stored for years. This was the empire’s energy in preserved form.

It was stored in *qollqas*, warehouses built in rows on slopes where the wind kept them cool and dry. Archaeologists have counted hundreds of these storehouses around the provincial center of Huánuco Pampa alone. Everything moved along the Qhapaq Ñan road network, estimated at between twenty-three thousand kilometers—the length surveyed by archaeologist John Hyslop—and thirty to forty thousand kilometers, with suspension bridges woven from grass spanning ravines and *tambo* way stations spaced a day’s journey apart. No carts traveled these roads: they carried people, llama caravans, and *chasqui* runners, who passed messages in relays. According to Spanish chroniclers, a message could cover roughly two hundred kilometers in a day.

The Incas knew metals—gold, silver, copper, bronze—but used them more for ornament, ritual, and symbols of power than for tools. The famous walls of Cusco, made of polygonal blocks fitted without mortar so tightly that a knife blade cannot pass between them, were worked with stone hammers and abrasives. It is astonishing craftsmanship—and at the same time a reminder of how much human labor went into every structure.

### What They Shared: A High Plateau

Placed side by side, the four worlds reveal a common feature. Each found its own way to extract more from the organic economy: China through intensive agriculture, canals, and metal; the Islamic world through irrigation of dry lands, the exchange of crops and knowledge, and cheap paper; India through craftsmanship of the highest order and monsoon trade; the Incas through terraces, preserved food, and roads. Each invested part of its surplus in something genuinely developmental: irrigation, infrastructure, literacy, science.

And each ultimately reached a high plateau rather than a runway. British historian Mark Elvin called the Chinese version a “high-level equilibrium trap”: the economy became so adept at using cheap labor and land that there was no reason to invest in labor-saving machinery. The idea is disputed, and we will return to it. But the question it raises applies to all four worlds. The issue is not whether they knew how to invent—they did, and often better than Europeans. The issue is who had an interest in turning inventions into sustained growth in output per hour of human labor, and why. The answer lies no longer in technology but in the way labor was organized and who received the surplus. That is the subject of the next section.

## Organization of Labor

Who stood behind the canals, storehouses, and finest fabrics? Almost everywhere the answer was the same: the peasant family. In each of the four worlds, the overwhelming majority of people—by varying estimates, eight or nine out of ten—worked the land. Beyond that, the similarity ends. A Chinese peasant paid taxes in grain and money and could sell the harvest at market. A water carrier in Baghdad or a weaver in Cairo hired out for wages. An Indian potter served the village by right of birth. An Andean community member gave the state a fixed number of working days and received food, *chicha*, and clothing from the storehouses in return. These are four different answers to the question we ask in every chapter: who decides what an hour of human labor will be spent on, and where does everything that hour creates beyond subsistence go?

### China: Households, Markets, and Registered Families

The foundation of the Chinese economy was the peasant household—five or six people farming several dozen *mu* of land (one *mu* is about one-sixteenth of a hectare). From ancient times, the state sought to see every household: censuses and household registers were kept with such regularity in China that historical demographers still use them, though with adjustments for avoidance of registration. Through the register, a household paid taxes and supplied people for labor service—repairing dams and canals, transporting state grain, and serving at the local administration.

The Song period loosened this supervision considerably. Some labor obligations were converted into monetary payments, and the state began hiring workers for wages. In the 1070s, the reformer Wang Anshi made this part of official policy: a household that paid “money in lieu of service” was freed from the ruinous duty of, for example, transporting state cargo at its own expense. His reforms provoked fierce conflict at court, and many were repealed after his resignation, but the overall direction—from compulsory service toward hired labor and monetary taxation—survived. Cities employed wage-earning porters, boatmen, weavers, workers at saltworks, and workers in metal shops. Farmers of the lower Yangtze increasingly planted not only for themselves but for sale: rice for the cities, mulberry trees for silkworms, and later cotton. Much of the land was cultivated by tenants who, according to abundant evidence, gave the landowner roughly half the harvest.

The Ming dynasty, which came to power in 1368 after driving out the Mongols, tried to turn back time. Its founder, Zhu Yuanzhang, himself from a poor peasant family, dreamed of a self-sufficient village where everyone was registered and no one wandered idle. The population was divided into hereditary categories: farming households, military households required to supply soldiers, and artisan households required to take turns working in state workshops. A soldier’s son was to become a soldier, an artisan’s son an artisan. Villages were grouped into units of one hundred ten households responsible for tax collection and order. On paper, the result was an almost perfect system of prescriptions: everyone knew their obligation, and the state knew everyone.

In practice, the system unraveled over a century or two. Artisans fled or bought their way out, military households emptied, and taxes were again converted into silver. In the sixteenth century, a series of reforms known collectively as the Single Whip consolidated numerous services and levies into a monetary tax assessed mainly on land. The market that the state had tried to push aside from above returned from below. The episode is worth remembering: fixing every person to a function proved expensive to supervise and poorly adapted to change. When prices, harvests, and family sizes changed, the register lagged behind life.

State monopolies occupied a special place in China’s organization of labor. Since the Han period, the treasury had controlled salt and iron, and later wine and tea. Families assigned to state saltworks boiled the brine, while merchants sold the salt under government licenses. The monopoly produced enormous revenue: in Song times, historians estimate that salt was one of the treasury’s largest sources of income after the land tax. But the income arose not because more or better salt was produced, but because its price was artificially raised above the cost of production. In our framework, this is classic rent: extraction rather than creation. Significantly, the Chinese themselves debated it. In 81 BCE, a court debate under the Han was later recorded as the *Discourses on Salt and Iron*. Confucian scholars accused the state monopoly of impoverishing the people and producing poor, expensive tools; supporters replied that without the money, there was no way to maintain the army on the frontier. This argument—whether the treasury should profit from its subjects’ need—would recur many times in history.

### The Islamic World: Free Labor, Slavery, and Mamluks

The Islamic city was above all a city of hired labor. Artisans—weavers, coppersmiths, dyers, copyists—worked in their own shops, often clustered on streets and in quarters devoted to one trade, and sold their goods in the bazaar. Above the market stood the *muhtasib*, an official who supervised weights and measures, the quality of bread, honesty in transactions, and public morality. Manuals for *muhtasibs* surviving from the twelfth through the fourteenth century read like detailed guides to combating fraud: how to tell whether milk had been watered or sand mixed into flour. This was oversight of honest exchange—a function that reduced the cost of trust between strangers.

The question of guilds is more complicated. For a long time, scholars believed that craft corporations resembling European guilds appeared very early in the Middle East. Today, many historians, including Geniza scholar Shelomo Goitein, believe that from the tenth through the thirteenth century there were apparently no formal guilds with charters and monopolies over a trade. Artisans instead joined informal associations based on neighborhood and kinship. Guilds in the full sense developed later, especially in the Ottoman Empire. The distinction matters: the absence of guild barriers made entry into a trade easier, but it also provided none of the apprenticeship system and quality standards we will encounter in medieval Europe.

Slavery existed alongside free labor and cannot be passed over. Enslaved people were brought from East Africa, the Eurasian steppes, and Slavic lands; most served in households and the army. But there was also grueling slave labor in production. On the saline lands of southern Iraq near Basra, thousands of enslaved East Africans—known as the Zanj—removed the salt crust in the ninth century to return the land to cultivation. In 869, they rebelled, and the uprising lasted roughly fourteen years, devastating Basra and forcing the caliphate into a long and bloody war. Bedouins and the poor also joined the rebels, so it was not solely a slave revolt—but the Zanj formed its core. It was one of the largest slave uprisings in history and demonstrates the same thing we saw in Rome: cheap forced labor not only caused suffering but made the economy vulnerable.

A special form of unfree labor was military slavery. Beginning in the ninth century, caliphs bought Turkic boys from the steppes, trained them in warfare, and formed them into a guard. Such warriors were called *ghilman* and later Mamluks. The logic was clear: a captive severed from his kin would be loyal only to his master. In time, however, the guard recognized its own power. In Egypt, the Mamluks seized power themselves in 1250 and ruled the country for more than two and a half centuries. What matters for our subject is this: an enormous share of the surplus went to maintain a professional army that itself became the chief recipient of rent. Commanders lived on taxes from lands assigned to them—the *iqta*. An *iqta* was not property: an emir could be transferred to different lands and had little reason to invest in the canals and fertility of the land from which he collected tax today but might no longer control tomorrow. The recipient of the surplus and the person who might have invested it in development were different people with different time horizons.

### India: Work by Birth

In India, the organization of labor was closely bound to *jati*—hereditary communities that determined what work a person did, whom they married, and with whom they could share a meal. Europeans called them castes, from a Portuguese word meaning “lineage” or “breed.” It is important not to confuse *jati* with the four *varnas* of ancient texts—priests, warriors, cultivators and merchants, and servants. The *varnas* were more an ideal scheme, while real life was divided among thousands of local *jatis*: weavers, potters, smiths, oil pressers, barbers, washerpeople, leatherworkers.

In a village, this often took the form of a system of standing obligations. The smith repaired plows for every household, the barber shaved, the washerperson washed, and at harvest each received a fixed share of the crop from every household. The American missionary and scholar William Wiser, who described such relationships in the 1930s, called this the *jajmani* system. Later researchers caution that Wiser observed a twentieth-century village and that many of its features cannot be projected back into the Middle Ages without qualification. But the general principle appears durable: services within the village were exchanged not through a market but through hereditary relationships.

This organization had real advantages. Knowledge of a craft passed from father to son from early childhood, and the delicacy of Indian textiles was due in large part to precisely this: families accumulated the secrets of dyes and techniques over generations. The community provided a person with insurance—one’s own people offered care in times of trouble. Nor were Indian weavers cut off from the market: they produced for long-distance trade, receiving advances from merchants for yarn and subsistence and agreeing to deliver a set quantity of cloth by a deadline. This arrangement—the merchant advances the inputs while the artisan works at home—would later become known in Europe as the putting-out system. It was flexible, but contained a trap: a weaver who accepted an advance often fell into debt to the merchant and lost the ability to bargain over price.

Hereditary occupation also had a cost. A talent born into the wrong family had almost no way to find its use. The hardest and most “unclean” tasks—the removal of waste, tanning leather, handling the dead—were assigned to communities deemed untouchable, whose members lived for centuries in poverty and humiliation. In our framework, this is not only an injustice but a direct loss: society systematically failed to use the abilities of a substantial share of its people.

### The Incas: Labor in Lieu of Tax

The Andes developed the form of labor organization least familiar to us. Its basic social unit was the *ayllu*, a kin community that held land collectively and periodically redistributed plots among families according to their size. Mutual assistance within the *ayllu* was routine: neighbors built a house or gathered a harvest together, and the recipient later repaid the help with labor for others.

The empire erected a vast machine atop this principle. According to Spanish chroniclers, provincial lands were divided into three parts: one for the communities, another for the state, and a third for the Sun—that is, for temples and worship. The proportions probably varied greatly from place to place, and the scheme as retold by the Spaniards looks excessively tidy. But the central fact is beyond doubt: tax was paid neither in grain nor in money but in labor. This obligation was called the *mita*. Each household supplied workers in turn—to cultivate state fields, serve in the army, build roads and terraces, work in mines, and carry loads. While they worked, the state fed them and supplied drink from its storehouses, then released them to return home. Work on state fields was staged as a festival with songs and feasting: authority emphasized that it was not taking but accepting a gift and answering with generosity. We have already encountered this logic of reciprocity among hunter-gatherers and in the temple economies of Mesopotamia; here it expanded to the scale of an empire.

Recordkeeping used a decimal system: households were grouped into units of ten, fifty, one hundred, five hundred, one thousand, and ten thousand, each with its own person in charge. *Quipucamayoc* accountants recorded on *quipu* cords the numbers of people and workers, llamas, measures of grain, and textiles. In effect, this was countrywide accounting of labor and stocks—without writing in the conventional sense, but with astonishing numerical discipline.

The Incas also had forms close to unfreedom. The *mitmaq* were entire communities resettled in other regions to develop new lands, hold garrisons, or dilute a rebellious population with loyal people. The *aclla*, or “chosen women,” were selected as girls and gathered in special houses, where they wove fine clothing and brewed *chicha* for state festivals. The *yana* were people removed from their communities to serve the nobility, temples, or the ruler for life. Textiles in the Andes were more than clothing: they were given for service, awarded to warriors and leaders, and burned as offerings. The labor of women weavers was therefore one of the empire’s principal sources of wealth and power.

How efficient was this system? The answer is mixed. It could mobilize immense numbers of people for common projects—roads, terraces, storehouses—and do so without money, markets, or wage labor. Its storehouses provided insurance against crop failure: the chroniclers were unanimous in their amazement at the quantities of provisions the Spaniards found in the *qollqas*. But the system rested on two supports that cannot be called reliable: the strength of the center and the concentration of decisions about labor allocation in very few hands. People did not choose where or on what to work; they served their turn. Knowledge of what was needed more—another road or another terrace, another storehouse of clothing or another temple for a dead ruler’s mummy—flowed to a narrow circle of rulers and their accountants, while only orders flowed back. The surviving sources are too sparse to judge how well this circle used its knowledge; but no one outside it had either the right or the means to check.

### What They Shared: Who Controls an Hour of Labor

Set side by side, these four worlds form a spectrum. At one end were Song China and the cities of the Islamic world, where much labor was bought and sold and a person could, albeit within limits, decide for themselves what to do. At the other were the Incas, where the center allocated nearly all labor beyond the needs of the household. India lay in between: a market for long-distance trade, hereditary exchange within the village. Ming China, meanwhile, shows how the same society could swing from markets toward an attempt to assign everything through registers, and then back again.

None of these arrangements was inherently worse than the others. Wage labor offered flexibility and allowed people to move where their work was needed most. Hereditary occupations fostered skill and provided security. Labor obligations made it possible to build what no single family could have accomplished alone. Yet in every case we find a place where the surplus drained away: wherever someone received income not for their contribution but for their position—in salt rents, in the *iqta* of a Mamluk emir, in the privileges of the higher *jatis*, in storehouses that served not only as insurance but also the splendor of the ruling dynasty. And the greater the share of labor allocated by a few people who were accountable to no one else, the more important it became what they directed it toward. How the resulting product was exchanged and distributed—through money, credit, and storehouses—is the subject of the next section.

## Exchange and Money

At the beginning of the eleventh century, a merchant setting out for the market in Chengdu, the chief city of Sichuan, risked putting his back out. The local coinage consisted of iron discs, and according to surviving Song accounts, a single bolt of silk cost strings of them weighing dozens of kilograms. The merchants themselves found a way around the problem. Several wealthy houses began accepting iron coin for safekeeping and issuing paper receipts in its place—*jiaozi*, or “exchange notes.” The receipts passed from hand to hand because anyone could present them and redeem them for metal. Thus paper money appeared, without decree or theory. All it required was trust in whoever had issued the paper.

What followed was a story the world would repeat many times. Some private houses abused that trust: they issued more receipts than they held in metal and could not honor them. In 1023, the treasury took control of issuance and established a special bureau for it. At first the state proceeded cautiously: the issue was limited, and the notes had expiration dates and backing. But paper proved far too convenient a way to pay the armies on the northern frontier. Every new war with nomadic neighbors meant another issue, and by the end of the Song era paper money had depreciated. The Mongol Yuan dynasty made paper the empire’s principal currency—Marco Polo described it with the astonishment of a European who had never seen anything like it—and ended in inflation as well. The Ming “precious notes,” issued from 1375 onward without any convertibility into metal, depreciated even faster: by the fifteenth century, people had almost stopped using them.

The lesson is not that paper money is bad. It sharply reduced the cost of exchange: heavy metal no longer had to be carried across the country. The lesson is something else. Paper turns trust into money, and whoever issues it gains the ability to take part of someone else’s surplus without asking anyone. If issuance pays for war and the court rather than the growth of trade, every holder of paper quietly pays a tax that no one approved. It is the same debasement we saw in third-century Rome, only faster and cheaper.

The Chinese economy answered the collapse of paper by fleeing into silver. Merchants and peasants began reckoning in bullion, and the sixteenth-century Single Whip reform formally converted taxes into silver as well. But China had little silver of its own. The metal came from abroad: first from Japan, then from the mines of Spanish America—through Manila, which Pacific galleons from Acapulco began visiting in 1571. Historians Dennis Flynn and Arturo Giráldez even date the birth of global trade to the founding of Manila: silver mined by Andean commoners in Potosí now closed a ring around the planet and settled in China in exchange for silk and porcelain. We will return to this silver in Chapter 8. For now, note that the world’s largest economy became dependent on a monetary metal whose production it did not control.

### The Islamic World: Money Built on Trust

The caliphate inherited two monetary traditions—the Byzantine gold tradition and the Persian silver tradition—and minted the gold dinar and the silver dirham. These coins circulated from Spain to Central Asia, while hoards of dirhams found in Scandinavia and Rus’ show how far Islamic trade extended. More interesting than the coins, however, was what allowed merchants to do without them.

Merchants used the *suftaja*, a bill of exchange: money was deposited in one city and collected by letter in another, without risking its transport across the desert. Written payment orders known as *sakk* also circulated. Many linguists derive the European word “check” from this term, though the etymology is disputed. We owe the most detailed evidence of how all this worked to the Cairo Geniza—the repository of old papers at the synagogue in Fustat mentioned earlier. Letters written by eleventh- and twelfth-century merchants show people trading between Sicily, Egypt, and India who went months without seeing one another and nevertheless entrusted their partners with goods and money.

How was this trust maintained? Economist Avner Greif proposed that the “Maghribi” merchants of the Geniza formed a closed coalition: anyone who cheated one member lost the trust of all, and the threat of losing one’s reputation within the network was more powerful than a court. His interpretation became famous—and was challenged. Jeremy Edwards and Sheilagh Ogilvie, rereading the same documents, pointed out that merchants also made active use of courts. Both mechanisms probably operated. What matters is that trust between strangers was not a miracle here, but a constructed system of letters, witnesses, records, courts, and reputation.

Islam prohibited *riba*, usually understood as usurious interest. The prohibition did not stop credit, but gave it a distinctive form. A widespread partnership known in Islamic law as *mudaraba* or *qirad* joined one partner who provided the capital with another who carried the goods and conducted the business; they divided the profits by agreement, while accidental losses fell on the owner of the capital. This was not an interest-bearing loan, but a division of risk. Historian Abraham Udovitch believed that Italian merchants adopted a similar contract—the *commenda*—through Mediterranean trade; others, such as John Pryor, trace the *commenda* to Byzantine and Roman law, so the question of direct borrowing remains open.

The *waqf* also deserves separate mention: an inalienable endowment to which a person permanently transferred property so that its income would support a mosque, school, hospital, water source, or caravanserai. Through *waqfs*, a significant share of the surplus of Islamic cities went into public goods without passing through the treasury. But the *waqf* had a reverse side, emphasized by economist Timur Kuran in *The Long Divergence*: the conditions written by the founder were almost impossible to change. A century later, a school might no longer be needed, yet the income would continue to flow to it. Capital became frozen in the decisions of people long dead.

### India: The Silver Funnel

For centuries India sold more to the world than it bought. Everyone eagerly purchased its cotton textiles, pepper, and precious stones, while India wanted little in return besides metal. As early as the first century, Pliny the Elder complained that Rome was paying immense sums to India and the East every year, and finds of Roman coins in southern India confirm that the complaint was not empty. Later, the same funnel was fed by silver from the Islamic world, and then by American silver carried by European trading companies.

Within the country, exchange operated on several levels. Small trade was often served by cowrie shells brought from the Maldives: they had little value, could not be counterfeited, and were convenient for buying a handful of rice. Merchant-bankers transferred large sums through *hundi*—bills of exchange accepted throughout the subcontinent. The network of these bankers, linked by kinship and community, functioned as a private payment system, and rulers often depended on it when they needed to find money quickly to pay their armies. Here we see the same thing as in Cairo: trust built on community and reputation allowed wealth to move without moving metal.

### The Incas: An Economy Without Money

Once again, the Andean world proves the least familiar. The Incas had no coinage, no money in any form, no merchant class, and no urban markets in the sense known in Chengdu or Cairo. Local exchange—potatoes for salt, wool for pepper—did exist, especially on the empire’s fringes, but the principal flow of goods followed a different channel. Communities provided labor, the state gathered its products into storehouses, and then distributed them to *mita* workers, warriors, artisans, the nobility, and temples. Anthropologist John Murra described this as a redistributive economy in which the center receives and gives while almost nothing is sold between individuals.

Murra also drew attention to another Andean solution: the “vertical archipelago.” In the mountains, very different zones lie close together, from warm valleys to highland pastures, and communities tried to maintain members at different elevations: they grew maize at one level, potatoes at another, and grazed llamas at a third. Exchange among the zones occurred not through merchants, but within a single community scattered across the slopes.

This system avoided the afflictions that tormented the Song and Ming: no one could print too much money, because there was no money. But with money disappeared something else it does unobtrusively—the signal of what is scarce. A market price tells everyone where their labor is needed most. A storehouse has no prices; it has only an accountant with a *quipu* and an order from above. If a ruler decided that another fortress mattered more than another grain storehouse, communities could object with neither voice nor purse. Redistribution was apparently impressive and could avert famine. But it was arranged so that every decision about where the surplus would go was made in one place and visible to only a few.

### Where the Surplus Went

The four worlds offer four answers to the question of how to make exchange cheap. China invented paper money—and showed that without limits on issuance, it becomes a way to extract surplus quietly. The Islamic world and India built systems of trust among merchants—bills of exchange, partnerships, reputation—and used them to move capital thousands of kilometers. The Incas managed without money at all, entrusting distribution to the center. Every solution reduced some costs and opened new opportunities for abuse: to the ruler with a printing press, to the merchant network closed to outsiders, to the founder of a *waqf* who bound the hands of descendants forever, to the court that alone decided what was needed most. The question of who can scrutinize the person through whose hands the surplus passes, and how, will become central in the next section—on power and institutions.

## Power and Institutions

Once every three years in Song-era Kaifeng, there came days when the city filled with young men carrying bundles of books. They traveled from across the empire to sit the metropolitan examination for the highest scholarly degree, the *jinshi*. The candidates were confined in the examination compound for several days; scribes recopied their papers so the examiner could not recognize their handwriting, and names were concealed so no one could favor an acquaintance. Those who passed became officials—judges, tax collectors, district magistrates. Civil service examinations had appeared under the Sui dynasty around 605, but it was under the Song that they became the principal road to power. According to the American historian John Chaffee, by the mid-thirteenth century hundreds of thousands of people were taking the lower-level examinations.

It was a remarkable invention. In a world where offices were inherited or bought almost everywhere, China selected administrators for their knowledge and, at least in principle, offered a chance to the son of a family of modest means. Private schools, printed study guides, and family libraries grew up around the examinations. A significant share of the surplus of educated families went into training their sons—that is, into knowledge, which in our framework is a developmental investment.

But what kind of knowledge was tested matters. The examination demanded flawless command of the Confucian classics, their commentaries, and the art of composition under strict rules; under the Ming, this hardened into the rigid form of the “eight-legged essay.” Mathematics, engineering, and medicine scarcely entered the main path upward. The empire’s finest minds spent decades memorizing the same texts: examination competition consumed talent, and the reward was office, not discovery. We do not know how many inventors China lost along this road—but we do know where ambitious people went.

The system had another side. Under the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven, the emperor ruled as long as Heaven favored him; famine, floods, and rebellions were taken as signs that the mandate had been lost. This pushed the court to care for dams and granaries, but created no one who could lawfully limit the ruler’s power today. China had no self-governing cities with their own charters, no assembly of estates that approved taxation. A merchant might be wealthy, but in the official hierarchy he ranked below a peasant; the best investment for his son was considered education and an official career, not expanding the business. The surplus of merchant families flowed out of trade and into land and examinations.

### The Islamic World: Law Without the State and a State Without Law

The Islamic world developed almost the opposite arrangement. Law—the Sharia—was made not by rulers but by legal scholars, the *ulama*, who interpreted the Quran and traditions about the Prophet. Caliph al-Ma’mun, the same patron of translators, tried in 833 to force theologians to accept a single official doctrine and instituted a test known as the *mihna*, with interrogations and imprisonment for dissenters. A decade and a half later, his successors abandoned the *mihna*. From then on, in the Sunni world law remained the domain of scholars rather than rulers: a *qadi*, or judge, was appointed by the authorities but judged according to law the authorities had not written.

This gave merchants and artisans a great deal: partnership contracts, inheritance rules, and debt obligations were known in advance and remained the same from Córdoba to Samarkand. But the Sharia regulated relations among individuals; it did not restrain the treasury. Subjects had almost no protection against the ruler’s own arbitrariness. The Abbasid caliphs practiced *musadara*—the confiscation of the fortunes of disgraced officials and wealthy tax farmers—and at times this became an almost regular source of revenue. A wealthy person who understood that his property might be seized when the vizier changed preferred to keep his wealth in hidden coins, jewels, or a *waqf*, which the law protected better than private property. Money hidden from confiscation is money not invested in a workshop or a ship.

Moreover, as we have seen, power increasingly passed to the military. An emir who received an *iqta*, and a Mamluk sultan who ruled through a guard of enslaved warriors, took surplus by right of force. Islamic cities knew many forms of self-government from below—neighborhoods, communities, *waqfs*—but they did not develop self-governing cities as political bodies capable of bargaining with a ruler, of the kind we will encounter in Chapter 8 in Italy and Flanders.

### India: The Empire That Transplanted Its Governors

The best-known state of medieval and early modern India was the Mughal Empire. Under Akbar, who reigned from 1556 to 1605, his finance minister Todar Mal surveyed the land and set taxes according to average yields and prices over previous years. It was one of the most sophisticated tax systems of its time. The nobility served the emperor under the *mansab* system—ranks that determined pay and the number of cavalrymen a noble was obliged to maintain. Pay usually took the form not of money, but of the right to collect taxes from an assigned territory—a *jagir*.

Here we meet again what we saw in the *iqta*. A *jagir* was not hereditary, and its holders were regularly moved from place to place so that none could take root in a province and become a threat to the throne. From the standpoint of dynastic security, this was sensible. From the standpoint of the economy, it was dangerous. The French physician François Bernier, who spent about twelve years in India in the mid-seventeenth century, wrote that a temporary holder sought to squeeze everything from the peasants while the land remained in his hands and cared nothing for what happened afterward. Many historians consider Bernier’s picture exaggerated—he compared India with France and sought arguments in favor of private property. But the mechanism itself is clear: the person who collected the surplus and the person who might have invested it in wells and canals operated on different time horizons.

### The Incas: The Power of Dead Rulers

Among the Incas, all power converged on the Sapa Inca, the “Only Inca,” son of the Sun. Most curious, however, was what happened after his death. The dead ruler was mummified and continued to “own” his lands, servants, and herds; his lineage, the *panaca*, managed this property in the mummy’s name, brought it food, and carried it out during festivals. A new ruler therefore inherited his predecessor’s power but not his wealth, and had to obtain new lands and rents for himself. Archaeologists Geoffrey Conrad and Arthur Demarest proposed that this “split inheritance” pushed each new Inca toward conquest. It is a hypothesis, and it has critics. But the fact remains that a substantial share of the finest land around Cusco belonged to the dead, and the surplus produced by its workers went to sustain the cult and noble lineages.

### What They Shared: Who Scrutinizes the Recipient of the Surplus

The four worlds built very different institutions, and each was ahead of Europe in some respect. China devised the selection of officials by ability. The Islamic world created a body of law shared across a vast territory and independent, in private matters, of the ruler’s whim. The Mughals developed precise records of land and harvests. The Incas built a state capable of feeding its subjects in a bad harvest. But none of these worlds developed a force capable of lawfully restraining the ruler himself and his military: no independent cities, no assemblies that approved taxes, no reliable protection of property against confiscation. Nowhere was the recipient of the largest share of the surplus accountable to those who created it. What this meant in practice—which investments helped these societies develop, and which destroyed wealth or simply went to waste—is what we will examine in the sections that follow.

## What Fostered Development

In 1202, a book appeared in Pisa whose author, a merchant’s son named Leonardo and later known as Fibonacci, called it the *Book of Calculation*. As a boy he had lived in the Algerian port of Béjaïa, where his father represented Pisan merchants, and there local teachers taught him to calculate with “Indian numerals.” His book explained to European traders how to use nine signs and zero to add, multiply, convert the coins of different cities, and divide profits among partners. The journey of these ten signs is itself a brief history of what fostered development in the worlds of this chapter. Positional notation with zero was invented in India; as early as 628, the mathematician Brahmagupta described rules for treating zero as a number. In the ninth century, al-Khwarizmi set out Indian calculation in Arabic. Through Islamic trading ports, it reached an Italian merchant. None of these people collected rent from the invention, yet the investment continued to bear fruit across the world for centuries.

This scene offers a convenient starting point for discussing development. Let us apply the criteria from Chapter 0 to the four worlds: does output per hour of labor rise, does knowledge accumulate, does the majority gain something rather than only the elite, does society become more resilient to shocks, does exchange between strangers become cheaper? By every criterion, these societies have something to boast of—and by every criterion, we can see exactly what worked.

### Plants and Practices That Spread by Themselves

The era’s most widespread developmental investment required neither palaces nor armies. It was the spread of new crops and agricultural techniques. When the Song court distributed seeds of fast-ripening Champa rice to peasants, and local officials circulated instructions on how to sow it, the cost to the treasury was negligible and the return immense: a second harvest from the same field. The peasant household benefited first. Though landlords and the treasury took a substantial share of the crop, the increase in grain itself formed the basis for China’s population surpassing one hundred million under the Song.

The same process occurred in the Islamic world, even if Watson’s “agricultural revolution” was overstated. A common space stretching from Spain to Central Asia allowed a crop or irrigation method discovered in one place to reach the other end of the inhabited world within a generation or two. In the Andes, centuries of selection did the same work: hundreds of potato varieties adapted to different altitudes represented knowledge accumulated without a single book, yet transmitted from generation to generation no less effectively than written knowledge.

Such investments share a common feature: they are cheap for the person who spreads them and useful to everyone who receives them. A new seed is not consumed once—it is sown again. In the terms of our framework, this is development in its pure form: output per hectare, and often per hour of labor, rises, while a broad majority benefits.

### Infrastructure Everyone Uses

The second mechanism consists of shared structures that no family could build alone but many could use. The Grand Canal, underground *qanats*, the waterwheels of the Orontes, Andean terraces, and the Qhapaq Ñan road demanded immense labor, but once built they served for centuries. The canal reduced the cost of carrying grain from south to north so greatly that the large cities of northern China could grow without stripping the surrounding fields bare. A *qanat* turned a desert slope into an oasis for centuries as long as its shafts were cleaned and repaired. Terraces held soil that the first downpour would otherwise have washed away.

One qualification is needed here, and we will return to it. Infrastructure fosters development as long as it is used for the purpose for which it was built and as long as it is maintained. Local communities built and repaired *qanats*, drank the water themselves, and irrigated their own fields with it—and such systems lasted for centuries. The state maintained the Grand Canal, and its condition rose and fell with the strength of the dynasty: in times of disorder, sections silted up and traffic ceased. Andean roads primarily served armies and officials, but long after the empire collapsed, local communities continued to use them. As a rule, the closer the decision about what to build and repair lay to the user, the longer the structure survived.

### Knowledge That Grows Cheaper

The third mechanism is making knowledge cheaper. Paper, woodblock printing, translations, schools, positional notation—each innovation lowered the cost of learning, recording, and transmitting information. In the eleventh century, the Song government established a special bureau to collate and publish medical treatises; private printers in Fujian produced inexpensive books for examination candidates, merchants, and ordinary readers. The book gradually ceased to be the treasure of a monastery or nobleman and became something a family of moderate means could afford.

In the Islamic world, paper made not only books but business documents cheap: letters, receipts, contracts. This was no luxury for scholars, but a tool for merchants and artisans. Europeans, as we will see in Chapter 7, adopted paper from this world: one of Europe’s earliest known paper mills operated in twelfth-century Xàtiva, in Muslim Spain.

Knowledge has a property that makes it the most profitable investment of surplus: use does not diminish it. Grain eaten by one person cannot be eaten by another. Euclid’s theorem or the rules of long division can be taught to a million people without leaving the first holder with any less. Everything that made knowledge cheaper and freer—paper, translation, printing, schools open to many—therefore acted as a multiplier for every other investment. The journey of the “Indian” numerals shows this most vividly: the era’s most valuable knowledge developed the world precisely because no one could lock it away.

### Trust Between Strangers

We have already seen the fourth mechanism in the section on exchange. The *suftaja* and *hundi*, the *mudaraba* partnership, witnesses and *qadis*, the *muhtasib* in the bazaar, reputation in a merchant network—all were technologies of trust. They produced nothing by themselves, but made possible what otherwise would not have happened at all: an Egyptian merchant entrusted a partner in Aden with cargo whose fate he would not learn for six months; a Gujarati banker honored a bill drawn at the other end of the subcontinent. The cheaper trust becomes, the more transactions occur, the further specialization can go, and the more each person can produce of what they do best.

It matters that these technologies grew mostly from below—from the practices of merchants, jurists, and communities—and depended only partly on the ruler’s goodwill. They were made reliable by rules known in advance and the same for every participant, and by the ability to verify whether a partner had kept their word. We will encounter these two properties—predictability and verifiability—again and again wherever exchange flourished.

### Reserves for a Rainy Day

The fifth mechanism is insurance. As early as the first century BCE under the Han, China developed the idea of “ever-normal granaries”: in a good harvest year, the treasury bought grain while it was cheap; in a bad year, it sold grain at a reduced price, smoothing price spikes and keeping people from starving. The idea was forgotten and revived many times; under the final Qing dynasty, according to the research of historians Pierre-Étienne Will and R. Bin Wong, a network of state and community granaries covered the entire country and, in the best decades of the eighteenth century, genuinely mitigated famine. Inca storehouses played the same role in the Andes, as did *waqfs* with free kitchens and hospitals in Islamic cities.

Resilience to shocks—one of our criteria—is easy to underestimate because its successes are invisible: a famine that never happened does not enter the chronicles. But a society that survives a failed harvest without mass death and without selling its last ox preserves both its human and material capital, and after the disaster can continue from where it left off instead of beginning again.

### Selection by Ability

The sixth and final mechanism is an open road upward. For all the narrowness of their curriculum, the Chinese examinations asserted a principle almost revolutionary for their age: the person who governs should be the one who has demonstrated knowledge, not the one born into the right family. Scholarly circles and madrasas in the Islamic world welcomed capable people from many different regions: al-Khwarizmi came from Khwarazm, Ibn Sina from near Bukhara, and Ibn al-Haytham from Basra. The wider the pool from which a society selects its physicians, scholars, and administrators, the better its chances of finding the best. India, where occupation was largely determined by birth, offers the opposite example, whose cost we have already discussed.

### What Developmental Investments Share

If we put the six mechanisms together, a pattern emerges that will serve us for the rest of the book. Development was fostered above all by investments with three properties. First, they were multiplicative: a seed, a body of knowledge, a rule, or a structure served many people and many generations without being depleted by use, or only slowly. Second, their fruits reached a broad circle—the peasant, merchant, artisan—not only the person who controlled the surplus. Third, whether they worked could be learned from below: the peasant saw the second harvest, the merchant the honored bill, the community the water in its *qanat*; if something failed, those affected learned of it quickly.

And here is what matters: in each of the four worlds, it appears that only a share of the surplus went into developmental investment—and probably not the largest share. Measuring that proportion precisely for the Middle Ages is impossible, and any figures would be invention. But the structure of spending can be judged indirectly. Historians of Song finances agree that in the eleventh century the lion’s share of state revenue was consumed by maintaining the immense army on the northern frontiers. Where the rest went, and why even the most inventive of these societies so often lost what they had accumulated, is the subject of the next two sections.

## What Destroyed Wealth

In the winter of 1128, war raged in northern China. The Jurchens—forest and steppe peoples who had founded their own Jin state in the northeast—had already taken Kaifeng, the city from Zhang Zeduan’s scroll, and carried two Song emperors into captivity. To halt their cavalry, the Song commander Du Chong ordered the dikes on the Yellow River destroyed. The river, held within embankments for centuries by thousands of laborers, burst across the plain. It did not stop the Jurchens. But historians estimate that hundreds of thousands of residents died—there is no exact count—still more became refugees, and the Yellow River abandoned its course and turned south for several centuries toward the Huai River, silting its valley and transforming one of the country’s breadbaskets into a land of perpetual floods.

This scene is almost a blueprint for the destruction of wealth in the preindustrial world. Dams, canals, terraces, and *qanats*, which we described as the finest investments of the age, took generations to create and could be destroyed in a day. The sources of destruction recur across the four worlds, and it is useful to examine them in order.

### War Against Structures

The first and heaviest source was war, especially war directed at infrastructure. The Mongol campaigns of the thirteenth century were a catastrophe for the Islamic world and northern China on a scale whose consequences are still debated. In 1219–1221, Genghis Khan’s armies swept through Khwarazm and Khorasan; Merv, Nishapur, and Urgench were taken and devastated. The Persian historians Ibn al-Athir and Juvayni give death tolls in the millions—almost certainly exaggerated—but archaeology confirms the main point: many cities remained deserted for a long time, while some *qanats* and irrigation canals, with no one left to clean them, fell into decay. In 1258, Genghis Khan’s grandson Hulagu captured Baghdad and executed the last Abbasid caliph. The story that the Tigris ran black with ink from books thrown into it is probably a later legend rather than precise testimony, but the city’s libraries and scholarly communities truly were destroyed or dispersed.

In China, censuses under the Mongol Yuan dynasty show a population far smaller than under the Song. Some of the difference reflects undercounting: people hid from taxes, and the new authorities counted less effectively than the old. But most historians still see in the figures a real decline caused by war, famine, and epidemic. A little over a century and a half later, Timur repeated the Mongol lesson on a smaller scale: his army devastated Delhi in 1398 and Baghdad again in 1401.

Why were these blows so severe? Because the wealth of these societies lay not in coins but in functioning systems: water flowing through a canal because it was cleaned every spring; a city alive because artisans, merchants, and scholars were gathered there. A coin can be seized and spent. A system cannot be seized—it can only be broken, and then its value disappears for everyone, including the victor.

### War Within

The second source was civil war, and the Incas provide the most striking example. Around 1527, before the Spaniards appeared in the Andes, smallpox probably reached the region, traveling from Central America faster than the conquerors. The ruler Huayna Capac apparently died of it, along with his designated heir. Two of his sons—Huáscar in Cusco and Atahualpa in the north—began a war for the throne that lasted several years and, according to the chroniclers, cost tens of thousands of lives. Atahualpa won—and almost immediately, in November 1532, was captured at Cajamarca by Francisco Pizarro’s force of fewer than two hundred men.

We will discuss the conquest of the Americas in Chapter 8. One detail matters here. A system in which everything converged on a single center—storehouses, roads, *quipu* records, the allocation of labor—proved extraordinarily effective in peacetime and extraordinarily fragile when the center was struck. Once the head had been cut off and the nobility split by civil war, the immense, organized empire could not resist as a whole. But the *ayllu* communities, sustained by their own labor and mutual assistance, survived both the Incas and the Spaniards: many of their customs endure in the Andes to this day.

In India, the endless wars of the later Mughals played a similar role. From 1681 until his death in 1707, Emperor Aurangzeb fought almost continuously in the Deccan against the Marathas and the southern sultanates. An immense army, described by contemporaries as resembling a wandering city, fed itself for more than twenty years from the provinces through which it passed. The empire never recovered, and in 1739 the Persian ruler Nader Shah captured Delhi, massacred its population, and carried away the Mughal treasury, including the famous Peacock Throne.

### Blows to Trust

The third source of destruction was quieter than war but no less effective: the undermining of trust. We have already seen how Yuan and Ming notes, issued without restraint, turned into mere paper; by the mid-fifteenth century, the Ming authorities themselves had almost stopped accepting them for taxes. The damage went beyond the losses borne by paper holders. China abandoned paper money for centuries and moved to silver bullion that had to be imported from Japan and the Americas. An invention six centuries ahead of Europe was discredited so thoroughly that the Qing attempted state paper money only twice, briefly and unsuccessfully—in the 1650s and 1850s—and a permanent paper currency returned to China only on a Western model in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

The confiscations discussed in the section on power belong here too—the *musadara* of the Abbasid caliphs and the squeezing of *jagirs* by temporary holders. Every confiscation destroys more than one person’s fortune: it teaches everyone else to hide rather than invest.

### The Rejection of Openness

The fourth and final source was voluntary closure. Even before Zheng He’s voyages, in 1371 the founder of the Ming imposed the *haijin*, a ban on private maritime trade; the expeditions themselves were state trade conducted on top of that ban. When the voyages ended, the prohibition remained—sometimes relaxed, sometimes tightened; a partial repeal in 1567 permitted trade through the port of Yuegang in Fujian. But two centuries of bans did not stop trade; they merely drove it into smuggling. The “Japanese pirates,” or *wokou*, who ravaged the coast in the sixteenth century were, historians conclude, mostly Chinese merchants and sailors made criminals by law. A ban intended to create order produced disorder and surrendered Asian maritime trade to other hands.

This case is especially instructive because no enemy destroyed anything. No one took away the knowledge of how to build large ships and sail them across the ocean—people simply stopped using it, and within a generation or two there was no one left to pass it on.

### What Destructive Forces Share

Taken together, these cases show that the most destructive blows were those that struck multiplicative investments—the very structures, knowledge, and networks of trust identified in the preceding section as the chief sources of development. War destroyed dams and libraries. Debased money destroyed trust in exchange. Confiscation destroyed the desire to invest. Prohibitions destroyed knowledge that lives only while it is practiced.

And there is another pattern. Systems in which decisions and reserves were concentrated at a single point endured shocks worst: the Inca Empire centered on Cusco; irrigation dependent on state works that ceased with a dynasty. Systems distributed among their users endured best: communal *qanats*, Andean *ayllus*, merchant networks that moved their business to another city after one was destroyed. Surplus invested in such systems proved the most resilient.

But war, debased money, and confiscation are not the whole story of where the surplus disappeared. A substantial share did not burn in fires, but flowed away quietly and lawfully—into things that neither fostered development nor caused destruction, but simply yielded nothing. That is the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

In 1631, Mumtaz Mahal, the beloved wife of the Mughal emperor Shah Jahan, died in Burhanpur while giving birth to her fourteenth child. A year later, construction began on her tomb on the bank of the Yamuna in Agra. The main mausoleum was completed around 1643, and the full complex by 1653. White marble came from Makrana in Rajasthan, hundreds of kilometers away; precious and semiprecious stones for the inlays came from across Asia. The French traveler Jean-Baptiste Tavernier, who saw the construction, wrote of twenty thousand workers; the exact number is unknown, but clearly thousands of stonecutters, carvers, porters, and drivers labored over the course of a generation. Around the same time, the Peacock Throne was made for the same emperor, consuming, according to the court historian Abd al-Hamid Lahori, seven years of jewelers’ labor and immense quantities of gold and gemstones.

The Taj Mahal is beautiful, and millions see it today. But let us ask of it what we asked of the pyramids in Chapter 3: where did the surplus go, and what did it give those who created it? The marble, gold, and labor were paid for from the land tax, which, as we have seen, temporary holders of *jagirs* collected from peasants. A peasant near Agra received from the mausoleum neither a well, nor a road, nor lower taxes. And in 1658 Shah Jahan’s son Aurangzeb overthrew his father and confined him in Agra Fort, from which, legend says, the old emperor gazed at his wife’s tomb for the rest of his life. As we already know, Nader Shah carried the Peacock Throne to Persia eighty years later. A treasure amassed for prestige became the most convenient prize for a plunderer.

Wasteful spending differs from destruction in that it breaks nothing. It simply turns surplus into something that does not multiply: it does not serve production, make knowledge and exchange cheaper, or insure against disaster. In the four worlds of this chapter, it took several recurring forms.

### Palaces and Capitals

The first form was prestige construction beyond function. Samarra offers the clearest example. In 836, the Abbasid caliph al-Mu’tasim, weary of clashes between his Turkish guard and the inhabitants of Baghdad, moved the capital more than a hundred kilometers up the Tigris. Over several decades, palaces, mosques, barracks, and racecourses arose there; their ruins extend for tens of kilometers along the river, and remain one of the largest archaeological sites in the world. Caliph al-Mutawakkil added another city with its own palace and the Great Mosque with its spiral minaret. In 892, the court returned to Baghdad, and Samarra rapidly emptied. A city that consumed the revenues of a vast empire served as its capital for about sixty years.

The reason for the move is revealing: the capital was built not for the economy or its inhabitants, but to protect the court from its own army and subjects. It is the same logic as transplanting the holders of *jagirs*: decisions sensible for the ruler became losses for everyone else.

### The Court and Its Kin

The second form was the support of a growing circle of people who received income by right of birth. Zhu Yuanzhang, founder of the Ming dynasty, granted every one of his descendants lifelong support from the treasury—immense for princes, modest for distant relatives, but without exception—and forbade them from pursuing any occupation. Two centuries later, their numbers had grown so large that their support became a calamity. In 1562, the official Lin Run reported to the throne that the grain owed to imperial relatives in several northern provinces exceeded what those provinces sent to the capital. Historians estimate that by the early seventeenth century, the imperial clan registers listed tens of thousands of people, perhaps more than one hundred thousand. Many lived in poverty and often did not receive what they were owed, but the mechanism itself was pure waste: surplus was taken from those who plowed and given to those whom the law forbade to plow, trade, or serve.

Among the Incas, the same pattern took a form already described: the lands, herds, and servants of dead rulers who continued to “own” them after death. Chroniclers also describe another expense—the ritual burning of fine *qompi* textiles, the finest and most labor-intensive product of Andean weavers, together with other offerings. Cloth was a value of the highest order in the Andes: it was given in return for service and used to seal alliances. Burning it as an offering meant destroying months of another person’s labor to affirm power and piety.

### Splendor That Devalued Gold

The third form was status consumption and gift-giving. In 1324, the ruler of Mali, Mansa Musa, traveled through Cairo on pilgrimage to Mecca. The Arab historian al-Umari, writing in Egypt a decade and a half later, reports that Mansa Musa and his retinue distributed and spent so much gold in Cairo that its price in the city fell and did not recover for years. The episode is usually retold as an amusing tale of fabulous wealth. But from the standpoint of the economy, the surplus of an entire West African state—gold from mines worked in part by enslaved laborers—was transformed into the ruler’s prestige in a single journey.

China knew a similar expense on the scale of the state: the tribute system. Foreign embassies brought local rarities to the Ming court and received gifts in return that were often more valuable than what they had brought; the emperor was obliged to display his generosity. Zheng He’s voyages partly served the same purpose. We should be fair: tribute was also a way of conducting trade and preserving peace on the frontiers, and the expeditions produced knowledge of seas and lands that could have become an investment had it continued to be used. This is an ambiguous case. What made it wasteful above all was what happened afterward: the accumulated knowledge became neither trade nor new voyages—it went into the archive and was eventually lost altogether.

### Competition for Office

The fourth form is the least obvious. In the section on development, we praised the Chinese examinations for opening the road upward. But that openness had a cost. According to historian Benjamin Elman, by the mid-nineteenth century probably around two million people took the lowest-level examinations, yet only a small share passed, and fewer still received office. Many spent twenty or thirty years preparing—and remained perpetual candidates for life.

Economists call this phenomenon rent-seeking: many people invest effort competing for a limited number of places, while society as a whole gains almost nothing because the number of places does not increase. Much of the knowledge tested in the examinations was knowledge of how to pass them. Some of those who failed became teachers, physicians, or scribes, so the effort was not entirely lost. But a substantial share of the finest minds in a vast country spent decades engaged in what our framework calls waste: investing their families’ surplus in a lottery whose prizes created no new wealth, but merely conferred the right to distribute old wealth.

### The Wall: Protection or Waste?

The hardest case is defense. The Ming Great Wall in its present form—brick-built, with towers and garrisons—was constructed mainly in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, after the abandonment of the fleet. It demanded immense resources and permanent garrisons over thousands of kilometers. Was it waste? Not entirely: nomadic raids were real, and in 1449 the Oirat Mongols defeated a Ming army at the fortress of Tumu and captured the emperor himself. Protection from plunder is protection of the surplus, without which no investment would make sense.

But defense has a property we will encounter throughout the rest of the book: its scale is determined less by need than by fear, and it rarely contracts by itself. The Wall did not stop the Manchus in 1644—the gates at Shanhai Pass were opened to them by the Ming commander Wu Sangui. Defense spending fosters development only to the extent that it prevents destruction; everything beyond that becomes waste, and often a tool of those who extract.

### What Wasteful Spending Shares

Compare these cases with developmental investments. There we found three common properties: multiplicative effects, a broad circle of beneficiaries, and verification from below. Wasteful spending turns all three inside out. A mausoleum, a throne, burned cloth, gold distributed in Cairo do not multiply—they serve once or not at all. Their fruits go to a narrow circle: ruler, court, relatives, priests. Most importantly, the person who created the surplus could neither ask how it was spent nor refuse to surrender it.

The counterarguments must be stated honestly. Many of these expenditures produced side benefits: the skill of carvers and architects, later useful in other buildings; monuments that feed tourism four centuries later; knowledge of the seas. Prestige was not meaningless to people of the time, either: the splendor of a court supported order, and order also has a cost. All true. But the right question is not “Did this expenditure yield any benefit at all?” It is “What else could have been done with these resources?” By the author’s rough estimate (see “Accounting in Grain and Lives”), the marble and stones of the Taj Mahal could have paid for tens of thousands of wells. No one calculated the value of that alternative because there was no one to calculate it: the decision belonged to those who needed a mausoleum more than a well.

## The Lesson of the Era

Between the Kaifeng of Zhang Zeduan’s scroll and Cajamarca, where a force of fewer than two hundred men captured the ruler of an empire of millions, lie four centuries and half the world. We have traveled through four civilizations, each of which at one time could do more than Europe: print books and money, translate Aristotle and calculate with zero, weave muslin no one elsewhere could reproduce, and feed millions from storehouses without a single coin. Yet all four, having reached a high plateau, failed to move from it into sustained growth in output per hour of labor. Let us summarize the argument in five theses.

**First: invention does not mean growth has begun.** Northern Song cast iron, Bi Sheng’s movable type, paper money, the compass, al-Khwarizmi’s algebra, the decimal notation of Indian mathematicians—all appeared here before Europe, often centuries earlier. But invention becomes growth only when someone has an interest in applying it repeatedly to make work cheaper. In China, Mark Elvin argued, the cheap labor of densely populated valleys made machinery unprofitable. In the Islamic world, Timur Kuran maintains, merchant capital survived the death of partners poorly and was fragmented by inheritance, preventing large, durable enterprises from taking shape. In India, crafts belonged to castes, and an artisan had little reason to seek a new method if his son would inherit the old one anyway. None of these explanations is beyond dispute, and debate continues. Together, however, they show that an ability is not yet surplus invested in that ability. Between the two lies the question of who receives the gain from a new method.

**Second: surplus controlled without accountability goes to the controller’s security and splendor.** Samarra, built to shelter the caliph from his own guard; the Taj Mahal and Peacock Throne; tens of thousands of Zhu Yuanzhang’s descendants supported by the treasury; *qompi* cloth burned as an offering. None of these expenditures was madness—each was rational from the standpoint of the person who approved it. We saw in the section on power that none of the four worlds developed a force capable of lawfully asking the ruler what he was doing with what he collected. Where that question is absent, the choice between a well and a mausoleum favors the mausoleum not through malice, but because the well is needed by someone without a voice. The Athenian stele and Roman court from the previous chapter were attempts to ask precisely such a question; here it had no forum at all.

**Third: distributed systems outlive centralized ones.** The Incas showed that an immense economy could be managed without money or markets: people and stocks could be recorded on *quipus*, labor assigned, storehouses maintained, and victims of bad harvests fed. This was a genuine achievement, disproving the claim that no complex economy is possible without prices. But the Incas also showed the cost of such an arrangement: the whole system converged on one point—Cusco, the Sapa Inca, a narrow circle of nobles—and when epidemic, civil war, and a handful of conquerors struck that point, the empire fell apart in a few years. What survived was not the roads or storehouses, but the *ayllu* communities that decided for themselves how to divide their labor. We saw the same in the Islamic world: *qanats* cleaned by communities and merchant networks that moved their business from a ruined city to its neighbor survived the fall of dynasties. Centralization can gather surplus quickly in one place and direct it to a great undertaking. But whoever gathers everything in one place also gathers every vulnerability there—and every error that no one below can detect.

**Fourth: trust is the cheapest capital to create and the most expensive to restore.** The merchant in Fustat who sent a letter instead of silver to his partner; the Chinese trader who accepted a treasury paper note; the Andean commoner who deposited his harvest in a storehouse expecting it back in a famine year—all invested in trust, and that investment lowered the cost of exchange for millions. Destroying it proved easier than building it. Ming notes issued without restraint discouraged China from state paper money for almost five centuries. Abbasid *musadara* and the squeezing of *jagirs* by temporary holders taught people to hide wealth rather than invest it. Each confiscation brought the treasury a one-time gain and took part of the future from society as a whole.

**Fifth: closing yourself off costs more than it seems.** Baghdad’s translations of Greek scholars and the exchange of plants from India to Spain show what open knowledge was worth: it multiplied as it passed from hand to hand. The *haijin* and the abandonment of the fleet show the reverse. No enemy took away the knowledge of how to build treasure ships; people stopped using it, and within two generations it was gone. The trade ban did not stop trade, but turned merchants into smugglers and surrendered the Indian Ocean to newcomers from the other end of Eurasia. The Ming court believed it was saving money. In reality, it abandoned an investment already made.

We can now answer the book’s recurring question. Where did the surplus of the great civilizations beyond Europe go? As always in the preindustrial world, most went to feeding a growing population—China expanded many times over during these centuries and by the end of the eighteenth century, estimates suggest, fed around three hundred million people, more than all of Europe. A notable share went into development, and in some areas more than anywhere before: canals and dams, terraces and *qanats*, paper and printing, state granaries, examinations that opened the road upward, law intelligible to merchants from Spain to India. A large share went into destruction: Mongol campaigns, civil wars, Aurangzeb’s wars, debased money, confiscation. And a large share went into waste: capitals, mausoleums, thrones, support for relatives, prestige gifts, and decades of competition for a limited number of offices. What was new compared with antiquity was the scale and repetition: these societies reached a high level several times, lost part of it in catastrophe, and rebuilt—each time to roughly the same plateau.

We should state the limits of these conclusions honestly. We can measure almost nothing: the share of surplus spent on mausoleums or canals is a conjecture, not a calculation. Pomeranz’s dispute with his opponents shows that even the question of when Europe overtook China remains unsettled. It is easy to fall into the temptation of calling each society “stagnant,” though the Song, Abbasids, and Mughals were periods of rapid change. Nor should we forget that Europe pulled ahead not only because it allocated surplus better: it was helped by coal lying close to the surface and colonies, whose cost to conquered peoples we will discuss in Chapter 8. The five theses are not a verdict, but a list of questions worth asking of every era that follows.

The most unexpected of these questions concerns Europe. While Kaifeng printed money and Baghdad translated Ptolemy, Western Europe was a poor periphery, fragmented into hundreds of domains, where most people could not read and cities were dozens of times smaller than Kaifeng. None of its kings could have equipped Zheng He’s fleet. But neither could any one of them ban maritime trade across the entire continent: if one imposed a ban, trade moved to his neighbor. How this poverty and fragmentation produced a world that surpassed all others within a few centuries is the subject of the next part of the book, beginning with the village, plow, and watermill of feudal Europe.

## The Numbers

For China, more figures survive than for any other preindustrial country: dynastic censuses, tax registers, lists of successful examination candidates. For the Islamic world and India, there are fewer and they are fragmentary; for the Incas, almost nothing remains apart from Spanish counts made after the catastrophe. Everything below is an estimate, and disagreements among specialists are noted.

**Population.** The Han census of 2 CE counted about fifty-seven million people—one of the earliest surviving censuses in the world. At the turn of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, Northern Song registers listed about twenty million households, which historians translate into more than one hundred million people. After the Mongol conquest and upheaval, the first Ming census in 1393 counted around sixty million, though many researchers consider it an underestimate. By the end of the eighteenth century, Qing data recorded about three hundred million inhabitants. Estimates for the Mughal Indian population around 1600 range from one hundred to one hundred and fifty million (William Moreland gave the lower figure, Irfan Habib the higher). Estimates for the Inca Empire vary enormously—from six to twelve million and above. Noble David Cook estimated the population of the territory of modern Peru alone at roughly nine million around 1520, and fewer than seven hundred thousand by 1620. Behind that figure lie smallpox, war, and forced labor, which we will discuss in Chapter 8.

**Income per person.** Angus Maddison estimated average income in China in 1 CE at about 450 “1990 international dollars”—the same as India and slightly less than the Roman Empire. For 1500, his figures put China at about 600, India at about 550, and Western Europe already at about 770. For Western Asia around 1000, Maddison gave about 620 against roughly 430 in Western Europe—the Islamic world was richer then. More recent calculations by Stephen Broadberry, Guan Hanhui, and David Li (2018) place the Northern Song higher: by their estimate, at the beginning of the eleventh century the average Chinese person lived better than the average English person. Their figures then show income declining slowly, leaving China well behind England and the Netherlands by the mid-eighteenth century. Broadberry and his coauthors find a similar pattern for India: around 1600, a level comparable to poorer European countries, followed by a gradual decline. Pomeranz, by contrast, argues that China’s most developed regions, such as the Yangtze Delta, remained level with England until the end of the eighteenth century. The dispute remains unresolved, but both sides agree on the central point: a high plateau, not an ascent.

**Wages.** Robert Allen and his coauthors (2011) compared how many “subsistence baskets”—the minimum food, clothing, fuel, and housing required by a family—an unskilled worker could buy with their wages. In eighteenth-century Beijing and Canton, the figure was about one basket, roughly the same as in Milan or Florence at the time. In London and Amsterdam, it was several times higher.

**Production and scale.** Robert Hartwell estimated Northern Song iron output around 1078 at approximately 125,000 tonnes a year—more than England produced in 1788. The Grand Canal extends for roughly 1,800 kilometers. According to the *History of Ming*, Zheng He’s first expedition in 1405 included more than sixty large ships and around twenty-eight thousand people; the dimensions of the “treasure ships” themselves remain disputed. UNESCO puts the Inca road network at around thirty thousand kilometers; at the Cotapachi storage complex in present-day Bolivia, archaeologists have counted roughly 2,500 storehouses.

**Knowledge and examinations.** According to John Chaffee’s calculations, the number taking the first level of the Song examinations grew from about eighty thousand around 1100 to four hundred thousand by the mid-thirteenth century. Yet only a few hundred people usually received the highest *jinshi* degree in each examination—fractions of one percent of those participating.

**Money and prestige.** Song state paper money appeared in 1023, and Ming notes in 1375; by the mid-fifteenth century the notes circulated at a tiny fraction of face value, and the treasury switched to silver. Later calculations put the cost of the Taj Mahal at around thirty-two million rupees, while the empire’s annual land revenue amounted to hundreds of millions. The figure is often cited but cannot be verified precisely.

**Bodies and life expectancy.** Using eighteenth-century genealogies and registers, James Lee and Wang Feng (1999) estimated life expectancy at birth in China at about thirty years—close to the European level of the same era. Direct data for India and the Islamic world are almost nonexistent; reconstructions yield twenty to thirty years. As in Rome, the average was determined by infant mortality. And, as in Rome, the chapter’s most sobering figure is this one: canals, printing, and storehouses changed the world, but over fifteen hundred years the average length of an ordinary person’s life barely moved.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate and may be wrong by a factor of several. For India, I assume twenty to forty hours of peasant labor per fifty-kilogram sack of grain; for China’s wet rice, thirty to sixty hours: rice yielded more per hectare but required far more hands. A working life is approximately seventy thousand hours.

### The Taj Mahal

**Labor.** No construction accounts survive. Tavernier writes of twenty thousand workers; assuming that was a peak rather than an average, I use ten to twenty thousand people working three hundred days a year for twenty years—forty to one hundred and thirty million person-days, including the quarrying and transport of marble. A monetary cross-check: according to Abu’l-Fazl’s *Ain-i-Akbari*, an ordinary laborer earned about two *dams* a day, with forty *dams* to a rupee, so one rupee represented ten to twenty labor-days after allowing for rising prices. The five million rupees sometimes cited for the tomb itself yield fifty to one hundred million days—the same order of magnitude. The popular figure of thirty-two million for the whole complex would produce a total several times larger, apparently because of the stones and gold; both sums require verification.

**Grain, hours, lives.** Eight hundred thousand to two and a half million sacks; sixteen to one hundred million hours of peasant labor; including the builders’ own time, four hundred million to one and a half billion hours. Six to twenty thousand working lives, with roughly twelve thousand—the order of magnitude of Khufu’s pyramid.

**Alternative.** A brick-lined well ten to fifteen meters deep required, by my rough estimate, three hundred to one thousand person-days (this needs verification). The labor spent on the Taj Mahal could therefore have built forty thousand to several hundred thousand wells. A well with an ox-powered lift irrigated several hectares and made a second, winter harvest possible: hundreds of thousands of hectares of irrigated farmland in the very provinces whose taxes paid for the mausoleum.

### Allowances for Ming Relatives

**Starting figures.** According to Lin Run’s report of 1562, support for princely households amounted to about 8.5 million *dan* of grain a year—twice as much as was transported annually to the capital along the Grand Canal. One *dan* of rice was about eighty kilograms, for a total of nearly fourteen million sacks. Much of it went unpaid; I use one-third to two-thirds—five to nine million sacks.

**Hours and lives.** One hundred and fifty to five hundred and fifty million hours of peasant labor, or two to eight thousand working lives every year: Khufu’s pyramid every three to ten years. The relatives would have eaten without their allowances, so the net loss lies in the elite share—a prince of the first rank was entitled to ten thousand *dan* a year, enough grain for two to three thousand people—and in the hands legally forbidden to work: thirty to sixty thousand adult men by the end of the sixteenth century, another one to two thousand working lives annually (the population figure needs verification).

**Alternative.** Eight and a half million *dan* was a year’s grain for roughly two million people. Half would have been enough to replenish the “ever-normal granaries,” the insurance against famine, year after year—in a country where the famines of the 1630s helped cause the rebellions that buried the dynasty.

**Conclusion.** The mausoleum cost India about as many lives as the pyramid cost Egypt, but it was one construction project. The Ming relatives cost as much every few years—and did so endlessly, under a law no one could repeal.
