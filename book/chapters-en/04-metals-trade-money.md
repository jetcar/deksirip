# Chapter 4. Metals, Trade, and the First Money

## Portrait of the Era

In the summer of 1982, Turkish sponge diver Mehmet Çakır was diving off Cape Uluburun, near the town of Kaş on Turkey's southern coast, when at a depth of roughly fifty meters he saw strange metal “bars with ears.” That was how he described them after surfacing. Archaeologists knew what he meant: they were copper ingots shaped like stretched oxhides—the form in which metal was cast for transport in the Bronze Age, allowing an ingot to be carried on the shoulder and loaded into a ship's hold. Over the next ten years, a team from the Institute of Nautical Archaeology led by George Bass and Cemal Pulak made more than twenty thousand dives to the wreck and raised what is now considered the richest cargo of the ancient world.

The ship, about fifteen meters long, sank around 1300 BCE. Its hold contained roughly ten metric tons of copper—more than 350 ingots—and about one metric ton of tin. The ratio was no accident: ten parts copper to one part tin produce bronze, the era's best material for weapons and tools. Isotope analysis traced the copper to Cyprus; the tin had come from far away—deposits were rare in the Mediterranean, and the metal passed through many hands. According to an analysis of tin isotopes published in 2022 by a team led by Wendy Powell, roughly two-thirds had been mined in the mountains of Anatolia and about one-third at a mine in what is now Uzbekistan, more than three thousand kilometers away. Beside the metal lay ingots of blue glass, logs of African blackwood, ivory and hippopotamus teeth, more than a hundred jars of pistachio-tree resin burned as incense, ostrich eggs, spices, Canaanite jewelry, Mycenaean pottery, weapons from several peoples, and a gold scarab bearing the name of the Egyptian queen Nefertiti. Folding wooden tablets coated with wax were found as well—notebooks in which someone once kept accounts.

We do not know whose ship it was or where it had come from and was going. It may have carried a gift from one king to another; it may have been a merchant's cargo; it may have been both at once, because in that era the boundary between a royal gift and a commercial transaction was blurred. But one thing is clear: by the end of the Bronze Age, the eastern Mediterranean had become an interconnected system. Egypt, the Hittite kingdom in Anatolia, Babylonia, Assyria, Mitanni, the cities of Canaan and Cyprus, and the Mycenaean palaces of Greece all depended on one another. Tin for bronze came from one region, copper from another, grain from a third; without bronze there were no chariots, weapons, or tools.

We know about this system not only from sunken cargoes. In 1887, at the ruins of the Egyptian city founded by Pharaoh Akhenaten as his new capital—now known as Amarna—a local woman found clay tablets. This discovery revealed an archive of diplomatic correspondence from the mid-fourteenth century BCE: about 380 tablets, of which roughly 350 were letters. The kings of Babylon, Mitanni, Assyria, the Hittites, and Cyprus wrote to the pharaoh in Akkadian—the language of Mesopotamia, which had become the international language of diplomacy—and addressed one another as “brothers.” Almost every letter discusses gifts: so much gold, so many horses, chariots, textiles, or copper. The Babylonian king complains that the gold sent to him weighed less after it was melted down than had been claimed; the king of Cyprus apologizes for sending too little copper because his country is suffering from plague. Beneath the diplomatic etiquette, ordinary bargaining shows through—only between states.

This chapter covers a long period, roughly 3000 to 500 BCE, during which three developments reshaped the economy for millennia to come. The first was metals. Copper, then bronze, and after 1200 BCE iron gradually displaced stone, making tools stronger and, eventually, cheaper. The second was long-distance trade. In the previous chapter, surplus was collected by temple and palace; now a merchant appeared beside them, deciding independently what to buy, where to carry it, and how to invest the profit. The Assyrian merchants of Kanesh, whom we have already encountered, were the first signs of this shift; they were followed by the merchant houses of Ugarit and Cyprus and, in the early first millennium BCE, by the Phoenicians, who created a trading network stretching from Lebanon to Spain and gave the world the alphabet. The third change was coinage. Around 600 BCE, in Lydia, a kingdom in the west of present-day Turkey, pieces of precious metal began to be struck with an official mark, giving exchange for the first time an instrument that did not have to be trusted by weight.

But midway through this period, catastrophe struck. Around 1200 BCE, the interconnected world of the Bronze Age collapsed: within a few decades the palaces of Mycenae were burned, the Hittite kingdom vanished, the rich trading city of Ugarit on the Syrian coast was destroyed, and Egypt barely repelled an invasion by the “Sea Peoples.” Historian Eric Cline chose a conventional date for the title of his book *1177 B.C.*—roughly the year Ramesses III repelled the second invasion by these peoples. The causes remain disputed: drought, earthquakes, invasions, revolts, the severing of trade routes—most likely all at once. Writing disappeared from Greece for several centuries. The interconnectedness that had brought wealth also proved to be a path along which disaster passed from one kingdom to another.

This gives us the chapter's central question, a continuation of the book's recurring one. While surplus was collected in temple granaries, its fate was decided by kings and priests. Metals, ships, and coinage created a new possibility: surplus could be transported, exchanged, and accumulated in a form convenient for a private individual. Where did this new, mobile share of surplus go—into tools, ships, and knowledge that expanded the capabilities of many, or into weapons, chariots, and gold for royal treasuries? And why was a system so skilled at generating wealth unable to protect itself from collapse? We will seek an answer by beginning, as in the previous chapters, with energy and materials—that is, with metal.

## Energy and Technology

In 1991, two hikers came upon a human body frozen in the ice of the Ötztal Alps, on the border between Austria and Italy. He became known as Ötzi. He died around 3300 BCE, and almost all his equipment was found with him: a bow, a quiver, a flint knife, a fire-starting kit—and an ax with a blade of nearly pure copper mounted on a yew handle. Ötzi was neither king nor priest, but a copper ax was expensive and rare at the time and apparently marked its owner's status. The discovery neatly illustrates how the age of metal began: metal was a valuable possession before it became a common tool.

Why did metal remain a luxury for so long? The answer is energy. Extracting copper from ore requires heating it above one thousand degrees Celsius, which in turn requires charcoal—wood slowly burned with almost no air. A smelt consumes a great deal of charcoal, and making that charcoal consumes still more wood. Add the labor of miners who broke rock with stone hammers, of those who carried the ore, and of those who pumped the bellows that drove the fire, and every kilogram of copper can be seen to have “consumed” an enormous quantity of human time and forest. Scholars studying ancient smelting sites on Cyprus and in the Timna Valley in the south of present-day Israel find mountains of slag and traces of deforestation around them. Bronze Age metallurgy was the first industry to consume the environment on a serious scale: geologists studying slag heaps estimate that centuries of smelting noticeably depleted the forests of Cyprus, the Mediterranean's principal copper supplier—though the scale and chronology remain disputed, since most Cypriot slag dates from the Roman period.

Copper itself is rather soft: an edge quickly dulls and bends. Craftspeople learned early that adding arsenic made the metal harder, producing arsenical bronze—and possibly poisoning foundry workers, though direct evidence is scarce. In the third millennium BCE, a formula that gave the era its name spread across the Near East: copper plus roughly one-tenth tin. Tin bronze is harder, casts more readily in molds, and produces sharp blades and durable tools. But it had one major weakness: tin. Copper ores occur in many places; tin occurs in a few distant ones—in the mountains of present-day Afghanistan and Central Asia, in the mountains of Anatolia, whose importance was long disputed, in Central Europe, and at the far western edge in Iberia and Cornwall. Casting a single bronze sword in Mycenae or Babylon required tin to pass through the hands of many intermediaries over thousands of kilometers.

This leads to an important consequence that will matter when we discuss the catastrophe at the end of the era. Bronze was a technology of an interconnected world. It could not be produced without continuous long-distance trade, making the Bronze Age fragile by nature: break one link and whole kingdoms lost their principal material. The parallel is modern dependence on rare metals or on a handful of semiconductor plants.

Where did costly bronze go? Here the question of surplus takes a very concrete form. Much of it went into weapons, armor, ornaments, ritual vessels, and chariots. For a long time, the Egyptian peasant still harvested with a sickle set with flint blades and loosened the earth with a wooden hoe; flint tools continued to be made and used in everyday life throughout the Bronze Age. Metal was concentrated wherever power was concentrated: in palace workshops, arsenals, and tombs. Tablets from Mycenaean Pylos, to which we will return, list smiths who received metal by weight from the palace and were required to deliver finished goods—and a substantial share of those goods was intended for war.

The chariot formed a separate category of expenditure. The light war vehicle, with spoked wheels and drawn by a pair of horses, spread across the Near East in the first half of the second millennium BCE and became the principal weapon of the great powers. At the Battle of Kadesh around 1274 BCE, between the Egypt of Ramesses II and the Hittites, traditional estimates place thousands of chariots on each side. It was an extremely expensive technology: horses had to be bred, fed grain, and trained; charioteers trained for years; the vehicles themselves required constant repair. The chariot was a symbol of the aristocracy and absorbed a considerable share of surplus that could have gone into plows and canals. We will return to it in the section on wasteful expenditure.

But the era also possessed another kind of technology—one that served exchange. The first component was the ship. The Uluburun vessel was built of cedar using the method known as mortise-and-tenon construction: hull planks were joined by numerous wooden tenons inserted into carved mortises, producing a strong hull capable of carrying ten metric tons of metal on the open sea. Under its square sail, such a ship harnessed wind energy—in effect, the first major source of nonliving mechanical energy put to economic use. Water and wind could transport a cargo that would have required hundreds of donkeys and many weeks of travel by land.

The second component was pack animals. In the nineteenth century BCE, Assyrian merchants drove caravans of black donkeys into Anatolia loaded with tin and textiles; according to their records, a single donkey carried several dozen kilograms of cargo. Around the turn of the second to the first millennium BCE, the one-humped camel came into widespread economic use in Arabia, opening desert routes along which incense and spices would later travel. A camel drinks infrequently, carries more than a donkey, and needs no road. It was a genuine transport revolution—quiet and nearly invisible in the chronicles.

The third component was iron. People had known it for a long time: the tomb of Tutankhamun, from the fourteenth century BCE, contained a dagger with an iron blade, and an analysis published by Italian and Egyptian researchers in 2016 showed that the metal was meteoritic—literally fallen from the sky. People also learned fairly early to extract iron from ore, but for a long time the process was difficult and temperamental. Iron does not fully melt in an ancient furnace; the result is a spongy bloom, a mixture of metal and slag that must be hammered repeatedly. Pure wrought iron is softer than good bronze; it becomes hard only if the smith infuses it with carbon and heat-treats it correctly—a craft learned over centuries through trial and error. A letter survives from the Hittite king Hattusili III to an Assyrian ruler in the thirteenth century BCE, politely explaining why he cannot send good iron just then: the season for making it is unsuitable. The letter was long interpreted as evidence of a Hittite monopoly on iron; today most historians regard that idea as exaggerated—iron was simply still rare and difficult to produce.

Iron's decisive advantage lay not in quality but in availability. Iron ore occurs almost everywhere: in bogs, red sandstones, and mountains. It requires no distant tin. When the Bronze Age trade routes broke after 1200 BCE, smiths on Cyprus, in the Levant, and in Anatolia had to master what lay beneath their feet, and over the next few centuries iron spread from the Mediterranean to Iran and India. Metal became “democratic”: an iron sickle, ax, or plowshare could now appear in an ordinary peasant household. An iron ax made it possible to clear forests; an iron plow point could work heavy soils that a wooden ard handled poorly. That—not the gleam of swords—was the era's principal economic change.

Note the paradox. Bronze technology, which depended on long-distance trade and palaces, brought great wealth to a few. Iron technology spread largely as a forced substitute after catastrophe—and brought less splendor but more opportunity to many. Historians still debate how directly the collapse of trade drove the transition to iron. British archaeologist Anthony Snodgrass argued that a bronze shortage provided the impetus; Jane Waldbaum and James Muhly countered that bronze remained plentiful in the twelfth century, while iron spread because of its own merits and the low cost of its ore. The answer was probably different in different regions. But the broad picture is this: for the first time, metal left palace storerooms and entered the field. Energy per person scarcely increased—it still came from human and animal muscle, charcoal, and wind in sails—but the same muscular force, equipped with iron, yielded more. That is productivity growth in its simplest sense.

What remains is to understand who held these tools, who smelted the metal, drove the caravans, and sailed the ships—and whom they worked for.


## Organization of Labor

We begin with those at the very start of the chain: the miners. For centuries, Egyptians mined turquoise on Sinai—above all at Serabit el-Khadim—and copper in neighboring valleys. The work was carried out not by permanent settlements but by expeditions: the pharaoh sent a party of hundreds and, according to some inscriptions, even more—officials, scribes, stonemasons, porters, and guards. Inscriptions left by expedition leaders list participants by occupation, like a staffing table. The men arrived for a season, received rations from royal stores, and left. It was the same redistributive economy as in the previous chapter, merely extended hundreds of kilometers beyond the Nile: the state assembled the workers, fed them, and took the entire product.

Mines in Timna in the south of present-day Israel operated differently, without detailed expedition lists—though an Egyptian sanctuary to the goddess Hathor stood there, while excavations by Erez Ben-Yosef show that smelting peaked only in the eleventh and tenth centuries BCE under local copper producers. So did mines in the Alps, such as those near Mitterberg in Austria, where Bronze Age shafts ran deep into the mountain. Archaeologists find timber supports, ladders, and sluices for washing ore. Such production required dozens of people working in coordination: some broke the rock by building a fire against the working face and then dousing the heated stone with water; others carried out the ore; still others crushed and washed it; a fourth group felled trees and burned charcoal. We usually do not know whether these people were free members of communities working in shifts or dependent laborers. But it is clear that metallurgy required, for the first time, professional crews engaged not in producing food but in one narrow link of a long chain.

Next in the chain came the smith. Clay tablets from Mycenaean Pylos in southwestern Greece provide the clearest account of how his labor was organized under a palace. They are written in Linear B, a syllabic script for an early form of Greek deciphered in 1952 by English architect Michael Ventris. The tablets survived by accident: the palace burned around 1200 BCE, and the fire baked the raw clay, turning rough notes into durable pottery. They show that the palace distributed bronze by weight to smiths in villages—the allotment is called *talasia* in the tablets, meaning a “weighed share of work”—and the smith had to return finished goods. The documents name hundreds of smiths and, curiously, many received no metal at all: the records state that they were on the rolls but received no commission. It appears that in its final years the palace lacked copper and tin—the very thread of long-distance trade.

The same tablets list hundreds of women and children who received palace rations of grain and figs. They spun, wove, and ground grain. Beside the names of their groups appear place-names—Miletus, Cnidus, Lemnos—coasts and islands of the Aegean. Many researchers believe these were captives or purchased slaves taken in raids. This was the other side of palace wealth: some of the labor force was acquired by the same means as metal—force or trade.

The labor described in the archives of the Assyrian merchants of Kanesh was organized very differently. These are history's first detailed records of private enterprise, and they contain almost no orders from above. Trade rested on the family. The head of the household lived in Ashur; sons and brothers lived in Kanesh and other Anatolian cities, each with his own role: one bought tin arriving from the east, another led the caravan, a third sold the goods and collected debts. Much of the cloth exported was produced by women in Ashur. Letters survive from Lamassi, wife of the merchant Pushu-ken: she tells her husband how many pieces of cloth she has sent with the caravan, complains about high prices, and demands silver to pay for wool. Here the woman is not a palace ration worker but an independent participant in the enterprise who keeps her own accounts.

The merchants also invented a way to pool capital. It was called a *naruqqum*, literally a “bag.” Several investors, sometimes more than a dozen, placed gold in a common “bag” and entrusted it to a merchant for a long term, often ten years or more. The merchant traded with the money, and profits were divided by contract: investors received their share, while the merchant took an agreed portion—usually around one-third, by the calculations of Danish scholar of these archives Mogens Trolle Larsen. In essence, this was a distant precursor of the partnership and even of equity capital: people unable or unwilling to lead caravans invested surplus in someone else's venture and shared the risk. Here we see clearly for the first time how surplus becomes not a reserve in a granary but working capital—money that operates and returns with an increment.

At the bottom of this chain stood hired donkey drivers. They were paid in silver for the journey and given money for travel expenses; some contracts allowed a driver to carry a small amount of his own merchandise. Wage labor was another novelty of the era, though modest in scale. Most people still worked the land, surrendered part of their harvest to a palace, temple, or lord, and performed compulsory labor. But alongside this mass there emerged for the first time a stratum of people whose labor was bought and sold.

The port city of Ugarit on the Syrian coast, destroyed around 1185 BCE, shows how the palace and merchant worlds coexisted. Its archives list dozens of occupations: smiths, potters, weavers, archers, charioteers, mariners, shepherds, and scribes. Many of these people were called “the king's men”: they received land or rations from the palace and owed service in return. Major merchants too were often listed as royal agents, trading both for the palace and for themselves. One of the city's wealthiest merchants, Urtenu, kept his own archive, conducted business with Cyprus and Anatolia, and appears to have been both a private entrepreneur and a trusted agent of the state. Here the boundary between public servant and private businessman ran through a single person.

Among the Phoenicians, heirs to this tradition, labor was tied even more closely to the sea and to craft: shipwrights, carpenters, rowers and helmsmen, glassblowers, ivory carvers—and dyers. The famous purple dye that gave the Phoenicians their Greek name—the Greek *phoinix* means a dark red or purple color—was extracted from glands of predatory sea snails. Each snail yielded a speck of pigment, which is why enormous heaps of crushed shells can still be found near Sidon and Tyre. It was hard, foul-smelling, painstaking work that required an enormous number of labor hours for every length of dyed cloth. Purple was a luxury, and the labor embedded in it served to adorn the elite—another example of how a substantial share of the era's working time went not into bread and tools but into markers of status.

After the palaces collapsed around 1200 BCE, the organization of labor in Greece became markedly simpler. Scribes, ration distributors, and palace workshops disappeared with the palaces. Villages, families, and local smiths remained; in time, those smiths began to forge iron from local ore and work for their neighbors rather than for a palace official. Several centuries later, the Greek poet Hesiod, who lived around 700 BCE, depicted in *Works and Days* a world in which the central figure was the free holder of a small plot. He plowed it himself, kept one or two laborers or slaves, borrowed from a neighbor, and feared debt above all. No palace stood over him—only local aristocratic judges, whom Hesiod accused of bribery and crooked verdicts.

Over these two and a half millennia, three distinct ways of organizing labor thus took shape. The palace model: workers were assembled, fed, and deprived of the entire product. The merchant model: family and partnership, investors' capital, hired caravan drivers. The peasant model: an independent farm with an iron plow. Which one prevailed largely determined where surplus went. In the palace, it accumulated in arsenals and treasuries. With a merchant, some returned to circulation—in new caravans and ships. With a free peasant, it went into land, livestock, and tools, though some still disappeared into debts and dues. What remains is to understand how these worlds exchanged with one another—and what they used to pay.


## Exchange and Money

In the mid-twentieth century, Hungarian-born economist and historian Karl Polanyi proposed a bold idea: the ancient world had no market in the modern sense. Exchange, he argued, was “embedded” in society—in kinship, religion, and power. Kings exchanged gifts, temples distributed rations, while the merchant was less an entrepreneur seeking profit than a royal agent trading at fixed prices in special “ports of trade.” This idea, set out in the 1957 collection *Trade and Market in the Early Empires*, shaped historians' thinking for decades. The Amarna Letters seemed to confirm it: kings called one another brothers and spoke not of prices but of gifts.

Polanyi knew the Kanesh tablets and interpreted them in support of his theory—as “trade without markets.” But when Mogens Larsen and Klaas Veenhof studied them in detail in the 1960s and 1970s, a different picture emerged. The archives contained precisely what the theory said should not exist. Assyrian merchants constantly reported prices to one another: tin had grown cheaper in Ashur, textiles more expensive in Anatolia; do not rush to sell, hold the goods until the next caravan arrives. Prices fluctuated, and merchants profited from the movement. According to calculations by scholars of the archive, tin bought in Ashur sold for roughly twice as much in Anatolia; textiles yielded no less. From that difference came the cost of donkeys, drivers, fodder, and road tolls—and a profit still remained large enough for people to spend half their lives traveling. It was a market: small, surrounded by power and custom, but real.

The truth, as often happens, lies between the extremes. Three ways of distributing goods coexisted in the Bronze Age, and we have already encountered each in earlier chapters. Gift exchange among kings and nobles, in which not only the object but the relationship, honor, and obligation to reciprocate mattered. Redistribution, in which palace and temple collected goods and distributed them according to quotas. And trade, a transaction between people each seeking advantage. The Uluburun ship may have carried all three at once: a royal gift, a palace order, and a merchant's goods in a single hold. What was new in the era was that the third mode grew faster than the first two.

Power naturally wanted its share. Anatolian rulers whose lands the caravans crossed collected duties and reserved the right to buy some of the imported textiles first. Merchants, in turn, sought loopholes. The Kanesh letters contain an expression translated as “the narrow track”: a mountain detour along which a caravan could avoid the collectors. Merchants advise one another to send tin by that track if it is safe—and immediately warn that those caught are punished and their goods confiscated. The dispute over how much surplus should go to the person who guards the road and how much to the person who carries goods along it began long before coinage.

What, then, did people use to pay? As we saw in Chapter 3, silver measured by weight served as a unit of value in the Near East. In the age of long-distance trade, this practice spread far beyond Mesopotamia. Archaeologists excavating cities in the Levant find hoards of so-called hacksilver—cut pieces of ingots, rings, and jewelry stored in clay jars or cloth pouches. Analysis shows that the silver came from far away—from Anatolia and the Aegean and, later, from Spain. A piece of silver knew no borders: it could be weighed and accepted in any port. But this form of payment had plenty of inconveniences. Every transaction required weighing; weights could be false, and silver could be diluted with copper or lead. Traders therefore carried their own balances and weights and nicked pieces of metal to check for a counterfeit core. Recall the Babylonian king who complained to the pharaoh that gold weighed less after melting than promised: distrust of metal was a headache even at the very top.

After the catastrophe around 1200 BCE, the palaces disappeared but trade did not. The Phoenician cities took it up: Byblos, Sidon, and above all Tyre. The Phoenicians did not create a great empire; they created a network. According to tradition, settlers from Tyre founded Carthage on the coast of present-day Tunisia around 814 BCE; in roughly the same centuries, their trading posts appeared on Cyprus, Sicily, Sardinia, and at Gadir—modern Cádiz—beyond the Strait of Gibraltar. The great attraction in the west was Iberian silver: mines around Río Tinto in southern Spain supplied metal that flowed eastward on Phoenician ships. The biblical prophet Ezekiel devoted a long lament to Tyre, listing goods that passed through the city—from Spanish silver, tin, and lead to horses, ivory, and costly textiles. To contemporaries, Tyre was what Venice or Amsterdam would later become: a crossroads through which other people's wealth flowed.

In the fifth century BCE, the Greek historian Herodotus recorded a story about how Carthaginians traded with inhabitants of the African coast beyond the Pillars of Heracles. The merchants unloaded their goods on shore, lit a smoky fire, and returned to their ships. Local people came, placed gold beside the goods, and withdrew. If the merchants considered the payment sufficient, they took the gold and sailed away; if not, they waited again, and the local people added more. According to Herodotus, neither side touched the other's property until the transaction was complete. Whether truth or legend, the story captures the essence of trade among strangers: it is possible even without a common language if both sides are confident they will not be robbed.

The alphabet belongs here because it too was a technology of exchange. Mesopotamian cuneiform had hundreds of signs, as did Egyptian hieroglyphs; training a scribe took years, and writing remained the craft of a narrow circle attached to temple and palace. The first inscriptions in a script in which each sign represented a consonant sound, with only about two dozen signs in all, appeared around the nineteenth to eighteenth centuries BCE—in Egypt and Sinai, including at Serabit el-Khadim itself. Egyptologist Orly Goldwasser argues that they were left by Semitic-speaking workers and hired members of Egyptian expeditions—people who had not trained as scribes and adapted familiar hieroglyphs into signs for the sounds of their own language. On the Levantine coast, the script was refined, and by the end of the second millennium the Phoenician alphabet had taken shape. One early Phoenician inscription, on the sarcophagus of King Ahiram of Byblos, is usually dated to around the tenth century BCE. By the common estimate, the basics of such a script could be learned in weeks or months, not years. For a merchant who needed to record a debt, draft a contract, or send a letter to a partner in a distant port, this was an enormous relief. The alphabet sailed across the Mediterranean with the merchandise; the Greeks adopted it around the eighth century BCE, adding signs for vowels, and the Latin and Cyrillic alphabets descended from Greek. It may be the most enduring “commodity” ever exported from Tyre—and the clearest example of trade spreading not only things but knowledge.

Now for the final and most important invention of the era. The Pactolus River, which flowed through Sardis, the capital of Lydia, carried grains of electrum—a natural alloy of gold and silver—in its sand. Around 600 BCE—the exact date is disputed, with estimates ranging from the late seventh to the first half of the sixth century—the Lydians began making small pieces of electrum of standard weight and stamping them with a mark, a lion's head on royal coinage. An early hoard of such coins was found during excavations at the Temple of Artemis in Ephesus. Herodotus wrote that the Lydians were the first to mint and use coins of gold and silver and the first to engage in retail trade. Later, under King Croesus, who ruled in the mid-sixth century and whose wealth became proverbial, electrum was replaced by separate coins of nearly pure gold and nearly pure silver.

Why did the stamp matter so much? Because it transferred verification from every buyer to a single issuer—the authority minting the coin. Weighing and cutting were no longer necessary: the stamp promised weight and purity. Transaction costs fell, making small purchases profitable when they had previously not justified the trouble of scales. The Lydians even minted tiny fractions, coins weighing less than a gram. Coinage spread rapidly through the Greek cities of Asia Minor and the Aegean: Aegina issued silver coins bearing a turtle; Athens later issued its famous “owls.” Remarkably, coinage appeared independently in other places in roughly the same centuries: bronze money shaped like small spades and knives circulated in China, while India used silver pieces bearing punch marks. Growing markets and states in different parts of the world arrived at the same solution.

But the solution had another side. Analyses of early Lydian coins show that they contained markedly less gold than the river's natural electrum—about 55 percent rather than roughly 70 percent: the alloy was apparently deliberately diluted with silver, while the coin circulated at face value. Some researchers, including Jane Ramage and Paul Craddock, argue that miners in the Pactolus were chiefly washing for gold and that the electrum used for coinage was produced artificially—in which case “dilution” was the technology of the alloy itself, though the treasury's gain remained unchanged. The difference stayed with the treasury. From its beginning, coinage was therefore not only a convenient instrument of exchange but a source of income for whoever minted it. In *Debt: The First 5,000 Years*, David Graeber goes further, arguing that coinage arose primarily to pay soldiers and collect taxes, and that markets grew around armies and treasuries. Other researchers see it first and foremost as a response to the needs of trade. Most likely both are true: coinage is useful to merchant and king alike, though their interests differ.

Here the question of surplus acquires a new urgency. While value was stored in a granary or an ingot, it was difficult to hide and difficult to put to work. Coinage made surplus mobile: it could be accumulated, lent, sent overseas, invested in a merchant's “bag” or in a ship. This opened a path to development—to capital that works. But the same mobility also made extraction easier: taxes were easier to collect in coin, mercenaries easier to maintain, and the temptation to “dilute” the metal never disappeared. In the next chapter we will see how the Roman Empire yielded to this temptation and what it cost. For now, we turn to who set the rules for this new mobile economy—and who ensured they were obeyed.


## Power and Institutions

Around 1259 BCE, the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II and the Hittite king Hattusili III made peace. A decade and a half earlier, their armies had met at Kadesh, and neither side had been able to prevail. The treaty has come down to us in two versions: the Egyptian text was carved on the walls of the temples at Karnak and the Ramesseum in Thebes, while the Hittite version was written in Akkadian on clay tablets from the Hittite capital of Hattusa. It is the earliest peace treaty between great powers whose complete text survives, and a copy now hangs in the United Nations building in New York. It is worth reading not for the solemn oaths but for the dull clauses. The kings promise not to attack one another, to provide aid against rebels—and to extradite fugitives. If a nobleman or an ordinary subject flees from one country to the other, he is to be returned but neither executed nor mutilated. Two states had agreed on rules that operated across borders.

This was the principal institutional achievement of the Late Bronze Age: a club of great powers emerged, with its own etiquette and norms. Kings called one another “brother,” exchanged envoys, gifts, and brides, concluded treaties, and invoked them in disputes. This mattered for trade. A merchant traveling from Ugarit to Egypt or from Cyprus to Anatolia crossed the domains of several rulers. Without agreements among them, every border would have become a lottery.

The archives of Ugarit show how these arrangements worked in practice. The city was a Hittite vassal, and Hittite kings adjudicated its disputes with its neighbors. Agreements survive specifying what was to happen if a merchant was killed in foreign territory: the city or ruler in whose lands the killing occurred had to find the murderers; if they failed, they had to pay a fixed compensation in silver and return the missing goods. Responsibility for the safety of a road fell to whoever controlled it. This was, in essence, an early form of insurance, except that the insurer was the state and the incentive was the threat of a diplomatic scandal. Another decree by the same Hattusili III regulated the presence in Ugarit of merchants from the Anatolian port of Ura: they were allowed to trade, but had to return home for the winter and could not buy houses or fields in the city. The local authorities protected their subjects from excessively wealthy outsiders while still leaving the door open to trade.

Even more interesting is how the merchants governed themselves. The Assyrian community at Kanesh was called the *karum*—a word that meant “quay” or “merchant quarter” and later came to mean the merchant corporation itself. The *karum* had an assembly, “great and small,” that settled disputes between merchants, collected debts, levied contributions for common needs, and negotiated with local Anatolian rulers. Above it stood a supreme court known as “the City”—that is, the assembly of citizens in Ashur, whose decisions were carried to Kanesh with the caravans. Ashur itself was a remarkably modest state: in inscriptions, its ruler called himself not king but steward of the god Ashur and acted together with the city assembly. Treaties that Ashur concluded with Anatolian princes fixed the level of duties and guaranteed merchants security and access to justice. In other words, the rules of trade were created not only from above but also from below, by those with a stake in them, and rested on written agreements.

The Mycenaean palaces were organized quite differently. The Pylos tablets call the supreme ruler the *wanax*; alongside him stood a military commander and an entire hierarchy of officials who administered the districts. The palace knew how many sheep each village had, how much bronze had been issued to every smith, and how many women received rations. But everything we see in these records flows in one direction—toward the center. The tablets contain neither contracts between private individuals nor merchants' letters. Mycenaean power was an efficient machine for accounting and extraction, and for that very reason, once the machine broke down, almost nothing survived it: the scribes, writing, and the very habit of managing a complex economy vanished together with the palaces. An institution that keeps all its knowledge in one building dies with that building.

After the catastrophe, two new forms of power came to the fore, and both matter to our inquiry. The first was the Neo-Assyrian Empire, which rose between the ninth and seventh centuries BCE. The Assyrian kings built the first truly great military power, stretching from Iran to Egypt. They laid royal roads with relay stations, maintained a standing army, and carried on detailed correspondence with their governors. Their main source of income, however, was tribute. Conquered cities sent silver, metal, textiles, and horses every year, while those that rebelled were devastated and their people deported. Assyrian inscriptions proudly enumerate the deportees; the historian Bustenay Oded, who compiled these figures, estimated their total over three centuries at several million people—a rough estimate, but an impressive order of magnitude. The displaced were settled on depopulated lands and in new capitals, so the Assyrian state simultaneously destroyed and reshaped the economies of entire regions. The Phoenician cities paid Assyria tribute in silver, and Susan Frankenstein hypothesized in 1979 that this demand for silver pushed Tyre to seek new sources of the metal ever farther west, as far as Spain. If so, the empire that extracted surplus indirectly spurred the trade that created it. The counterargument comes from radiocarbon dates from Huelva in Spain and analyses of hacksilver from Levantine hoards: the Phoenicians were reaching the west as early as the tenth and ninth centuries, before Assyria began demanding silver. Tribute may not have opened the route west but merely widened it. The hypothesis remains disputed, but it vividly shows how extraction and development intertwine.

The second form was the city governed not by a god-king but by citizens—or at least by wealthy citizens. The Phoenician cities had kings, but councils of elders operated alongside them; in time, Carthage developed a system with elected chief magistrates, the *suffetes*, and a council. In the fourth century BCE, Aristotle examined the Carthaginian constitution alongside those of Sparta and Crete and rated it highly. In Greece, meanwhile, the polis rose after the “Dark Ages”—a civic community in which decisions were made by a popular assembly and elected officials.

How this new form of government worked can be seen clearly in an example that echoes the previous chapter. At the beginning of the sixth century BCE, Athens stood on the brink of civil war: many peasants were indebted to aristocratic neighbors, their lands had been pledged, and they themselves or their children were falling into slavery for debt. Around 594 BCE, the Athenians elected Solon—poet and, according to tradition, merchant—as archon, the highest official, and charged him with reconciling the two sides. Solon carried out a reform known as the *seisachtheia*, the “shaking off of burdens”: debts secured by land and by the person of the debtor were canceled, debtors sold overseas were redeemed, and henceforth the law prohibited loans secured by a person's freedom. This resembles the Mesopotamian decrees “restoring justice,” but with one crucial difference. There, a king graciously canceled debts, and a generation later the cycle began again. Solon made the prohibition a permanent rule, written into law and binding on future rulers as well. A free citizen ceased to be a potential commodity—and thus remained a soldier, taxpayer, and master of his own labor.

Finally, another institution entered history quietly alongside Lydian coinage: the state as guarantor of the standard. The stamp on a coin is a promise by the authorities, and its value depends on whether they keep their word. As we have seen, the Lydian kings allowed themselves to profit from it. In time, the Greek cities that minted silver realized that reliable coinage was itself a source of power: Athenian “owls” were valued throughout the Mediterranean precisely because their weight and purity remained dependable for so long. Trust in money became a public good for the first time—one that governments could preserve or squander.

To sum up: three types of power took shape in this era, and each answered the question of where surplus went in its own way. The palace gathered it at the center and kept it under control, but was fragile. The empire took it from its neighbors by force and spent it on the army, palaces, and new conquests. The city and the merchant community established rules protecting transactions, debts, and individuals—and thereby left more surplus with those who produced it and put it back into circulation. In the following sections, we will examine which of these paths brought development, and which brought destruction or waste.


## What Drove Development

If we return to the five measures from the introduction—output per person, the accumulation of knowledge, the lives of the majority, resilience, and transaction costs—it is not difficult to find forces of development in this era. What is harder to notice is that almost all of them were at work somewhere other than where the chroniclers were looking. Chronicles celebrated chariots and victories; real growth was taking place in the smithy yard, in the ship's hold, on the merchant's wax tablet, and at the market stall. Let us examine five mechanisms.

The first was **cheaper tools**. Bronze made tools better but kept them rare; iron made them commonplace. When ore can be dug beside the neighboring marsh instead of awaiting tin from thousands of kilometers away, the metal sickle, axe, and plowshare cease to be palace treasures and become part of a peasant household. The iron axe sped the clearing of forests; the iron plowshare opened heavy soils to cultivation; iron picks and wedges made the work of quarrymen and miners easier. None of this increased the amount of energy available to a person—it still came from muscles, oxen, and charcoal. But every hour of muscular labor produced more. This is the simplest and most reliable kind of development: the same labor, better equipped. And it matters who received the equipment. The bronze sword served the few; the iron sickle served the many.

The second mechanism was **specialization through trade**. Cyprus could not supply itself with bronze without tin; Egypt could not build ships without Lebanese cedar; Mesopotamia could make nothing of metal without imported ore. Long-distance trade allowed each region to do what it did best and obtain the rest in exchange. Three thousand years later, the English economist David Ricardo would call this comparative advantage: even if one people is more skilled than another at everything, each benefits by concentrating on what it does with the greatest advantage—or the smallest disadvantage—and trading. The merchants of Ashur had not read Ricardo, but they lived by this rule: they carried tin where there was none and textiles where they were valued, and both sides gained. To this was added the wind in the sails, the first great nonliving force used for transport. A single ship like the one from Uluburun replaced a caravan of hundreds of donkeys.

The third mechanism was **knowledge made cheap**. The alphabet we discussed was more than a convenient notation. It reduced the time needed to learn to read and write from many years to a few weeks or months and thus took writing out of the hands of a narrow scribal caste. The Mycenaean accounting system died with the palaces because the knowledge lived in a few dozen heads; Phoenician writing outlived every kingdom because merchants, mariners, and artisans possessed it in dozens of ports. This is a lesson to which we will return: knowledge distributed among many people is more resilient than knowledge concentrated in a single center. Other skills traveled the trade routes along with writing—glassmaking, textile dyeing, shipbuilding techniques, and later ironworking. Trade functioned as a school whose pupils learned without knowing the teacher's name.

The fourth mechanism was **lower transaction costs**. Economists use this term for everything that must be spent not on the good itself but on buying it: finding a trading partner, checking quality, negotiating, ensuring you are not deceived, and collecting a debt. In the Bronze Age, these costs were enormous: every piece of silver had to be weighed and cut, every road checked for bandits and toll collectors. The era produced several answers at once. Standard weights and measures. A written contract that could be presented to the assembly of the *karum*. Interstate agreements on compensation for murdered merchants. And finally coinage, which shifted the task of verifying weight and purity from every buyer to a single issuer. Each of these inventions made transactions profitable that had previously not been worth the trouble—and the more transactions there were, the deeper the division of labor and the greater the output per person. Most importantly, coinage made petty trade possible: for the first time, a peasant selling a basket of figs could receive payment that did not have to be consumed immediately.

The fifth mechanism was **rules protecting the producer**. The *naruqqum* contract allowed people who did not know how to lead caravans to invest their surplus in someone else's venture and receive a share of the profit; accumulated wealth ceased to sit in a chest and began to work. The *karum* settled disputes without a royal court. Solon's reform prohibited turning a free person into collateral. At first glance, this is morality rather than economics, but its economic meaning is direct. A farmer who knows that a failed harvest will not cost him his freedom can take a risk: plant vines that will yield only after several years, buy an ox on credit, or send his son to learn a craft. Protection from arbitrary seizure—of land, labor, and the person himself—extends the planning horizon from a single season to an entire lifetime.

How much did all this change the lives of the majority? For the Bronze Age, the answer must be cautious: most of the gains accumulated in palaces, and a peasant in Egypt or Pylos was unlikely to have lived noticeably better than his great-grandfather. But for the Iron Age, especially in Greece after the “Dark Ages,” we have an unusual form of evidence: houses. The American historian Ian Morris compared archaeological evidence from Greek dwellings and showed that between roughly 800 and 300 BCE, the typical house grew several times larger and became better built, with separate rooms, internal courtyards, and tiled roofs. He estimates that consumption per person rose by a factor of one and a half to two over those five centuries—slow growth by modern standards, but unusually long and sustained for the ancient world. The estimate is of course indirect and disputed, but the direction matters: where iron, coinage, the alphabet, and the protected citizen came together, surplus began to remain in many hands.

Finally, there was an ambiguous case that cannot go unmentioned. Assyrian royal roads and relay stations were built for war and tribute collection, while deportations devastated entire regions. But those same roads also sped up trade and the transmission of news, while displaced artisans carried their skills to new lands. Here the developmental effect was a by-product of extraction—and, as we will see in the next section, it came at a very high price.

So where was the developing share of surplus invested in this era? In metal that left the palace for the field. In ships and caravans that linked different regions. In writing that became accessible to many. In measures, contracts, and coinage that lowered the cost of trust. And in a law that forbade taking a person's freedom for debt. None of these investments looked magnificent. That is precisely why they are so often underestimated—and why we must now look at what destroyed them.


## What Destroyed

In the archives of Ugarit, archaeologists found a letter sent by the city's last king, Ammurapi, to the ruler of Cyprus. The king reports that enemy ships have already arrived, burning his cities and wreaking havoc throughout the land. His own troops and ships, he explains, have gone far away—to Hittite territory and the coast of Lycia—at the demand of his overlord, leaving the city defenseless. The famous story that the letter was never sent and was found in a kiln for firing tablets is now regarded as a misunderstanding: archaeologists have reassessed the circumstances of the find. But even without this romantic detail, the picture is clear. Soon after 1190 BCE, Ugarit was burned and never rebuilt. Only a mound remained; in 1928, a Syrian farmer's plow struck an ancient tomb on the shore of a nearby bay, and that was how Ugarit was rediscovered three thousand years later.

Another group of letters from the same circle reveals a different side of the disaster. The Hittite court insistently demands that Ugarit send a shipload of grain to the Anatolian port of Ura; one message describes the matter as one of life and death. Pharaoh Merneptah records in an inscription that he sent grain to the Hittites to sustain their country. A great power that had recently fielded thousands of chariots was begging a vassal for bread. A few decades later, its capital Hattusa was abandoned, and the kingdom itself disappeared so completely that until the end of the nineteenth century, the Hittites were known only from a handful of references in the Bible.

**The Bronze Age collapse** is the principal destructive episode of this chapter and perhaps one of the most instructive in the entire book. Eric Cline lists the suspects as though in a detective story. Drought: pollen analysis of sediments from the bed of the Sea of Galilee, published in 2013 by Israeli and German scholars Dafna Langgut, Israel Finkelstein, and Thomas Litt, shows a prolonged dry period between roughly 1250 and 1100 BCE; similar traces have been found in a salt lake near Larnaca on Cyprus. Earthquakes: in several cities of Greece and the Levant, archaeologists have found collapsed walls and bodies crushed beneath rubble, while geophysicist Amos Nur has proposed an “earthquake storm,” a series of shocks extending over half a century; the hypothesis is disputed because earthquake damage in ruins is difficult to distinguish from the traces of war. Invasions: the inscriptions of Ramesses III describe an alliance of “Sea Peoples” moving by land and sea, while reliefs at his temple in Medinet Habu show their ships and wagons carrying wives and children—not a raid, but a migration. Revolts: in some places, the palaces and wealthy districts were destroyed rather than the whole city, and some historians see this as the anger of subjects against those who took their grain. The breakdown of trade: as fewer ships brought copper and tin, workshops fell idle—recall the smiths of Pylos who remained on the rolls but received no metal.

Cline inclines toward the answer known as “systems collapse”: no single cause would have been fatal on its own, but they arrived almost simultaneously and amplified one another. Drought raised the price of grain and weakened states; starving people left their homes and became migrants and raiders; raiders cut the sea routes; without sea routes there was no tin or copper and therefore no bronze weapons for defense; the palaces that controlled accounting, warehouses, and workshops lost the ability to feed and arm their people. This is not the only view—some scholars believe the decline was slower and more uneven than the dramatic dates suggest, while Egypt and Assyria, though weakened, survived. But almost everyone agrees on one point: the connectedness that had brought wealth became a channel through which disaster flowed from country to country.

What exactly was destroyed? First of all, knowledge. In Greece, writing ceased: Linear B disappeared with the scribes, and for almost four centuries, until the arrival of the Phoenician alphabet, the Greeks were a nonliterate people. Complex crafts vanished—fresco painting, construction in dressed stone, fine ivory carving. According to archaeologists who have studied Greek settlements, Anthony Snodgrass among them, the number of known inhabited sites fell severalfold in the twelfth and eleventh centuries BCE; the population probably did too, though by how much remains disputed. Institutions disappeared as well: trade treaties, rules of compensation for murdered merchants, and the “club of great powers” with its envoys and dynastic marriages. Rebuilding them took centuries. By our five measures, the collapse struck every dimension at once: output fell because metal was unavailable; the transmission of knowledge broke down because writing was lost; life worsened for the majority through hunger and flight; the system's fragility was laid bare; and transaction costs soared as pirates ruled the sea.

There is an uncomfortable twist here. From the previous section, we know that the collapse accelerated the transition to iron, while the fall of the palaces cleared space for the polis, the alphabet, and the free farmer. Does that mean destruction was beneficial? I do not think so. The new grew not because of the catastrophe but despite it—among the ruins, at the cost of several lost centuries and innumerable lives. We can say only that the palace system was so inflexible that it could not change until it broke. This is not a lesson about the benefits of collapse, but about the dangers of a system that leaves itself no alternative path.

The era's second destructive force was **war as a mode of economy**. We have already seen that the Neo-Assyrian Empire lived on tribute and deportation. Let us look more closely. In 701 BCE, King Sennacherib besieged the Judean city of Lachish; reliefs from his palace at Nineveh, now held by the British Museum, show the assault, the execution of defenders, and a long column of captives carrying bundles and driving livestock. In 689 BCE, after yet another revolt, the same Sennacherib destroyed Babylon—the region's most ancient cultural center—and, according to his own inscription, released canal waters over the ruins to erase the city from the face of the earth. His grandson Ashurbanipal ravaged Susa, the capital of Elam, around 647 BCE and boasted in his inscriptions that he scattered salt over the fields and left the land empty. Every such campaign destroyed what generations had invested in canals, houses, orchards, and workshops.

The empire reaped what it had sown. In 612 BCE, the Babylonians and Medes—peoples whom Assyria had plundered for centuries—captured Nineveh. The city, probably one of the largest in the world at the time, burned and never rose again; two centuries later, the Greek commander Xenophon marched past the Assyrian ruins with his army and, judging by his account, did not even know whose they were. Then came the victors' turn: Nebuchadnezzar II destroyed Jerusalem in 587 or 586 BCE and carried part of its population into captivity, while according to Josephus, Tyre endured a thirteen-year siege. Destruction made into policy produces further destruction: every empire that lived by extraction left behind bloodless provinces and enemies waiting for their hour.

The third was **the devastation of nature**. We have already discussed the felled forests of Cyprus and the mountains of slag at Timna. The era's metallurgy consumed trees faster than they grew, and when the forest disappeared, smelters were abandoned or moved elsewhere. This damage was not sudden like the burning of Ugarit; it crept forward over centuries. But it resembles the salinization of Mesopotamian fields from Chapter 3: capital that never appeared in the scribe's accounts—forest, soil, water—was quietly consumed for the sake of today's metal. History's first industrial landscapes were landscapes of depletion.

The fourth was **debt slavery**. The Athenian crisis resolved by Solon was not a local anomaly. Wherever silver and credit appeared without protection for the debtor, debt turned free farmers into dependents and dependents into slaves. A person surrendered into bondage ceased to control his plot and his time; his entire surplus went to the creditor, and there was no longer any reason for him to invest in something long-term—an orchard, an ox, his son's training. Solon stopped this in Athens, but in many societies of that era and later, the spiral of debt continued to erode the very stratum of free farmers on which both the army and the tax system depended.

The fifth was something that still looked like a minor trick but would become one of the great destructive forces of the future: **currency debasement**. As we have seen, Lydian coins were minted from electrum diluted with more silver than the natural alloy contained, and the treasury kept the difference. In the sixth century BCE, this did not yet destroy the economy: there were few coins, and people quickly learned to test them. But the principle had been discovered. The authority that stamps the mark can promise more than it gives and, for a time, obtain surplus without creating anything. The price of this trick is trust, and as this entire section shows, trust is rebuilt far more slowly than it is lost.

Taken together, the era's main destructive force was not any single war or drought but the combination of a fragile system and a ruling power that invested surplus in force rather than resilience. Palaces kept knowledge, stores, and metal in the same hands—and when those hands opened, everything fell apart. Empires took what belonged to others and burned whatever resisted—and burned in turn. One third category of spending remains, the least conspicuous: spending that destroyed nothing but created nothing either.


## What Was Wasted

Around 879 BCE, the Assyrian king Ashurnasirpal II held a housewarming celebration. He had moved his capital to Kalhu—the city we know as Nimrud—surrounded it with a wall about eight kilometers long, built a palace with winged stone bulls at the gates, planted gardens with trees brought from conquered lands, and gave a feast. A stela found in the palace ruins lists the menu with an accountant's meticulousness: in Assyriologist Kirk Grayson's translation, one thousand fattened cattle, fourteen thousand sheep, thousands of ducks and geese, tens of thousands of doves and small birds, ten thousand jars of beer and as many skins of wine, and bread beyond counting. According to the inscription, there were 69,574 guests, and the feast lasted ten days. The figure was probably rounded in the king's favor, but the meaning is clear without an audit: the surplus of a vast country was gathered in one place and eaten in a week and a half so that everyone could see how much of it the king possessed.

The feast was a trifle compared with the capitals themselves. The Neo-Assyrian kings built them one after another, in what is perhaps the finest example of waste in all ancient history. In the late eighth century BCE, Sargon II decided Kalhu was not enough and founded an entirely new city near Nineveh—Dur-Sharrukin, the “Fortress of Sargon.” For about ten years, workers and craftsmen were driven there from across the empire to build walls, temples, and a palace on an artificial terrace. The city was consecrated around 706 BCE. A year later, Sargon died on campaign and his body was never found; his son Sennacherib, apparently regarding the place as ill-omened, moved the court to Nineveh—and began building an even larger palace there. Dur-Sharrukin never truly came alive as a capital. A decade's labor, brick, cedar, and stone became an almost empty city, excavated by French archaeologists in the mid-nineteenth century.

Why do we classify this as waste rather than destruction? After all, deportations supplied the workers for these building projects, which means that behind every palace stood a ruined village. That is true, and we have already counted the destruction in the previous section. But the construction itself destroyed nothing; it absorbed. The bricks did not burn. Everything people might have made instead—canals, fields, workshops—simply never appeared. Waste is like an engine running in place: fuel is consumed, but the machine goes nowhere.

The era's second great category of waste was the **arms race**. A Late Bronze Age war chariot was extremely expensive. Horses had to be bred and trained—the Hittite treatise of the horse trainer Kikkuli from Mitanni lays out a day-by-day training program lasting about seven months. A light body of bent wood, leather, and bronze had to be made; a driver and warrior maintained; the horses fed grain that people could have eaten. According to Egyptian sources, the Hittite king deployed about two and a half thousand chariots at Kadesh in 1274 BCE; the number may be exaggerated, but the order of magnitude is clear. Each power expanded its chariot force because its neighbor did the same, and in the end neither gained a decisive advantage: the Battle of Kadesh ended in a draw and later in the very peace treaty with which the section on power began. This is the essence of an arms race: both sides spend ever more merely to remain where they are. Worse still, by the end of the era, chariot armies may have been outmatched by infantry armed with long swords and javelins. That is the view of the American historian Robert Drews, though many scholars dispute his theory. If he is right, centuries of investment in chariots lost its value almost overnight.

The third category was **prestige exchange**. The Amarna Letters we discussed contain plenty of practical correspondence, but even more bargaining over gifts. The kings of Babylon and Mitanni ask the pharaoh for gold—in the pharaoh's land, they write, gold is like dust—and complain when what he sends weighs less after being melted down than he had promised. Tushratta, king of Mitanni, repeatedly and indignantly reminds Akhenaten that his father had promised statues of solid gold but sent gilded wooden ones instead. These gifts had diplomatic meaning: they cemented alliances and marriages, and peace among great powers, as we have seen, aided trade. But a large share of the gifts were simply markers of rank. Gold passed from one treasury to another, while horses, chariots, and enslaved women served the same purpose as Ashurnasirpal's feast: to display who was equal to whom.

The fourth category was **treasure buried in the earth**. When Howard Carter discovered Tutankhamun's tomb in 1922, the world saw how much metal the Egyptian elite sent after the dead. The innermost coffin of this young and relatively minor pharaoh was made from about 110 kilograms of gold; the burial mask weighs about ten. Mycenaean shaft graves with their gold masks, Lydian tumuli, and royal burials throughout the Mediterranean all removed metal from circulation. An honest qualification is necessary here. To the Egyptians themselves, a tomb was not an expense but an investment in eternal life, and from their point of view our judgment is unfair. Tomb robbers, whose trials are described in papyri from the twelfth century BCE, returned some of that gold to circulation—through an act regarded as a grave crime in Egypt. But by the measures used in this book—output, knowledge, and the lives of the majority—gold inside a sarcophagus did nothing.

And finally, the classic case that gave its name to an entire idea: **the wealth of Croesus**. The last Lydian king, who ruled in the mid-sixth century BCE, was so rich that his name became proverbial. Herodotus writes that Croesus sent the sanctuary of Apollo at Delphi more than a hundred gold half-bricks, a golden lion, enormous bowls of gold and silver, and much else besides. The gift was an act of prestige and politics—Croesus sought allies among the Greeks—and of faith: he asked the oracle whether he should go to war against Persia. According to Herodotus, the answer was that if he crossed the River Halys, he would destroy a great empire. Croesus crossed it and destroyed one—his own. Around 547 BCE, the Persian king Cyrus captured the Lydian capital, Sardis. There is bitter irony in the fact that the country that gave the world coinage—the most practical invention of the era—spent its surplus on the least practical things: gold for sanctuaries and war for glory.

How large was the wasted share? It is impossible to calculate precisely, but comparisons are possible. In a decade, an Assyrian king could build a new capital that was never needed. In the same time, a Tyrian merchant could make many voyages to the western Mediterranean and back, each time carrying metal, textiles, and skills to places that lacked them. A Greek farmer could plant olive trees that would feed his grandchildren. All three spent surplus, but only the latter two invested it in something that kept working.

There is a counterargument, and it deserves a hearing. Palaces, feasts, and tombs gave work to thousands of people. Stonecutters, carvers, and jewelers honed their skill on royal commissions, and without that demand, many crafts might never have reached the level later useful in ordinary life. Prestige gifts kept the great powers at peace. All this is true—just as it is true that building the pyramids, discussed in the previous chapter, taught the Egyptians to organize immense numbers of people. But such a side effect is an effect achieved “despite,” not “because of”: the same craftsmen could have learned by making ships, bridges, and millstones. Waste is not always entirely barren. It merely gives development a small fraction of what it could have given.

So where did the surplus of the age of metals go? In three directions at once. One part went into the iron sickle, the ship, the alphabet, and coinage, and that part changed the world. Another went into the fires of war and deportation, turning Ugarit, Babylon, and Nineveh to ash. A substantial share went into palaces that were abandoned, chariots that became obsolete, gold that was buried, and gifts that merely passed from hand to hand. This share burned nothing and built nothing. It simply vanished—which is why it is so easy to overlook.


## Lesson of the Era

Almost three thousand years separate Ötzi's copper axe from the silver “owl” of the Athenian marketplace. In that time, metal moved from the royal treasury to the peasant farmstead; the palace scribe gave way to the merchant with a wax tablet; and a weighed piece of silver became a stamped coin. In the middle of this journey, the interconnected world of the great powers collapsed within one or two generations. Let us summarize the argument in five propositions.

**First: mobile surplus empowers both those who invest and those who extract.** In the temple economy, surplus lay in the granary, and its fate was decided by whoever held the key. The ingot, the investors' “bag,” and above all coinage made surplus mobile: it could be lent to a merchant, sent overseas, invested in a ship. For the first time, not only the palace but also a private individual could direct the increment, opening a path to capital that works. Yet the same mobility made it easier to collect tribute, hire armies, and debase currency. The invention that reduced the cost of exchange also reduced the cost of extraction. Which of these two possibilities prevailed was determined not by coinage but by those who set the rules around it.

**Second: a cheap tool for the many matters more than a perfect tool for the few.** Bronze was the era's superior material, but it depended on distant tin and accumulated in arsenals and tombs. At first, iron was inferior in quality, but its ore lay almost underfoot, and iron sickles and plowshares reached ordinary farmers. This did not increase the energy available per person—the same muscles, the same ox—but every hour of labor produced more. The alphabet repeated the story in the realm of knowledge: a script that could be learned in weeks or months did more for the economy than the most sophisticated cuneiform. The era's development came not from the pinnacle of technical mastery but from making it cheaper and spreading it widely.

**Third: connectedness brings wealth and transmits disaster; what is distributed survives.** The Bronze Age built a system in which copper came from Cyprus, tin traveled thousands of kilometers, grain came from Egypt, and every region benefited from exchange. But the system had no alternative route. Drought, migration, earthquakes, and the breakdown of sea routes combined into a chain reaction, and palaces that concentrated accounting, warehouses, and expertise in the same hands died together with their archives. Linear B lived in the heads of a few dozen scribes and disappeared; Phoenician writing lived among merchants in dozens of ports and outlasted every kingdom. Resilience is determined not by the size of a system but by the number of independent supports it contains.

**Fourth: trust is infrastructure, and it is most reliable when secured by a rule rather than a favor.** Interstate treaties providing compensation for murdered merchants, the assembly of the *karum* that settled disputes, standard weights, honest coinage—all reduced the cost of a transaction just as surely as a sail reduced the cost of transport. The clearest example is Solon. Mesopotamian kings occasionally canceled debts, and a generation later the cycle began again. Solon made the prohibition of bondage a permanent law binding on future rulers as well. The difference between favor and rule is the difference between a respite and a planning horizon long enough for a farmer to plant olive trees for his grandchildren.

**Fifth: a power that lives by extraction burns not only what belongs to others but its own wealth as well.** The Neo-Assyrian Empire collected tribute from half the Near East and spent it on armies, deportations, and new capitals—one of which, Dur-Sharrukin, was abandoned almost immediately after its consecration. The chariot forces of the great powers grew in competition with one another and lost their value. Croesus gave gold to a sanctuary and to a war for glory and lost his kingdom. All these expenditures had one thing in common: the decision was made by a narrow circle that was not accountable to those whose labor was converted into the expense. Where the rules were created by the participants in exchange themselves—in Ashur with its city assembly, in the Phoenician cities with their councils, in the polis—a noticeably larger share of surplus returned to circulation.

Now for the answer to our recurring question. Where did the surplus of the age of metals go? As before, most of it went to feed a growing population. Of what remained, a visible share went into development: iron tools, ships and caravans, the alphabet, measures and coinage, treaties and laws that protected debtors. A significant share went into destruction: wars and deportations, burned cities, felled forests, debt bondage. And a substantial share went into waste: palaces and capitals, feasts, chariots, gifts, and gold in tombs. What was new compared with the temple economies was that surplus acquired a second decision-maker. The king still decided the fate of tribute, but the merchant, the investor in a “bag,” and the free farmer decided the fate of their own share—and, judging by the Greek houses studied by Ian Morris, on average they used it more productively.

We should also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. We judge the Bronze Age from the archives of palaces and merchant families, while we know the lives of the majority through indirect traces: the size of houses, bones, hoards. The causes of the collapse around 1200 BCE remain disputed, and the contribution of each—drought, invasions, revolts, the breakdown of trade—will be revised more than once. The origin of coinage is disputed too: was it born for the market, or for the treasury and the army, as David Graeber insists? Nothing here proves that trade in itself leads to prosperity: Phoenician Tyre grew rich through trade but paid tribute to Assyria and fell to its successors. Yet the general conclusion holds: in this era, development advanced where tools, knowledge, and trust became cheaper—and stopped where surplus was gathered by force and spent on displays of force. In the next part, these three ways of directing the increment—palatial, imperial, and civic—will converge in one world, Greece and Rome, and we will see which one prevailed and what it cost.

## Figures

The age of metals left more documents than any earlier era: merchants' letters, diplomatic archives, royal annals, coin hoards. But documents were written by those who had something to count—merchants and kings—and the figures still speak only indirectly about the majority. Everything below is an estimate, and disagreements among specialists are noted.

**World population.** Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones estimate that about fifty million people lived on Earth around 1000 BCE and roughly one hundred million by 500 BCE. A doubling in five centuries was faster than before, but it still amounted to only fractions of a percent per year, and nearly all growth in surplus continued to be consumed by new mouths.

**Metal.** Bronze consists of roughly nine or ten parts copper to one part tin; that was the approximate proportion in the Uluburun ship's cargo: about ten tons of copper in more than 350 ingots and about one ton of tin. The value of iron changed most dramatically. Old Assyrian texts from Kanesh mention a rare metal called *amutum*, which many scholars identify as iron; according to these texts, it cost tens of times more than silver. The interpretation is disputed, but the order of magnitude shows how precious iron was before people learned to extract bog ore on a large scale. A thousand years later, the iron sickle had become an ordinary tool of a peasant household.

**Trade.** By Mogens Larsen's count, the archive of the Kanesh merchants contains about twenty-three thousand tablets from dozens of family collections—the largest body of private business records from the Bronze Age. The journey from Ashur to Kanesh covered about a thousand kilometers and took several weeks; according to these letters, a donkey carried around seventy to ninety kilograms of cargo, usually about sixty-five kilograms of tin with textiles loaded on top. Larsen and Klaas Veenhof calculate that tin sold in Anatolia for roughly twice its purchase price in Ashur, while the markup on textiles could be higher still; that margin paid for the journey, duties, losses, and profit. The usual interest rate on loans in Old Assyrian contracts was about thirty percent per year.

**Diplomacy.** The Amarna archive contains about 380 tablets from the mid-fourteenth century BCE, roughly 350 of them letters. Gold, horses, chariots, and textiles are counted in tens and hundreds; lists of wedding gifts extend for hundreds of lines, while the gold in them is measured in dozens of minas—that is, tens of kilograms.

**Coinage.** The first Lydian coins were minted from electrum, an alloy of gold and silver. Natural Lydian electrum contained about seventy percent gold on average, while laboratory analyses put the coins at about fifty-five percent; the remainder was added silver and a little copper. By numismatist John Kroll's estimate, the treasury that accepted the coin as natural electrum gained roughly 15–20 percent. The principal denomination, the stater, weighed about fourteen grams; the smallest fractions were 1/96 of a stater, about 0.15 grams. The Athenian drachma weighed around 4.3 grams of silver, while the famous owl tetradrachm weighed about seventeen grams. The silver of Laurion quickly became power: according to Herodotus, in 483 BCE a rich vein was used, at Themistocles' urging, to build two hundred warships.

**Violence and display.** Bustenay Oded counted about 1.2 million deportees explicitly numbered in Neo-Assyrian inscriptions; extrapolation to all known cases gives roughly four and a half million people over three centuries. Oded himself stresses that figures in royal annals may be exaggerated, but the order of magnitude is comparable to the entire population of Mesopotamia at the time. Ashurnasirpal II's stela reports that 69,574 people were invited to the ten-day feast celebrating the new capital at Kalhu.

**Collapse and recovery.** After 1200 BCE, the number of inhabited settlements in Greece fell severalfold according to archaeologists including Anthony Snodgrass, and writing disappeared for four centuries. The return path can be seen in houses: Ian Morris estimated that the average Greek dwelling grew from roughly fifty to seventy-five square meters around 800 BCE to more than three hundred by 300 BCE. This is one of the few figures from antiquity that speaks not of a royal treasury but of an ordinary family's life—and it shows that mobile surplus reached that family at least in part.

**Life expectancy.** By most estimates, life expectancy at birth remained between twenty and thirty years, still governed by infant mortality. Metal, coinage, and ships changed who directed surplus, but not yet how long a human being lived.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author's estimate: no scribe ever totaled the construction of a city or the maintenance of an army in person-days, and an error by a factor of several is possible. Assyria and Anatolia depended on rain-fed agriculture—on rainfall rather than floods—so I assume twenty-five to forty hours of peasant labor for a fifty-kilogram sack of grain. A working life amounts to roughly seventy thousand hours.

### Dur-Sharrukin

**Labor.** The construction letters studied by Simo Parpola and Pierre Villard contain no total number of workers, so I calculate from volume. The city measured about 1,760 by 1,635 meters; the wall was roughly 6.8 kilometers long and, according to excavation reports, about fourteen meters thick and no less than twelve meters high, though these dimensions require verification. Including the citadel terrace, palace, and temples, the total was between one and a half and three million cubic meters of mud brick. At two to five person-days per cubic meter—to dig the clay, mold it, dry it, carry it, and lay it—and doubling the total for stone, cedar, and finishing, the result is five to thirty million person-days: between two and ten thousand people working year-round for ten years.

**Grain and hours.** At one kilogram per day, this required one hundred to six hundred thousand sacks of grain, equivalent to 2.5–24 million hours of peasant labor. Adding the builders' own hours, at a ten-hour day, gives 55–325 million hours.

**Lives.** Between eight hundred and four thousand five hundred working lives, with roughly two thousand at the midpoint of the range. That is an order of magnitude less than the Pyramid of Khufu, but Dur-Sharrukin was only one of three new capitals founded by Assyrian kings in a century and a half.

**Alternative.** By abandoning his father's city, Sennacherib himself showed what such labor could achieve: according to archaeologist Jason Ur's research, he built about 150 kilometers of irrigation canals to Nineveh, including the stone aqueduct at Jerwan. Under Charles Erasmus's earthmoving norms of two to three cubic meters per person per day, the labor spent on the abandoned capital would have sufficed for several such systems.

### The Chariot Race

**Assumptions.** According to Egyptian sources, the Hittites deployed around two and a half thousand chariots at Kadesh; I take this as the order of magnitude for one great power. Each chariot required two or three horses and three or four people—a crew, groom, and craftsman. In addition to hay, a working horse consumes three to five kilograms of grain per day. These are modern standards; ancient rations require verification.

**Annual account.** Sixty to one hundred and thirty sacks per chariot, or 150–330 thousand sacks for the entire force, equivalent to 4–13 million hours of peasant labor. Add eight to ten thousand men removed from productive work—another 15–20 million hours. The total is three hundred to five hundred working lives every year. Over a century of competition, that is thirty to fifty thousand lives for one power—more than the Pyramid of Khufu—and its rival spent a comparable amount. One qualification: only the share that grew in competition with the neighbor without providing an advantage was waste; if that share was one half, the estimate should be halved.

**Alternative.** A year's grain for chariot horses and personnel could feed twenty to forty-five thousand people for a year. In its final decades, the Hittite state begged Ugarit and Egypt for shiploads of grain; such a granary might have helped it survive the drought.

**Conclusion.** Temple states left stone behind from their expenditures. Bronze Age powers spent comparable amounts on capitals and chariots that emptied or became obsolete within a single generation.
