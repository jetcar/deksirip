# Chapter 14. The Postwar “Golden Age” (1945–1973)

## Portrait of the Era

New Hampshire, July 1944. In the mountains of the northeastern United States, at the enormous wooden Mount Washington Hotel in Bretton Woods, more than seven hundred delegates from forty-four countries meet for three weeks. The war is still raging: the Allies landed in Normandy a month earlier, and Germany’s surrender is almost a year away. But the people in the hotel are discussing not the front, but how money and trade will be organized after victory. The leading figures are John Maynard Keynes, already familiar to us, who has arrived from Britain gravely ill, and Harry Dexter White, an official of the US Treasury. Both remember what Chapter 12 described: currency wars, the collapse of trade, and the depression that brought dictators to power. Their task is to ensure that it does not happen again. From the debates of those weeks will emerge the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank, together with the rules by which the Western world will live for the next quarter of a century.

Germany, spring 1947. Cities lie in ruins; in Cologne and Düsseldorf, most housing has been destroyed. Surviving official ration schedules sometimes allot an adult fewer than fifteen hundred kilocalories a day. Money is almost worthless: the real currency is American cigarettes, for which people trade watches, porcelain, and their fathers’ medals. People leave the cities for the countryside as “bagmen,” bartering possessions for potatoes. The winter of 1946–1947, one of the coldest of the century, kills, by varying estimates, anywhere from thousands to tens of thousands through hunger and cold—no exact count was kept. It appears to be the familiar postwar picture: Europe had looked the same in 1919, followed then by hyperinflation, depression, and another war.

This time, everything turns out differently. Wolfsburg, August 1955: the millionth Volkswagen Beetle rolls off the assembly line of a factory built under the Nazis and half destroyed by bombing, painted gold for the occasion. Ten years later, a million Beetles will be produced each year, and the word *Wirtschaftswunder*—“economic miracle”—will become part of the German language. Tokyo, October 1, 1964: nine days before the opening of the first Olympic Games in Asia, the Shinkansen, a high-speed train between Tokyo and Osaka, begins service. A country that American aircraft had burned to the ground nineteen years earlier presents the world with the fastest railway on earth.

In 1979, French economist Jean Fourastié described these years in a book whose title became the name of the era: *The Glorious Thirty* (*Les Trente Glorieuses*). The book opens by comparing two villages—let us call them, as the author does, “Mader” and “Cessac.” In the first, most inhabitants work in agriculture, plow with horses, few homes have running water, meat is eaten on holidays, and life is short. In the second, fewer than a third of the inhabitants are peasants, almost every home has a refrigerator, washing machine, and television, many families own a car, and children remain in school until sixteen or beyond. Only at the end does Fourastié reveal that this is the same village, Douelle in southern France, in 1946 and 1975. In a single generation, life there changed more than it had over the preceding several centuries.

The figures confirm the impression. According to Angus Maddison’s estimates, world income per person grew by almost three percent a year between 1950 and 1973—faster than in any period before or since. Per capita growth was about four percent a year in Western Europe and roughly eight percent in Japan. This means that in just under a quarter of a century, the average Japanese person became about six times richer, and the average Western European roughly two and a half times richer. Unemployment fell to one or two percent in many Western European countries: labor was in short supply, and West Germany, France, and Switzerland invited millions of *Gastarbeiter* from Italy, Spain, Yugoslavia, and Turkey. An American family moved to the suburbs, into a standardized home like those developer William Levitt began building on Long Island in 1947—with a lawn, a refrigerator, and a car by the garage. For the first time in history, not a narrow elite but the majority of the inhabitants of entire countries lived in an abundance of which their grandparents could not have dreamed.

The question posed by this book is especially acute here: where did the era’s surplus go? It was enormous—and unlike that of the previous thirty years, it did not burn up in trenches and furnaces. Much of it went into machinery, roads, power stations, schools, universities, and hospitals, while a substantial share went directly into the pockets of ordinary workers in the form of rising wages. But the picture was not as cloudless as the advertising posters of the time suggested. This was also the age of the Cold War and the arms race discussed in the previous chapter: American military expenditure in the 1950s amounted to roughly one-tenth of national income. It was an age of colonial wars: France fought in Indochina and Algeria, Britain in Kenya and Malaya, and the United States in Korea and Vietnam. The “Golden Age” was far from golden for everyone: not for Black residents of the segregated American South, not for Algerians, and not for the peasants of Latin America and Africa, who will appear at the end of the chapter.

Most importantly, historians still debate where the miracle came from. Some—among them Barry Eichengreen in *The European Economy Since 1945*—emphasize institutions: new rules for currencies and trade, and agreements between trade unions and employers that allowed profits to flow into investment rather than strikes and conflict. Others, including economists Nicholas Crafts and Peter Temin, point to the simple mechanism of “catch-up growth”: a country lagging behind the technological leader can rapidly adopt ready-made machinery and methods—the American assembly line, American chemistry, American management. The larger the gap, the faster the growth, until the gap closes. In this account, the miracle was partly an inevitable rebound after two wars and a depression, and it ended once Europe and Japan drew close to the US level. Both sides are probably right: catch-up growth is possible, but it does not happen by itself. Latin America, Eastern Europe, and most of Asia also lagged behind the United States in those years, but caught up much more slowly or not at all. This chapter is about what allowed some countries to turn their postwar surplus into prosperity—and prevented others from doing so.

## Energy and Technology

If the “Golden Age” had a single material secret, it could be summed up in one word: oil. In 1950, Western Europe still ran on coal—it fired furnaces, locomotives, power stations, and blast furnaces, just as it had a century earlier. Oil supplied, by various estimates, only about one-tenth of its energy. By the early 1970s, it supplied more than half. The reason was simple: oil had become extraordinarily cheap. In the Middle East—in Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Iran, and Iraq—fields were developed where a single well yielded tens or hundreds of times more than one in Texas, and the cost of a barrel was measured in cents. The posted price of Saudi oil remained around two dollars a barrel for nearly two postwar decades, and after inflation oil actually became cheaper during the 1960s. In *The Prize*, historian Daniel Yergin describes how tankers, growing ever larger, carried this flow through the Suez Canal and around Africa to Rotterdam, Marseille, and Yokohama.

What cheap energy means for an economy is easy to understand if we recall the beginning of this book: the whole history of human development is the story of how much energy a person can direct toward work. The coal a miner extracts during a shift contains a certain amount of energy; the oil produced by a single well in the same span of time contains many times more. Energy obtained almost for nothing becomes what a fertile floodplain was to a Neolithic farmer: a surplus that can be invested somewhere. But there was another side to this bargain, remembered only in 1973: Europe and Japan had made their growth dependent on fuel of which they possessed almost none. The era’s surplus rested on someone else’s land—on the oil fields of countries that had only recently ceased to be colonies and protectorates, and that for a long time received only a small share of their oil’s final price. The next chapter will return to this fact.

The second pillar was electricity. Electricity itself had entered people’s lives long before—in the chapter on the Second Industrial Revolution, we saw Edison’s first power stations. But only now did the grid reach almost every home and village. In *The Rise and Fall of American Growth*, American economist Robert Gordon argues that postwar prosperity was largely the “harvest” of inventions made between 1870 and 1940: electricity, the internal-combustion engine, running water, chemistry, and the telephone. They had been invented earlier, but entered everyday life on a mass scale only after the war. In the mid-1950s, only about one French home in ten had a refrigerator; by the mid-1970s, the overwhelming majority did. The same was true of washing machines, vacuum cleaners, and televisions. Behind the dry statistics lies one of the greatest and least conspicuous liberations in history: the liberation of women’s time. Washing clothes by hand—boiling them and rinsing them in cold water—took a housewife an entire day every week. The machine gave that day back. It is no coincidence that during these same decades more and more women entered paid employment and then universities. Economists debate which was cause and which effect, but almost all observe the connection between household appliances and women’s employment.

The third pillar was the automobile. Before the war, a car remained a luxury for the well-to-do in Europe; after the war, it became a mass commodity. The Beetle in Germany, the Fiat 500 in Italy, the Citroën 2CV in France, and the Mini in Britain—all these small, inexpensive cars were conceived as vehicles for an ordinary family. In the United States, construction of the Interstate Highway System began in 1956 under a law signed by President Eisenhower; it ultimately extended for more than seventy thousand kilometers. As a young officer in 1919, Eisenhower himself had taken almost two months to cross the country with a military convoy, and later he had seen the German autobahns; he justified the project in part on defense grounds. But these roads served mainly peaceful life: trucks, suburbs, vacations, roadside motels, and diners. The automobile and the road show how the same investment can simultaneously develop and destroy: they expanded freedom of movement and markets, but displaced streetcars in many cities, cut neighborhoods apart with elevated highways, filled the air with exhaust, and bound whole countries to that same cheap oil.

This era also produced inventions that looked far more modest but had consequences no less profound. On April 26, 1956, the *Ideal X*, an old converted tanker carrying fifty-eight steel boxes on its deck, sailed from Port Newark for Houston. Its owner, former truck driver and trucking-company proprietor Malcom McLean, had conceived a simple idea: instead of transferring goods from truck to hold one sack at a time, load the truck body itself onto the ship—a standardized box that could then travel directly from the dock by truck or train. The shipping container was born. In *The Box*, economist Marc Levinson cites a calculation from the period: loading a ton of cargo by conventional means cost almost six dollars, but only about sixteen cents in a container. Within a couple of decades, the tens of thousands of dockworkers who had worked the ports of New York, London, and Liverpool had almost vanished as a profession, while the cost of shipping by sea fell so far that making goods on the other side of the world became profitable. Without this box, there would have been neither the Asian export “miracles” discussed below nor the globalization of the end of the century.

A similar revolution took place in the sky. In 1952, Britain’s Comet became the first jet airliner, and in 1958 the Boeing 707 inaugurated mass transatlantic service. A flight from New York to Paris was cut roughly in half—from long hours in propeller aircraft with intermediate stops to seven or eight hours nonstop. During the 1960s, Mediterranean beaches once visited only by the rich filled with tourists from Manchester and Düsseldorf arriving on charter flights.

Science is a story of its own: during the war, it became an undertaking of the state on an enormous scale for the first time. The Manhattan Project, radar, and the first electronic computers used to calculate artillery trajectories showed governments that investment in research could decide the outcome of a war. In 1945, Vannevar Bush, who had led the government’s Office of Scientific Research and Development during the war, submitted a report to the president with the telling title *Science, the Endless Frontier*. It argued that the state should fund basic science in peacetime as well. From this idea grew the US National Science Foundation and a vast system of university grants. Results soon followed. The transistor was created at Bell Laboratories in 1947; in 1958–1959, Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce independently invented the integrated circuit—a chip on which many transistors are placed on a single piece of semiconductor material. This laid the foundations of the digital economy, the subject of Chapter Sixteen. Medicines also moved from the laboratory to the masses: penicillin, produced during the war primarily for the military, became available to everyone; in 1955, successful US trials of Jonas Salk’s polio vaccine were announced, and by the end of the era the disease, which had recently crippled thousands of children every summer, had become rare in rich countries.

The atom promised even more. In 1954, the first nuclear power station connected to an electrical grid began supplying power at Obninsk near Moscow; in 1956, the first commercial nuclear power station opened at Calder Hall in England. That same year, 1954, Lewis Strauss, chairman of the US Atomic Energy Commission, predicted electricity “too cheap to meter”—although a number of historians believe he meant not uranium reactors but future nuclear fusion. The prediction did not come true: nuclear power proved important but expensive and politically difficult, while its military sibling, the nuclear arsenal, consumed resources without producing anything. The previous chapter discussed the arms race as wasteful spending; here it is enough to recall that much of the era’s “scientific state” was in fact a scientific-military state. Its defenders object that without military orders, the transistor, computer networks, and the jet airliner would not have arrived so quickly. This is a serious argument, but it leaves a question that we will pose again in the chapter on fusion: how many of the same discoveries would have appeared if the same money had gone directly to science, without bombs as the principal product?

Finally, there was the Green Revolution—perhaps the most underrated technological breakthrough of the century. American agronomist Norman Borlaug, working in Mexico under a Rockefeller Foundation program, developed short-stemmed wheat varieties that did not lodge under the weight of the grain and, given sufficient irrigation and fertilizer, produced far larger harvests. His colleagues at the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines introduced IR8, nicknamed “miracle rice,” in 1966. In the mid-1960s, India endured two consecutive droughts and depended on American grain shipments; many experts seriously predicted mass famine in South Asia. Instead, India’s wheat harvest roughly doubled within a few years. Borlaug received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970. Critics of the Green Revolution point to its price: the depletion of groundwater, soil salinization, farmers’ dependence on purchased seed and fertilizer, and the advantage enjoyed by large farms over small ones. But its central achievement is difficult to dispute: in an era when the world’s population was growing faster than ever before, harvests grew faster still, and the Malthusian trap discussed in the opening chapters released its grip for the first time across entire continents.

Technological abundance also had a price, one that was barely noticed at first. In December 1952, London was shrouded for five days in the “Great Smog”—a mixture of fog and coal smoke so thick that people could not see their own feet. Initial official estimates put the death toll at around four thousand; later studies suggest roughly twelve thousand. In 1962, American biologist Rachel Carson showed in *Silent Spring* how DDT, a pesticide that had saved countless people from malaria, accumulated in living organisms and killed birds. These warnings did not yet change the course of the era, but for the first time they raised a question without which the economy of the twenty-first century cannot be understood: some of the wealth of the “Golden Age” was not created but borrowed from nature—from the atmosphere, rivers, and soil—and the bill for that debt had to be paid by later generations.

The section’s conclusion can be put this way. Most of the technologies of the “Golden Age” were not entirely new; what was new was their mass diffusion. Cheap energy, electricity in every home, the automobile, the shipping container, the jet airliner, antibiotics, and new grain varieties made an hour of human labor far more productive than ever before. But technology only creates an opportunity. Whether it became prosperity for the majority depended on how work was organized, who received the profits, and what the state did. Those are the subjects of the sections that follow.

## Organization of Labor

Detroit, May 1950. After lengthy negotiations and the threat of a strike, General Motors and the United Auto Workers, led by Walter Reuther, sign a five-year collective bargaining agreement. A collective agreement is made not with an individual worker but with the entire union at once: it sets wages, shifts, vacations, and dismissal rules for tens of thousands of people. Its two principal formulas had first appeared in the 1948 agreement; in 1950 they were increased and guaranteed for five years, which made the agreement famous. The first was an automatic cost-of-living adjustment: if life became more expensive, wages rose with it, so inflation no longer consumed earnings. The second was an annual “productivity improvement factor”: since the factory produced more cars per worker each year, part of that gain passed into wages every year. In addition, for the first time the company assumed responsibility for pensions and half the cost of health insurance, while the union promised not to strike for five years. The company also assumed responsibility for pensions and health insurance. *Fortune* magazine dubbed the agreement the “Treaty of Detroit,” as though it were a peace treaty between great powers. In a sense, it was: after the decades of bloody strikes described in Chapters Eleven and Twelve, capital and labor signed a truce.

The essence of the truce was simple, and it answers this book’s central question. Workers agreed not to obstruct the company as it modernized its factories, installed new machinery, and sped up the assembly line; not to strike over trifles; and to leave production management to the managers. In return, the company agreed to give workers a steady share of the surplus created by the new machinery. The surplus was divided not at the owner’s whim or through a one-time fight, but according to a rule known in advance. Economic historians call this arrangement “Fordism,” after Henry Ford, who had raised wages at his factories to five dollars a day back in 1914, partly in the expectation that well-paid workers would themselves become buyers of his cars. That was Ford’s own explanation; economists Daniel Raff and Lawrence Summers showed that the desire to curb turnover played no smaller a role: in 1913, several times more people left his factories over the course of the year than worked in them at any one time. After the war, the logic behind one industrialist’s eccentricity became the system of entire countries: mass production requires a mass consumer, and rising wages create that consumer.

By the mid-1950s, roughly one-third of all nonagricultural workers in the United States belonged to unions—more than ever before or since. Europe went further, by different routes. In 1951, West Germany passed a law on “codetermination” (*Mitbestimmung*) in the coal and steel industries: worker representatives received half the seats on the supervisory boards of large companies—the bodies that appoint and oversee executives. A year later, the Works Constitution Act extended more modest rights to almost all enterprises: an elected works council gained a voice in work schedules, dismissals, and workplace rules, while employees in large joint-stock companies received one-third of the seats on the supervisory board. British and American businesspeople regarded this as dangerous socialism. German industrialists initially resisted as well, but over time many acknowledged that workers who were consulted went on strike less often and adopted new equipment more readily. Throughout these years, West Germany had one of the lowest numbers of working days lost to strikes in Western Europe. To be fair, historians debate the direction of causation: whether the reason was codetermination or rapid growth that simply left little cause for conflict.

Sweden built its system on an agreement reached as early as 1938 in the resort town of Saltsjöbaden: the central trade-union confederation and the employers’ association agreed to settle disputes between themselves, without state intervention whenever possible. After the war, trade-union economists Gösta Rehn and Rudolf Meidner proposed an unusual idea, the “solidaristic wage policy”: equal pay for equal work throughout the country, regardless of whether a company was rich or poor. The result was that backward firms unable to pay the common rate went bankrupt, while advanced firms that could afford it easily retained more profit and expanded. Meanwhile, the state did not abandon those who lost their jobs: it paid for their retraining and relocation to places where work was available. A trade union that deliberately accepts the closure of inefficient factories is rare in history. In its own way, the Swedish model solved a problem that had defeated many earlier systems: how to move labor and capital from worse uses to better ones without condemning people to poverty.

Japan organized labor very differently. After the turbulent strikes of the late 1940s—in 1950, a conflict over mass layoffs at Toyota cost company president Kiichiro Toyoda his own post—large Japanese companies arrived at a system later known as the “three treasures.” The first was lifetime employment: a worker was hired straight out of school or university and not dismissed until retirement. The second was seniority-based pay: wages rose with years of service. The third was a union organized not by industry, but separately within each company. An important qualification is needed: lifetime employment applied mainly to men at large corporations—by various estimates, a minority of all Japanese workers. Women, temporary workers, and employees of small suppliers had no such protection. But for those it covered, it changed a great deal. A worker who did not fear losing his job had no reason to hide from management a faster way to do it. A company that could not dismiss an employee had reason to train him, since its investment in his knowledge would remain with the company for thirty years.

On this foundation, Toyota engineer Taiichi Ohno built during the 1950s and 1960s a production system that would later travel the world under the name “lean production.” Its essence was the elimination of waste: excess inventories, unnecessary movement, waiting, and the reworking of defects. Parts were to arrive at the assembly line “just in time”—exactly when needed, signaled by simple *kanban* cards. And any worker who noticed a defect could stop the line by pulling a cord. At an American factory of the period, an ordinary worker stopping the assembly line was almost unthinkable: the line was supposed to keep moving at all times, and defects would be repaired later. Ohno reasoned in the opposite direction: a defect allowed to travel farther down the line cost more than a minute of downtime. Here we should note a point to which this book will return in its final chapters: knowledge of how to improve work resides above all with those who do it. A system that gives this knowledge a path upward outperforms one that relies only on instructions from above—a lesson the Soviet planned economy of the previous chapter never learned.

The era’s greatest shift, however, concerned not how people worked in factories, but where those people came from. The “Golden Age” marked the last great exodus from the countryside in the rich world. In France, as we saw in Fourastié’s village, about one-third of workers were employed in agriculture in 1946; by the mid-1970s, the share was around one-tenth. In Italy, millions of peasants left the poor South for the factories of Turin and Milan; in Japan, the sons of rice farmers became workers at Toyota and Matsushita. Economists see this transfer as one of the chief causes of rapid growth: a worker moving from a tiny plot, where an extra pair of hands added almost nothing to the harvest, to a machine powered by an electric motor immediately produced several times more. Economist Arthur Lewis, later a Nobel laureate, described this mechanism in 1954: as long as there are “surplus” hands in the countryside, industry can grow without sharply raising wages. Once the reserve is exhausted, growth slows. A number of researchers—among them Charles Kindleberger in *Europe’s Postwar Growth: The Role of Labor Supply* (1967)—see the exhaustion of this reserve as one reason the miracle ended.

When domestic peasants were no longer enough, workers were sought abroad. Beginning in 1955, West Germany signed labor-recruitment agreements—first with Italy, then with Spain, Greece, Turkey in 1961, and Yugoslavia. The recruits were called *Gastarbeiter*, or “guest workers”: they were expected to work for several years and then return home. Many stayed, and Germany is still grappling with the question of how a society that invited people as labor power should live with them as fellow citizens. Swiss writer Max Frisch captured this in a famous phrase: “We called for workers, but people came.” France invited workers from Portugal and its former colonies in North Africa, while Britain drew them from the Caribbean and South Asia. Much of the hard, dirty work on the construction sites and in the mines and foundries of the “Golden Age” was done by people who were paid less than native workers and had almost no political voice.

Another change concerned women. The war brought them into factories on a mass scale for the first time—the American poster of “Rosie the Riveter” became an emblem of the era—but after victory many were returned to the home, and the 1950s in the United States and Western Europe became the decade of the ideal “wife at home, husband at work.” The ideal did not last long. As we saw in the previous section, household appliances freed time; education expanded; and the service sector—offices, shops, hospitals, schools—needed ever more workers. By the early 1970s, the share of women in employment had risen markedly in most rich countries, although they were generally paid considerably less than men for comparable work. The United States did not pass the Equal Pay Act until 1963, and the gap disappeared neither then nor later.

Finally, Fordism had its own shadow, and toward the end of the era people began to speak of it loudly. Assembly-line work paid well, but its monotony was mind-numbing: the same operation hundreds of times per shift, the pace set by a machine, a foreman watching every movement. In *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), American sociologist Harry Braverman argued that scientific management systematically stripped workers of knowledge and skill, turning skilled labor into mechanical execution. His critics replied that the number of technicians, engineers, and office workers was growing at the same time, and the average skill level in the economy was rising. But the discontent was real. In May 1968, after the student unrest in France, an estimated seven to ten million people went on strike—the largest strike in the country’s history. The Grenelle Agreements, negotiated at the Ministry of Labor on Rue de Grenelle, raised the minimum wage by roughly one-third. In 1972, workers at General Motors’ new and most modern plant in Lordstown, Ohio—most of them young—went on strike not so much over money as over the exhausting pace and meaninglessness of the work. Journalists began to speak of the “blue-collar blues.”

Such was the labor order of the “Golden Age.” Surplus was divided according to rules negotiated by organized workers and employers under state supervision—and for that very reason it went not only to owners, but to the majority. This is the crucial difference from the First Industrial Revolution, when, as Chapter Ten showed, real wages lagged behind productivity growth for decades. But the bargain bought peace at the price of dependence: workers received more money, but no more control over what they did or how they did it, and no greater meaning in their working day. Those excluded from the bargain—migrants, women, workers at small firms—received less. And the bargain itself depended on rapid growth: as long as the pie grew every year, it was easy to divide. When growth stopped in the 1970s, the truce began to break down—but that is the story of the next chapter.

## Exchange and Money

Let us return to the Mount Washington Hotel. The dispute between Keynes and White was a dispute over who would pay for the failures of world trade. Representing a debtor country, Keynes proposed an international clearing bank with its own unit of account, which he called the “bancor,” and penalties not only for countries that bought too much from others, but also for those that sold too much and accumulated other countries’ money. White, representing the world’s largest creditor, rejected the idea: the United States had no intention of paying a penalty for its own competitiveness. The American plan prevailed, unsurprisingly: in 1944, the United States held about 60 percent of the world’s monetary gold, and by 1949 nearly 70 percent. Yet the American plan also incorporated the central lesson of the 1930s: money must not again become a weapon.

The arrangement that became known as the Bretton Woods system was simple. The dollar was pegged to gold: the US Treasury pledged to exchange dollars accumulated by foreign central banks for gold at thirty-five dollars per troy ounce—the same price Roosevelt had set in 1934. Other currencies were pegged to the dollar at fixed rates. The pound initially stood at $4.03 and, after the 1949 devaluation, at $2.80; from that same year, the Japanese yen stood at 360 to the dollar, a rate that lasted for more than twenty years. Devaluation—the official reduction in the value of one’s currency—was permitted, but only in the event of a “fundamental disequilibrium” and with the agreement of the new International Monetary Fund. The Fund was to make temporary loans to countries that ran out of foreign currency, so that restoring balance would not require them to wreck their own economies, as under the gold standard. The World Bank—officially the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development—provided long-term loans first for reconstruction and later for development.

The system’s most important feature looks almost heretical today. Bretton Woods permitted and even encouraged controls on capital movements. In other words, a government could prohibit or restrict its citizens and banks from freely moving money abroad. Trade in goods was welcome; shifting millions from country to country in pursuit of higher interest or out of fear of devaluation was not. Keynes explained to the House of Lords that this was not a concession but the essence of the design: without such controls, a country could not simultaneously maintain a firm exchange rate and fight unemployment on its own terms. Economists later called this the “impossible trinity”: a country cannot have a fixed exchange rate, free movement of capital, and an independent interest-rate policy all at once—it must choose two of the three. Bretton Woods chose the exchange rate and policy autonomy, sacrificing the freedom of capital. Under the law, a Briton going on holiday abroad in the second half of the 1960s could take no more than fifty pounds out of the country.

For the question posed by this book, that decision was enormously important. If money could not leave the country, then most of the surplus saved by its residents remained at home—in local banks, savings banks, and insurance companies, and through them in local factories, housing, and government bonds. A German worker’s savings became a loan to a German factory instead of flowing to wherever interest rates were higher. This restricted freedom, and sometimes efficiency, but directed the flow toward the machinery, roads, and houses waiting for it.

On paper, the system did not begin working immediately. In 1947, Europe was suffocating from a “dollar shortage.” It needed everything—grain, coal, machinery, fertilizer—and almost all of it could be bought only in America and only with dollars, which Europeans did not have. In the summer of 1947, as a condition of an American loan, Britain made the pound freely convertible into dollars—and lost so many reserves in five weeks that convertibility had to be suspended. The answer was the Marshall Plan. On June 5, 1947, in a brief speech at Harvard, US Secretary of State George Marshall proposed that the Europeans draw up a common reconstruction program and that America pay for it. From 1948 through 1951, the program sent about thirteen billion dollars to more than sixteen Western European countries—by some estimates, roughly one percent of American national income per year at the time.

The scale of the aid should not be exaggerated. In their well-known 1993 paper “The Marshall Plan: History’s Most Successful Structural Adjustment Program,” economists Bradford DeLong and Barry Eichengreen showed that the money’s direct contribution to European investment was modest—several percent of recipients’ GDP. Something else mattered more. First, the aid provided dollars for precisely the bottlenecks: fuel, animal feed, and raw materials without which factories stood idle. Second, the aid came with conditions: remove trade barriers among the European countries themselves, stabilize currencies, and balance budgets. The money was not simply handed to governments. A European importer paid for an American product in domestic currency into a special “counterpart” account, and those funds were then directed, in agreement with the Americans, toward reconstruction—in West Germany, for example, toward power stations and coal mines. Third, the Marshall Plan was also a political bargain: countries that accepted aid remained in the Western camp. The Soviet Union refused to participate and compelled Poland and Czechoslovakia to refuse as well—and the boundary between recipients and nonrecipients became the boundary of the “Iron Curtain.”

The most dramatic episode in the monetary history of the era took place in West Germany. By 1948, the Reichsmark was dead: prices had remained officially frozen since the Nazi period, but no goods were available in shops at those prices. Farmers held back produce, while workers left their shifts to barter possessions for food. On June 20, 1948, a currency reform was carried out in the three western occupation zones: every resident received forty new Deutsche Marks in cash—and later another twenty—while old savings and debts were converted at, roughly speaking, ten to one, with many deposits treated even more harshly. Almost simultaneously, Ludwig Erhard, director of the economic administration of the western zones, abolished most price controls, largely on his own initiative and at his own risk. Eyewitnesses recalled that within a day or two, shop windows empty only the day before filled with goods: sellers stopped hiding them because the new money was once again worth something. The scene entered textbooks as a symbol of the power of sound money and free prices.

Here too, however, the history is more complicated than the legend. Prices rose after controls were lifted, unemployment surged in 1949–1950, and in November 1948 the unions called a general strike against the high cost of living. The reform hit small savers hard: their savings almost disappeared, while owners of houses, machinery, and land retained their assets. To mitigate this injustice, West Germany passed the 1952 Equalization of Burdens Act (*Lastenausgleich*): owners of surviving property paid a special levy for the benefit of refugees and victims of bombing—a transfer spread over decades of part of the wealth of those who had been fortunate to those who had lost everything. The German miracle was not only about the market, but also about this agreement on how to share the losses.

The next task was to revive trade within Europe itself. In the late 1940s, European countries still traded with one another mainly through bilateral agreements, almost like barter: France could sell Belgium roughly as much as it bought from Belgium, because their currencies were not convertible. In 1950, with support from the Marshall Plan, the European Payments Union was created—a clearinghouse in which countries’ mutual debts were reduced to a single net balance. France no longer had to balance its trade with each country separately; it was enough not to run too large an overall deficit. This modest accounting invention, reminiscent of the settlement of debts at the Champagne fairs in Chapter Seven, liberated trade, and at the end of 1958 the principal Western European currencies once again became freely convertible for trade transactions.

At the global level, trade rules were established by the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade—GATT—signed in Geneva by twenty-three countries in 1947. A full-fledged International Trade Organization had been planned, but the US Congress never ratified its charter, and the “temporary” agreement survived for almost half a century, until the World Trade Organization was created in 1995. GATT’s central principle was “most-favored-nation treatment”: a concession made to one participating country was extended to all. Tariffs were lowered in rounds of negotiations; the largest of the era, the Kennedy Round of 1964–1967, reduced tariffs on manufactured goods by roughly one-third. In 1957, six countries—France, West Germany, Italy, Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg—signed the Treaty of Rome establishing the European Economic Community, and by 1968 tariffs among them had been abolished entirely. According to Maddison’s estimates, world trade grew by almost eight percent a year between 1950 and 1973—markedly faster than production itself. Chapter Twelve showed how trade contracted during the 1930s, taking the division of labor and productivity down with it. Now the flywheel was turning in the opposite direction.

Postwar money also had a less visible side, later termed “financial repression” by economists Carmen Reinhart and Belen Sbrancia. After the war, governments carried enormous debts: Britain’s exceeded 200 percent of its annual GDP, while the US debt approached 120 percent. Neither country defaulted or unleashed hyperinflation. The debts melted away quietly. Governments imposed ceilings on interest rates—in the United States, the interest paid on bank deposits was restricted by law—required banks and insurance companies to hold government bonds, and prevented capital from fleeing abroad. Inflation was moderate, but bond yields were often lower still. Depositors therefore lost a little purchasing power each year, while the real burden of public debt declined. This was a hidden tax on savings, paid above all by bondholders and depositors. Reinhart and Sbrancia estimate that this “liquidation” of debt yielded the United States and Britain an average of several percent of GDP a year. Defenders of the policy argue that it was the least painful way available to pay for the war; critics say that it distorted the allocation of credit and punished thrift. But for our question, what matters is this: almost none of the “Golden Age” surplus went toward servicing old war debts—they were dissolved by growth and moderate inflation.

Private bankers in this world were rather dull people. American banking in those years was described by the joking “3-6-3 rule”: take deposits at three percent, lend at six percent, and be on the golf course by three in the afternoon. Banking crises were almost nonexistent in rich countries—Michael Bordo and Barry Eichengreen note that the Bretton Woods era was the calmest period in the financial history of the past two centuries. Modern consumer credit, however, was born. The Diners Club card appeared in New York in 1950—according to legend, after its founder Frank McNamara forgot his wallet at a restaurant. In 1958, Bank of America mailed tens of thousands of unsolicited credit cards to residents of Fresno, California. Buying a home, a car, or a refrigerator on installment became normal for American and later European families. Credit made it possible to obtain a benefit now at the expense of future wages—and as long as wages kept rising, the bargain looked safe.

Belgian-American economist Robert Triffin identified the system’s weak point as early as 1960. Growing world trade needed ever more dollars. The world could obtain them only if the United States spent more abroad than it earned—on troops in Europe and Asia, aid, and purchases of foreign companies and goods. But the more dollars accumulated in foreign hands, the less confidence there was that America could exchange all of them for gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce. This contradiction became known as the “Triffin dilemma”: the system could either suffer from a shortage of money or undermine confidence in its center. French finance minister Valéry Giscard d’Estaing called the dollar’s position an “exorbitant privilege”—America could buy the world with money it issued itself. In 1965, General de Gaulle demonstratively began exchanging France’s dollars for gold. The Vietnam War and the expansion of American social programs increased the flow of dollars, and in 1971, as the next chapter will relate, the “gold window” slammed shut.

The conclusion of this section is as follows. The money of the “Golden Age” was organized to serve production rather than speculation: fixed exchange rates, restricted capital movements, cheap credit for industry and the state, and moderate inflation that slowly dissolved war debts. This arrangement kept surplus within national economies and directed it toward investment. But it had a price—hidden losses for savers and the special position of the one country that issued the world’s money—and a built-in contradiction that sooner or later had to blow it apart. Most of humanity also remained outside it: countries selling raw materials at prices that, as Argentine economist Raúl Prebisch and British economist Hans Singer argued in 1950, declined over the long run relative to the prices of manufactured goods. From this observation grew the policy of import substitution, whose fate will be discussed in the sections on what fostered development and what was destructive.

## Power and Institutions

London, December 1, 1942. The bombing has not yet ended, and British troops have only just won the Battle of El Alamein, but lines form outside bookshops for a government report with the dry title *Social Insurance and Allied Services*. Its author, economist and civil servant William Beveridge, promises to defeat the “five giants” standing in the way of a better life: Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor, and Idleness—the last his name for involuntary unemployment. By widely cited estimates, the report sells hundreds of thousands of copies, an unheard-of circulation for a government document. Soldiers at the front discuss it in their barracks. In July 1945, even before Japan’s surrender, British voters remove Churchill—the man who led them to victory—from office and give a majority to Labour, which has promised to put the report into practice. On July 5, 1948, the National Health Service begins operating: doctors and hospitals become free to every resident, funded through taxation. Health Minister Aneurin Bevan, the son of a Welsh miner, says that illness should be neither a luxury one pays for nor a disgrace for which one apologizes.

Thus a new figure moved to the center of power in the “Golden Age”: the welfare state. A “welfare state” or “social state” is one that guarantees basic benefits—pensions, health care, unemployment and sickness benefits, education—to all citizens, not only to the poor and not as charity. We saw its beginnings in Chapter Eleven: Bismarck’s worker insurance programs in the 1880s. But those were partial programs for particular groups. After the war, they grew into a system in almost every rich country. France created a universal social-security system in 1945; in Sweden, the Social Democrats, who governed without interruption from 1932 to 1976, built perhaps its most comprehensive form. In the United States, where the idea of universal health care never prevailed, Medicare and Medicaid—public health insurance for the elderly and the poor—were introduced in 1965. Economists Vito Tanzi and Ludger Schuknecht estimate in *Public Spending in the 20th Century* (2000) that the governments of rich countries redistributed on average about one-tenth of national income in 1913, but by the mid-1970s well over one-third, and in Sweden and the Netherlands about half.

Why then? Partly because of the war itself. The total war discussed in Chapter Twelve demanded sacrifices from everyone and therefore created a sense that the fruits of victory should belong to everyone as well. Partly because of fear. Memories of the 1930s, when unemployment and poverty drove millions toward fascism and communism, were fresh, while beyond the “Iron Curtain” stood a rival promising workers everything. And partly because of a new economic theory. Keynes, discussed at length in Chapter Twelve, had proved—or at least persuaded most politicians—that the state not only could but should support demand and full employment. In a 1944 White Paper, the British government formally accepted responsibility for maintaining a “high and stable level of employment”; the US Employment Act of 1946 set a similar goal. A government answerable to voters for work for all was a new phenomenon in the history of power. As recently as the 1920s, unemployment had been regarded as something like bad weather.

Here we should pause over the question most important to this book: is the welfare state developmental, wasteful, or both? From the beginning, its critics argued that redistribution creates no new benefits, but merely shifts them from one pocket to another, taking away people’s incentive to work and save along the way. As early as 1944, Austrian economist Friedrich Hayek warned in *The Road to Serfdom* that a state that undertook to plan welfare would inevitably begin planning its citizens’ lives as well. In 1947, he gathered like-minded thinkers in the Swiss village of Mont Pèlerin. Their society seemed at the time like a circle of eccentrics, but thirty years later its ideas would change the world—the subject of the next chapter. Defenders replied, and the era’s experience gave them serious arguments: much of this “redistribution” was in fact investment. A free school or university is an investment in knowledge that pays returns in productivity for decades. A healthy worker is one who works instead of lying in a hospital or dying at forty. A pension frees the young from supporting the old and allows them to move where jobs are available. Fear of poverty, as the Swedish retraining example showed, keeps people from accepting change; protection from poverty makes change acceptable. The line between developmental and wasteful runs here not between “market” and “state,” but within each program: does it expand human capabilities, or merely preserve them as they are?

Education offers the best example of investment. In 1944, the US Congress passed the Servicemen’s Readjustment Act, known as the G.I. Bill: soldiers returning from the war received funding for college or vocational school, low-interest mortgage loans, and benefits. According to the US Department of Veterans Affairs, about two million veterans used it to attend college, and several million more for vocational training. The sons of farmers and workers who had never considered university before the war became engineers, teachers, and doctors. In Europe, compulsory schooling was extended—to age fifteen in Britain from 1947, and to age sixteen in France under a 1959 reform that came fully into force by 1967—while during the 1960s universities opened their doors to children from families in which no one had ever studied beyond school. In many countries, student numbers multiplied several times over those two decades. Economists Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz argue in *The Race between Education and Technology* (2008) that the twentieth century became the “American century” above all because America was the first to make first secondary and then higher education available on a mass scale. But here too there was a shadow: local authorities and banks administered G.I. Bill benefits, and in the South Black veterans were often unable to use them—they were admitted neither to “white” colleges nor to the banks that issued mortgages.

How was all this paid for? Through taxes, at levels difficult to imagine today. In the United States, the top marginal income-tax rate—the rate levied on every dollar of income above a very high threshold—stood at 91–92 percent from 1951 through 1963, and even after the Kennedy–Johnson tax reform it was still 70 percent. In Britain, the highest rate on large incomes remained around 90 percent, and from 1974 through 1979 the rate on investment income, including a special surcharge, reached 98 percent. In practice, few people paid these rates: the rich found loopholes, and the threshold was so high that only a tiny number were affected. But the system’s point lay elsewhere: it made the pursuit of extremely high incomes almost meaningless. In *Capital in the Twenty-First Century*, Thomas Piketty shows that the share of national income received by the richest one percent in the United States and Europe was lower in these decades than at any other time in the century. He emphasizes that this compression of inequality was due less to the market than to wars, which destroyed old fortunes, and tax policy, which prevented their rapid restoration. Claudia Goldin and Robert Margo add another cause: during these same decades, mass education sharply increased the number of skilled workers, reducing their wage premium. Historians still debate which mattered more, taxes or schools. For our question, the implication is this: a smaller share of surplus settled into enormous private fortunes, while more went into wages and taxes, and through taxes into schools and roads. Critics object that high taxes stifled entrepreneurship; supporters reply that the era of the highest taxes coincided with the fastest growth. The honest answer is that neither fact by itself proves either claim—rapid growth had many causes at once.

The organization of power varied greatly among the countries of the “Golden Age,” and this matters: there was no single recipe. France chose “indicative planning.” In 1946, businessman and diplomat Jean Monnet became head of the General Planning Commission. Unlike the Soviet Gosplan of the previous chapter, the French plan did not order factories how much to produce. It brought officials, businesspeople, bankers, and trade unionists to the same table, secured agreement on which industries should be developed first—coal, steel, electricity, transport—and directed cheap state credit toward them. The state also took ownership of part of industry: coal mines, electric power—through EDF, created in 1946—the largest banks, and the Renault automobile works, confiscated for collaboration with the occupiers. Under Labour, Britain also nationalized coal, railways, electricity, and steel, but without the French planning system. In the view of many historians, its state-owned industries eventually became loss-making institutions rather than engines of growth. Economist Richard Pryke, however, showed in 1971 that their productivity during the 1960s grew no more slowly than in private industry—although a decade later he himself revised his conclusion in a less favorable direction.

West Germany chose what appeared to be the opposite: the “social market economy.” The term was coined by economist Alfred Müller-Armack, while its foundations were laid by scholars of the so-called Freiburg School, led by Walter Eucken, who were known as “ordoliberals,” from the Latin *ordo*, meaning order. Their idea was not that the state should step aside, but that it should strictly enforce the rules of the game: sound money, free competition, and a ban on cartels. The ordoliberals had seen cartels and monopolies merge with the Nazi state in the 1930s and regarded the concentration of economic power as no less dangerous than the concentration of political power. In 1957, West Germany adopted the Act Against Restraints of Competition and created an independent Bundesbank, charged above all with preserving the value of the mark. Burned by two inflations in a single generation, Germany entrusted money not to the government, but to people the government could not dismiss. And alongside the market stood the same institutions already described: worker codetermination, the equalization of burdens, and a generous pension system.

Japan built a third model, which American political scientist Chalmers Johnson called the “developmental state” in *MITI and the Japanese Miracle* (1982). The Ministry of International Trade and Industry—MITI—created in 1949, did not own factories, but determined which industries would be strategic: first steel, shipbuilding, and chemicals, then automobiles and electronics. They received cheap credit, access to scarce foreign currency for purchasing foreign licenses, and protection from imports. The companies themselves nevertheless competed fiercely with one another—and, crucially, on world markets, where no one protected them. Johnson saw MITI as the brain of the miracle. His critics, including economist Richard Katz, point out that the ministry repeatedly made mistakes: according to a well-known account, it tried to dissuade Honda from producing automobiles and long resisted Sony’s development, while Japan’s best companies grew in large part despite its advice. The truth probably lies somewhere in between, and we will return to it in the section on what fostered development.

The deepest institutional change in Japan, however, took place under the American occupation, and it directly answers the question of where surplus goes. The land reform of 1946–1950 bought land from absentee landlords at prices that inflation quickly eroded and transferred it to those who cultivated it. By widely accepted estimates, roughly one-third of all the country’s arable land passed through the state’s hands. Before the reform, a tenant surrendered about half the harvest to the owner as rent—rent in its purest form, surplus flowing from the person who worked to the person who owned. After the reform, this surplus remained with the farmer, who began investing it in fertilizer, small machinery, and his children’s education. South Korea (1950) and Taiwan (1949–1953) carried out similar reforms. In *How Asia Works* (2013), journalist and researcher Joe Studwell considers them the first and necessary step in the Asian “miracles,” and the absence of such reforms one reason the Philippines and most of Latin America did not follow the same path. Economist Dani Rodrik reaches a similar conclusion: the relative equality of land and income distribution in Korea and Taiwan was an initial condition of their growth. Land reform, however, is necessary but not sufficient: Mexico and Bolivia carried it out, but no Asian-style leap followed. During the same years, the occupation authorities dissolved the *zaibatsu*—the giant family conglomerates Mitsui, Mitsubishi, and Sumitomo, closely tied to the war machine. Before long, however, they partly reassembled as looser groups, or *keiretsu*, centered on large banks.

This Japanese example leads to one of the most interesting explanations of the miracle—and one of the most unsettling. In *The Rise and Decline of Nations* (1982), American economist Mancur Olson noticed something strange: after the war, the defeated countries—Germany, Japan, and Italy—grew fastest, while among the rich countries victorious Britain grew slowest. His explanation was that in a stable society, “distributional coalitions” accumulate over time—cartels, professional guilds, lobbies, and privileged groups concerned not with enlarging the overall pie but with grabbing a larger slice for themselves. They impede change because every change threatens someone’s rent. War, occupation, and the collapse of regimes in Germany and Japan swept these coalitions away, while in Britain, which had experienced neither invasion nor revolution, they remained—from craft unions defending obsolete trades to old clubs of industrialists. Olson was not advocating war; he was showing that societies pay a high price for accumulated rent and that clearing it away without upheaval is difficult. Readers of this book will recognize a familiar idea: back in Chapter 7, we saw how the Black Death broke the serf order and raised the price of labor. Historians dispute Olson’s account—Britain lagged for other reasons as well—but the question he posed—how can a society rid itself of rent without catastrophe?—remains one of the central questions in economics.

Finally, the power structure of the “Golden Age” had its own underside. The social bargain did not extend to everyone. In the American South, until the civil-rights laws of 1964 and 1965, Black citizens were effectively unable to vote, while segregation shut them out of the best schools, jobs, and mortgage loans; for decades, the celebrated mortgage-insurance program that built the Levittown suburbs denied loans to residents of “colored” neighborhoods. In the early postwar years, Senator McCarthy’s “witch hunt” cost thousands of Americans their jobs on suspicion of communist sympathies. France, while building a welfare state at home, fought brutal colonial wars in Indochina and Algeria. Dictatorships survived in Spain and Portugal until the mid-1970s. Power in Western Europe, North America, and Japan became markedly more inclusive—more open to broad participation, to use the terms of Acemoglu and Robinson from Chapter 9—than ever before. But inclusion advanced unevenly and often stopped at the boundary beyond which began the “others.”

To sum up: the institutions of the “Golden Age” varied greatly—British nationalization and German ordoliberalism, the French plan and the Japanese ministry, the Swedish compact between unions and employers—but they shared several features. Almost everywhere, those in power were accountable to voters for the employment and well-being of the majority. Almost everywhere, taxes and rules prevented surplus from accumulating in enormous private fortunes and directed it into education, health, housing, and infrastructure. Almost everywhere, old rents—landlord, cartel, and military-aristocratic—had been broken by war, reform, or law. And almost everywhere, this rested on a broad agreement that would later be called the “postwar consensus.” Like any agreement, it endured as long as every side received more than it gave up. In the following sections, we will examine exactly which mechanisms turned these institutions into growth, and where they themselves began to generate new rent.

## What Fostered Development

Seoul, early 1960s. South Korea is one of the poorest countries in the world: after the Korean War of 1950–1953, its industry lies in ruins, almost all the heavy industry built by the Japanese in the colonial era has remained in the communist North, and the budget depends on American aid. Political scientist Samuel Huntington later recalled that in the early 1960s Korea stood roughly level with Ghana in income per person, and few would then have bet on Korea. General Park Chung Hee, who seized power in 1961—a dictator who jailed his opponents—makes a bet that few at the time considered sensible: a country without oil and without significant reserves of coal or iron ore must make its living by selling its goods abroad. Every month, Park personally chairs an export meeting at which ministers and company owners report on their sales targets, while the best exporters receive cheap credit, tax breaks, and honors. According to official statistics, Korean exports amounted to about thirty million dollars in 1960; by 1977, they had passed ten billion. During the same decades, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Singapore made a similar turn. They would become known as the “Asian Tigers.”

Korea illustrates several mechanisms of growth, but its experience cannot be considered representative of every country of the era. Let us examine them one by one.

The first measurable indicator is gross investment as a share of national income; this is not the share of all surplus directed toward development. In the 1960s, Japan devoted about a third of its national income to new factories, machinery, roads, and homes; West Germany and most of Western Europe devoted about a quarter. For comparison, in Britain during the first Industrial Revolution, according to historian Charles Feinstein’s estimates, this share rose from six to eight percent at the beginning to ten or eleven percent by the 1830s. The money did not come from nowhere. Japanese households saved unusually large amounts—partly because pensions were modest and people had to pay for major purchases themselves. The Bretton Woods controls on capital discussed above prevented these savings from flowing abroad, while banks under state supervision directed them into industry. High taxes on very large personal incomes made reinvesting profits in a firm more attractive than consuming them. Here the thesis of this book can be seen with almost laboratory clarity: there was a surplus, and it went into machinery.

The second mechanism was borrowing knowledge. A country that is behind does not need to reinvent the transistor or the assembly line: it can buy a license, send engineers abroad to study, or carefully take apart someone else’s model. In 1953, a small Tokyo firm called Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, the future Sony, bought a transistor license from the American company Western Electric for twenty-five thousand dollars, according to widely cited accounts—and had to obtain permission from MITI to remit the money. The Americans saw the transistor primarily as a component for hearing aids and military equipment; within a few years, Japanese pocket transistor radios were being sold around the world. In 1950, at the invitation of the Union of Japanese Scientists and Engineers, American statistician W. Edwards Deming came to Japan. He explained to managers that defects should be sought not at the factory gate but within the production process itself—and the Japanese listened to him more attentively than his compatriots did. The Deming Prize for quality was established in 1951; twenty years later, “Made in Japan,” which only recently had meant “cheap and unreliable,” had come to mean the opposite. Europe did the same. From 1948 to 1952, under the Marshall Plan, dozens of British “productivity teams”—workers, foremen, managers, and trade unionists—toured American factories and published reports on what they saw. This reminds us of a simple fact that is easy to forget: knowledge of how to make something is just as much capital as the machine itself, but unlike a machine, it can be copied almost for free.

The third mechanism was people who knew how to use this knowledge. Germany and Japan emerged from the war devastated, but with educated populations: literate workers, experienced foremen, and engineers. Many economists believe this is precisely why recovery proceeded so quickly: a machine can be bought in a year, but it takes twenty years to raise a worker who understands it. In the 1950s, despite being poor countries, Korea and Taiwan invested surprisingly heavily in schools, and by the early 1970s had achieved nearly universal literacy and mass secondary education. In the rich countries, as we saw in the previous section, universities threw open their doors. Investment in education pays off neither this year nor the next—which is why it is so easy to cut. But the “Golden Age” showed that it was precisely this investment that determined whether technologies bought abroad would work at home.

The fourth mechanism was the open market as an examination. Here it is time to fulfill the promise made in the previous section and return to the dispute over Japan’s ministry. The state in Japan, Korea, and Taiwan did indeed select industries and give them credit and protection. But many countries in Latin America, Africa, and South Asia did the same—with far worse results. According to Joe Studwell and economist Alice Amsden, whose *Asia’s Next Giant* (1989) examined Korea, the difference was that Asian governments demanded results in return for their help, and exports were the measure of those results. A firm that received cheap credit had to sell its goods on the world market, where no tariff protected it and where a buyer in California or Hamburg ruthlessly compared it with the best. If it failed, its support was cut. Amsden called this “discipline”: the state did not simply hand out benefits but also punished failure. Exports were a test that was difficult to fake: one could report to one’s superiors that the plan had been fulfilled, but not to a foreign buyer. The same principle, without the involvement of officials, operated in Europe: as borders opened within the Common Market, French, Italian, and German firms faced direct competition for the first time in decades, and the weak had either to restructure or leave the market.

Taiwan gave this principle a simple physical embodiment. In 1966, one of the world’s first export-processing zones opened in the port city of Kaohsiung—a fenced-off area where companies could import components duty-free on condition that the finished product was exported in its entirety. Wages, skills, and management experience remained within the country. Singapore, after its unexpected independence in 1965—a tiny island with no raw materials of its own and not even a reliable source of fresh water—went further still: through the Economic Development Board, Lee Kuan Yew’s government courted large foreign corporations, promising order, stable rules, trained workers, and a determined fight against corruption. The wager was that investor confidence was itself a form of capital and could be earned if the rules did not change at an official’s whim. Yet the same Singapore was a state with limited political freedom—we will return to this ambiguity of the Asian “miracles.”

The fifth mechanism was a Europe that had stopped waging war against itself. On May 9, 1950, French foreign minister Robert Schuman, acting on Jean Monnet’s idea, proposed placing the coal and steel industries of France and West Germany under joint management so that war between them would become not only unthinkable but materially impossible. In 1951, six countries created the European Coal and Steel Community—the embryo of the future European Union. Coal and steel were the raw materials of war: the Ruhr and Saar had been fought over and occupied, and their output used to pay reparations. Now they became the raw materials of a common market. Within the framework of this book, this is perhaps the clearest example of transformation: a resource that had fueled destruction for half a century became developmental. Preparing for war against one another—one of the largest items of expenditure for European states in previous centuries—disappeared from their relations, freeing the surplus once spent on fortresses and armies aimed at a neighbor for other uses. A qualification is necessary, however: it was not freed in full, because the Cold War and its new arms race took the place of the old enmity.

The sixth mechanism was an arrangement for sharing the gains that gave the majority a stake in growth. We discussed it in detail in the sections on labor and power: a worker received part of the gains from productivity and therefore did not resist new machinery; after land reform, a farmer kept the harvest and therefore invested in the land; a worker dismissed when a factory closed received benefits and retraining and therefore did not cling to the old job. Barry Eichengreen sees this as a bargain extended over time: workers accepted restrained wage growth today so that firms would invest their profits, while firms invested because they trusted that tomorrow’s wage demands would not consume the entire return. Such a bargain required trust—the same thing that, as we saw in the chapters on medieval fairs and the Dutch stock exchange, lowers the cost of every exchange. Without it, each side tries to seize its share immediately, before someone else takes it, and nothing remains for the future.

The list of developmental forces also requires an honest qualification. In 1994, American economist Paul Krugman published a provocatively titled article, “The Myth of Asia’s Miracle,” drawing on Alvin Young’s calculations. Their conclusion was that the growth of the “tigers,” especially Singapore, was explained mainly not by rising efficiency but by accumulation: more people went to work, more people became educated, and more money was invested in machinery. According to Krugman, growth of this kind eventually hits a ceiling, just as the growth of the Soviet Union in the previous chapter did. The debate continues: calculating differently—from movements in wages and returns on capital—economist Chang-Tai Hsieh found a notably larger contribution from efficiency growth in Singapore. But even if Krugman and Young are right, the lesson remains: turning the savings of a poor country into machinery and schools rather than palaces and Swiss bank accounts is an achievement that most countries in history have not managed.

Taken together, the answer to the question “where did the surplus go?” in the successful countries of the “Golden Age” is this: into machines, knowledge, and people—through rules for sharing the gains that made every participant want the pie to grow. None of these mechanisms was invented in this era. Seventeenth-century Holland had known high investment, nineteenth-century Germany and America had borrowed foreign technologies, and Britain after the union with Scotland had enjoyed the benefits of a large market without internal tariffs. What was new was that they worked simultaneously for the first time in several dozen countries—and in Western Europe, North America, and Japan, under democratic control over who received the fruits as well. The next section will examine where the same mechanisms failed and what was nevertheless destroyed in the process.

## What Destroyed

Punjab, August 1947. British India gains independence and on the same day ceases to exist as a single whole: it is divided into India and Pakistan. The boundary that British lawyer Cyril Radcliffe drew in five weeks, without ever having visited the region, runs straight through the provinces of Punjab and Bengal. Millions of Muslims flee west and east, while millions of Hindus and Sikhs flee in the opposite direction. Trains arrive at stations packed with the dead. No one knows exactly how many people died: historians’ estimates range from two hundred thousand to two million, while about fifteen million people were uprooted, according to a widely accepted estimate. Historian Yasmin Khan describes the events in detail in *The Great Partition* (2007).

For our framework, it also matters that an economy was cut apart. Punjab’s canals, built under British rule, drew water from rivers whose upper reaches ended up in India while the fields lay in Pakistan; in April 1948, the Indian side temporarily cut off the water. Calcutta’s jute mills remained in India, while the fields where the jute grew were in East Bengal, which became part of Pakistan. Connections built up over a century were severed in a matter of weeks. This story also has a rare hopeful sequel: in 1960, with the World Bank acting as mediator, the two countries signed the Indus Waters Treaty, which continued to operate for decades despite the wars between them. Even the bitterest neighbors can reach agreement over something without which neither can survive.

This is the central paradox of the section. The “Golden Age” really was the period when, for the first time in thirty years, the rich countries stopped destroying one another. But wars continued, including in Korea, Indochina, and Algeria. They destroyed lives and economies in the countries they touched; what share of the world’s surplus this represented has not been calculated here.

The Korean War of 1950–1953 was the first “hot” war of the Cold War. Seoul changed hands four times. Estimates place the death toll between two and a half million and more than three million people, most of them civilians. According to historian Bruce Cumings in *The Korean War* (2010), American aircraft dropped more bombs on Korea than on the entire Pacific theater during the Second World War; by the war’s end, almost no intact cities remained in North Korea. Ten years later, destruction on an even greater scale came to Indochina: by widely cited estimates, more bombs were dropped on Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia from 1964 to 1973 than all sides dropped during the entire Second World War. During Operation Ranch Hand, American aircraft sprayed tens of millions of liters of herbicides over the forests and fields of South Vietnam, including Agent Orange, which contained dioxin. Estimates of deaths in the Vietnam War range from one million to more than three million. Unexploded ordnance in Laos continues to maim and kill farmers to this day: a destructive use of surplus keeps destroying capital and people half a century after it was paid for.

In fairness, we should note a duality already encountered in previous chapters. The Korean War gave neighboring Japan a powerful stimulus: the US Army purchased trucks, uniforms, and equipment-repair services from Japanese firms, and these “special procurements” helped Japanese industry emerge from its postwar stagnation. Toyota, which stood on the brink of bankruptcy in 1950, survived largely thanks to military orders for trucks. Destruction on one side of the strait became demand on the other—which, of course, does not make the war itself developmental.

Colonial wars added their own toll. The Algerian War of 1954–1962 cost the lives of between three hundred thousand and more than a million Algerians, according to various estimates; the official Algerian figure is higher still, at one and a half million. British historian Alistair Horne recounted the war in detail in *A Savage War of Peace* (1977). After it ended came something that our framework ranks among the gravest forms of destruction: an exodus of skilled people. In the summer of 1962, hundreds of thousands of European settlers left the country within a few months—almost nine hundred thousand in all—and with them went most of its engineers, doctors, teachers, accountants, and officials. The colonizers were responsible for a colonial system that had kept these professions almost closed to the indigenous population, but the new state had to pay the price in empty hospitals and idle enterprises. A similar story, this time without any war, unfolded in Uganda: in 1972, dictator Idi Amin gave tens of thousands of residents of South Asian origin—merchants and owners of workshops and factories—ninety days to leave. Their shops and businesses were handed to cronies who did not know how to run them, and over the following years the country’s economy collapsed. As in Spain in 1492 or France after the revocation of the Edict of Nantes, discussed in earlier chapters, the expelled took with them something that cannot be confiscated: skill.

Institutions were destroyed as well—the part of a society that takes longest to rebuild. In 1953, British and American intelligence agencies helped overthrow Iran’s prime minister Mohammad Mosaddegh, who had nationalized the country’s oil industry. In 1954, with CIA support, Guatemalan president Jacobo Árbenz was overthrown after launching a land reform that affected the holdings of the American United Fruit Company. The Soviet Union, in turn, used armed force to suppress the Hungarian uprising in 1956 and the Prague Spring in 1968. Every such coup or invasion sent the inhabitants a message: they did not write the rules in their own country, and anything invested for the long term could be taken away at any moment. Where this expectation takes root, people—as we saw as far back as the chapter on feudal Europe—prefer to hide, consume, or send their surplus abroad rather than invest it.

Finally, destruction was taking place where almost no one counted it—in the rich countries themselves. Minamata, a small fishing town on the Japanese island of Kyushu, in the 1950s. Cats in the coastal villages begin to “dance”—convulsing and throwing themselves into the sea. Then the strange illness strikes people: their hands go numb, their speech becomes confused, they lose their sight, and children are born with severe brain damage. The disease is officially recorded in 1956. The cause turns out to be mercury discharged into the bay by a chemical plant owned by the Chisso Corporation: it accumulated in fish as methylmercury, and fish was the local people’s staple food. In 1959, company doctor Hajime Hosokawa confirmed the cause in experiments on cats, but the results were not made public, and the dumping continued until 1968. More than two thousand patients were ultimately recognized officially, while estimates put the number affected in the tens of thousands; American historian Timothy George recounts the story in detail in *Minamata* (2001). In the United States in 1969, Cleveland’s oil-coated Cuyahoga River caught fire—and, as it turned out, far from the first time. The London smog and the pesticides discussed in the technology section belonged to the same category.

Economists call such losses “external effects,” or externalities—costs borne by people who did not take part in the transaction. As early as 1920, British economist Arthur Pigou wrote that a factory whose smoke fell on its neighbors cost society more than its accounts showed, because part of its expenses was paid in other people’s health and other people’s laundry. The Chisso plant made products needed throughout Japanese industry, and its output was duly included in the figures of Japan’s “miracle.” The poisoned bay, sick children, and ruined fishery were not included at all. This bears directly on the question “where did the surplus go?”: part of what the era counted as surplus was in fact the consumption of someone else’s capital—health, fish stocks, clean water, and clean air. In fairness, the reverse should also be added: it was at the end of the “Golden Age” that societies began to present this bill. In 1970, the United States created the Environmental Protection Agency and adopted a new Clean Air Act; that same year, the Japanese parliament passed a whole series of antipollution laws; and in 1973, a court ordered Chisso to compensate the victims. Democracy, a free press, and independent courts, discussed in the section on power, proved to be the mechanism through which victims could force those responsible to pay. As we saw in the previous chapter, residents of poisoned places in planned economies had almost no such mechanism.

The conclusion of this section is an awkward one for nostalgia about the “Golden Age.” Its wealth was real, but it coexisted with destruction displaced to the periphery of the world and into nature—places where it did not appear in reports of growth. Anyone who compares eras solely by income per person in rich countries sees only one side of the balance sheet. Yet there was a third column as well: expenditures that destroyed nothing but created nothing either. They are the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasted

Buenos Aires, early 1960s. Argentina decided that it would have an automobile industry of its own and closed its market to imported cars with high tariffs. The logic was understandable: why send foreign currency to Detroit and Wolfsburg if cars could be assembled at home? Foreign and domestic firms responded eagerly—the market behind the tariff wall was guaranteed. In 1959, the government of Arturo Frondizi approved twenty-three automobile-plant projects, according to historian Claudio Belini’s calculations, and by the early 1960s about twenty companies were assembling cars in the country. But all of Argentina bought fewer cars in a year than a single large European plant produced in a few months. Each firm assembled only a few thousand vehicles, using outdated equipment and without the scale that makes an assembly line cheap. A car cost the buyer far more than the same model did in Europe, and selling it abroad was impossible: it lost on both price and quality. The factories operated, people received wages, and the statistics showed growth in industrial production. But productivity—the chief sign of the developmental in our framework—hardly increased.

This is wasted expenditure in its typical “Golden Age” form. Not a palace or a pyramid, but a factory built in earnest, with workers and engineers—yet operating under conditions that gave it no reason to improve.

### Import Substitution: A Wall Without an Examination

The idea of import substitution—replacing imported manufactured goods with domestic ones by protecting infant industries through tariffs and bans—was not foolish. We saw in Chapter 11 that this was how the United States and nineteenth-century Germany rose, and in the section on money that commodity-producing countries had good reasons not to trust a world market in which, as Prebisch and Singer observed, the prices of their goods lagged behind the prices of machinery. In its first years, the policy often produced rapid growth: in the 1950s, Brazil, Mexico, Turkey, and India built factories at rates that others envied. The problem was not protection as such, but that it became permanent.

In the section on development, we saw what distinguished Korea and Taiwan from Argentina and India: there, protection was payment for exports; here, it was a gift with no conditions attached. A firm behind a tariff wall earned money not by making a better or cheaper product, but by obtaining the right to sell in a closed market. The most valuable asset became not the factory but a license, a quota, or permission to import machinery or obtain foreign currency. In 1974, American economist Anne Krueger, who studied India and Turkey, gave this phenomenon a name that became firmly established in the economic vocabulary: “rent-seeking,” the struggle for the right to income created not by production but by restriction. She cited estimates according to which such rents amounted to about seven percent of India’s national income and about fifteen percent of Turkey’s. Her figures themselves are disputed, but almost no one contests the mechanism: firms’ best efforts went not into the workshop or laboratory but into the waiting rooms of government ministries.

In India, this system acquired the mocking nickname “License Raj”—by analogy with British rule, which it had supposedly replaced with rule by officials. Expanding a factory, changing its products, or producing more than the authorized amount required papers from several government departments; companies sometimes applied for capacity they had no intention of building simply to keep a competitor from obtaining it. The Hindustan Ambassador automobile, produced from 1958 and modeled on a British Morris of the mid-1950s, remained almost unchanged for decades—people bought it because they had no choice. After 1950, the Indian economy grew by an average of roughly three and a half percent a year; economist Raj Krishna wryly called this the “Hindu rate of growth.” With the population growing by more than two percent, income per person rose by little more than one percent a year—slower than in almost any East Asian country. Defenders of India’s path object, and they deserve a hearing: a country that had only just emerged from colonial poverty created heavy industry, a network of technical institutes, nuclear and space programs, and, unlike many of its neighbors, preserved democracy. Part of the surplus invested went into genuine development. But another part burned up in queues for permits—as shown by the acceleration of growth when the government became friendlier to private business in the 1980s and began dismantling the licensing system in 1991. Economists disagree over which mattered more; Dani Rodrik and Arvind Subramanian argue that the turning point came before the major reforms.

The difference between Korea and Argentina shows that wasted and developmental expenditure often differ not in what the money is invested in—in both cases, factories—but in whether the investment faces a test. A factory required to sell its goods in a foreign market is forced to learn. A factory with a guaranteed market can live on rent for years.

### Prestige in Concrete

The second kind of wasted expenditure is familiar from the pyramids and the fountains of Versailles. The “Golden Age” gave it modern forms.

On April 21, 1960, Brazilian president Juscelino Kubitschek inaugurated the new capital, Brasília, built in about four years in the middle of an empty plateau according to Lúcio Costa’s plan, with palaces designed by Oscar Niemeyer. Kubitschek had promised “fifty years of progress in five”; the city was meant to draw development into the interior of the vast country. To some extent, it did—the development of Brazil’s inland regions accelerated. But the construction, financed largely by printing money, fueled inflation that would become a chronic disease of the Brazilian economy for decades. Architects debate Brasília as a masterpiece and economists as an investment; in our framework, it is a mixed case in which function and prestige were so tightly intertwined that they are almost impossible to separate.

Ghana provides a clearer example. Kwame Nkrumah, the first president of the country that in 1957 became the first south of the Sahara to gain independence, invested the surplus from cocoa exports in development—and this was no empty phrase: schools, roads, and the Akosombo hydroelectric dam on the Volta River, which brought electricity to the country. But alongside them grew projects that served above all the image of the new nation: for the 1965 summit of the Organization of African Unity, a luxurious complex for delegations was built in Accra—just as cocoa prices collapsed and the treasury emptied. State factories were located where there were neither raw materials nor markets. Nkrumah was overthrown in 1966, and Ghana entered a long decline. Such structures are called “white elephants,” after the legend of Siamese kings who gave a sacred white elephant to courtiers who had fallen from favor: it could neither be sold nor put to work, yet was expensive to maintain.

Rich countries built white elephants of their own, only larger. The Anglo-French supersonic airliner Concorde, which first flew in 1969, was a marvel of engineering and a source of pride for both countries. Yet it was already clear that the spacious and economical Boeing 747, which entered service in 1970, better answered aviation’s central question: how to carry large numbers of people cheaply. The program’s costs exceeded the original estimate many times over; Concorde was never sold to anyone except the two countries’ own national airlines—only twenty aircraft were built, of which fourteen entered scheduled service. Governments did not dare cancel the project: too much had been invested, too many jobs were at stake, and its prestige was too great. In 1976, biologists Richard Dawkins and Tamsin Carlisle called the habit of continuing to invest in a failing undertaking merely because so much had already been invested the “Concorde fallacy.” Economists and psychologists adopted the term.

The space race is a more difficult case. According to NASA, the Apollo program cost the United States approximately twenty-five billion dollars at the time; in July 1969, human beings set foot on the Moon. It was above all a gesture in the competition between systems—a response to the Soviet satellite of 1957 and Gagarin’s flight in 1961. But unlike Concorde, the race left behind rockets, communications and weather satellites, a leap forward in microelectronics, and a generation of engineers. Here wasted and developmental expenditure were once again mixed together—as in the military science discussed in the section on technology.

### Mountains of Butter and Lakes of Wine

The third kind was redistribution that froze the past in place. The European Economic Community’s Common Agricultural Policy came into operation in 1962. Its aims were understandable: Europe remembered the hunger of the war years, and farmers made up a large and politically influential share of the electorate. The Community guaranteed farmers prices above world levels and bought up the surpluses. Production increased, but there was no one to buy it at inflated prices. By the end of the 1970s, warehouses held “butter mountains” and “milk lakes,” joined by “wine lakes”; the surpluses were stored at public expense, distilled into alcohol, or sold abroad with subsidies, depressing prices for poor exporting countries. Agricultural policy absorbed most of the Community’s common budget—according to the European Commission, from two-thirds to three-quarters or more during the 1970s. It gave farmers stability, but to a large extent preserved small, low-productivity farms that would otherwise have given way to other occupations. Similar support systems existed in the United States and Japan. This was redistribution in its purest form: it did not make a single farmer more productive, but merely paid for everything to remain as it was.

### Affluence for Affluence’s Sake

Finally, there was the consumer dream itself. In 1958, Canadian-born American economist John Kenneth Galbraith published *The Affluent Society*. His observation sounded paradoxical: the economy had learned to produce more than people needed and now devoted enormous effort to creating demand for its own products—through advertising, fashion, and credit. A family in a new automobile, he argued, drove along broken roads through a littered city to a polluted river: private affluence coexisted with public poverty. Two years later, journalist Vance Packard described in *The Waste Makers* what would later be called “planned obsolescence”: changing models every year so that last year’s car would look old. The enormous tailfins on Detroit automobiles of the late 1950s became a symbol of the era—they contained more chrome than the road required, much like the feasts that Thorstein Veblen had called “conspicuous consumption” sixty years earlier.

Caution is needed here. Much of what critics regarded as waste was, for millions of families, their first refrigerator, washing machine, television set, or seaside holiday—in other words, a genuine rise in welfare. Market-oriented economists objected to Galbraith that it was not for him to decide which of people’s desires were “real” and which were “created,” and the dispute remains unresolved. But one part of his argument has stood the test: growth measured as the sum of sales does not distinguish between a refrigerator that frees several hours of a woman’s time each week and a chrome tailfin that adds nothing to a car but weight. For this book, that distinction is central.

### Where Did the Surplus Go?

Putting the sections of this chapter together produces a picture not seen before in this book. In the rich countries, investment in machinery, schools, hospitals, science, and roads increased. The share of all surplus represented by this spending is unknown: national accounts do not divide it among this book’s three categories. Wasted expenditure remained—the arms race, individual prestige projects, and subsidies with no test of results. Its total share has not been established. In poor countries, similar projects could weigh especially heavily on limited budgets, but these examples cannot tell us what happened to all the surplus of such countries. The lesson of the section is simple: waste rarely looks like wastefulness. It looks like a factory, an airliner, a capital city, or support for farmers—and it can be distinguished from development only by asking one question: did this expenditure enable anyone to produce more, know more, or live better than before?

## Lesson of the Era

The village of Douelle, with which Jean Fourastié began his book, is perhaps the best summation of this chapter. Over thirty years, no deposits were discovered there and no provinces were conquered. Something else changed: the tractor replaced the horse, running water replaced the well, schooling lasted longer, and the fruits of these changes went not to a landlord or the treasury but to the same families that worked the land. No earlier era in this book had offered such an answer to the question of where the surplus went: in a number of countries, investment in tools, knowledge, and social services was accompanied by rising living standards for broad sections of society. The share of all surplus represented by these investments cannot be estimated from the example of a single village. But the same chapter also showed us Minamata, Algeria, Punjab, and the butter mountains. Let us condense what we have seen into five theses.

**First: surplus goes into development when the majority has a stake in making the pie grow, rather than grabbing its slice as quickly as possible.** The high investment of Japan and Western Europe rested on more than thrift alone. It rested on the bargain described by Barry Eichengreen: workers restrained their demands today because they believed they would receive a share of tomorrow’s gains; entrepreneurs invested their profits because they believed they would not be consumed by a strike or confiscation; after land reform, a farmer fertilized the field because the harvest remained his. Where such confidence was absent—in countries of coups and foreign interventions—surplus was hidden, consumed, or sent abroad. This is the same lesson taught by the Dutch stock exchange and the English Parliament: confidence in tomorrow is not a moral virtue, but a condition for any long-term investment.

**Second: knowledge is the cheapest capital in the world, but it works only in minds prepared to receive it.** The miracles of Germany, Japan, and Korea were largely miracles of copying: a transistor license for twenty-five thousand dollars, Deming’s lectures, and British “productivity teams” in American factories. Once created, knowledge can be transferred almost for free—one of the most powerful developmental properties we have encountered in all of history, from the obsidian routes of the Stone Age to the printing press. But those who succeeded in copying had literate workers and engineers. A machine can be bought in a year; it takes twenty years to raise a person who understands it. This is why investment in schooling, which looks like a luxury in a poor country, is actually the condition that allows every other investment to pay off.

**Third: support without a test turns into rent.** The comparison of Korea with Argentina and India is central to the chapter. In both cases the state intervened, provided protection, and distributed credit; in both, surplus went into factories. The difference was the examination: a Korean firm had to sell its goods to a buyer who could not be fooled by a report, while an Argentine firm could live behind a tariff wall for years. We saw the same mechanism in the previous chapter, except that there a soft budget constraint took the place of the tariff. The conclusion goes beyond the debate over “market or state”: developmental expenditure differs from wasted expenditure not in who controls the money, but in whether the investment faces an independent and honest test of its results—and whether failure can stop further spending. To avoid oversimplifying: dictatorships, too, passed the export test, while democracies, too, built failed “white elephants.” The presence of a test is a property of a specific institutional arrangement, not a political banner.

**Fourth: ending destruction frees surplus, but destruction moved outside the accounts does not disappear.** By ceasing to wage war against itself, Europe received perhaps the largest “free” gain of the era: coal and steel were transformed from raw materials of war into raw materials of a common market. But alongside this came wars in Korea, Indochina, and Algeria, coups in Iran and Guatemala, the suppression of Hungary and Czechoslovakia, and, within the rich countries themselves, the poisoning of rivers and bays. Wars beyond the rich countries and environmental pollution often remained outside their growth statistics. The share of the total damage they represented has not been estimated here. This yields a methodological lesson for the entire book: comparing eras only by income per person in rich countries means looking at a single column of the balance sheet. The second—destroyed capital, people, and nature—was being filled in at the same time, only in other places. And a third lesson follows here as well: this imbalance began to be corrected when the victims gained a voice—a free press, courts, and elections.

**Fifth: the “Golden Age” rested on conditions that could not last forever.** Historians who explain the miracle through catch-up growth point to the obvious: the gap with America that made rapid copying possible had almost closed by the end of the 1960s, and with it the easiest source of growth was exhausted. Robert Gordon adds that the postwar decades were reaping the fruits of the great inventions of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries—electricity, the internal-combustion engine, running water, and chemistry—and these can be introduced only once: water cannot be piped into the same home a second time. Cheap Middle Eastern oil cost about two dollars a barrel, and few people considered that this price depended on the political will of a handful of governments. Controls on capital movements kept savings at home but were becoming increasingly porous. On August 15, 1971, President Richard Nixon announced that the United States would no longer exchange the dollars of foreign central banks for gold—the Bretton Woods system, born at the Mount Washington Hotel, effectively ceased to operate. The lesson is not that prosperity is doomed, but that an arrangement that distributes surplus well under one set of conditions will not necessarily cope under another. The societies of the “Golden Age” built a mechanism for sharing in a world of rapid growth—and gave almost no thought to what would happen when growth slowed.

Now for the answer to the question running through the book. Where did the era’s surplus go? In the rich countries of the West and in Japan, investment in machinery, roads, power plants, schools, universities, and hospitals increased, while the incomes of many workers rose. At the same time, military spending, failed subsidies, colonial wars, and pollution persisted. The examples of import substitution show the cost of protection without a test, but do not justify claiming that rent and prestige consumed most of the surplus of poor countries. We lack comparable estimates of the shares of the three categories for those countries. By measurable indicators—income per person and life expectancy—this was a period of rapid growth for many countries, but concluding that it had the “highest” share of developmental expenditure would require a separate calculation.

The limits of these conclusions must also be stated. The debate over the causes of the miracle is not settled: advocates of institutional explanations and advocates of catch-up growth rely on the same figures but assign them different weights, while estimates of how much efficiency contributed to the growth of the “tigers” diverge, as the dispute over Krugman and Young showed. A substantial share of the success occurred in countries that received American aid and access to the American market for Cold War reasons—something that cannot be replicated at will. Finally, many of the era’s benefits—short waits to see a doctor, free universities, and a lifetime job at a single factory—existed only in some countries and for some citizens; nostalgia often blends them into a single picture that in reality existed nowhere.

How many people lived in this world, how much their incomes and life expectancy increased, and how sharply the fortunes of rich and poor countries diverged will be covered in the section with the figures.

## The Numbers

The statistics of the “Golden Age” are the best we have encountered so far. After the war, national-accounting standards gradually spread, and international organizations began collecting comparable series. The completeness and quality of statistics still varied from country to country. But here too, income per person in poor countries, life expectancy where deaths were not registered, and especially damage to nature are estimates. Unless otherwise stated, income below is taken from Angus Maddison’s tables in “1990 international dollars,” adjusted for prices and purchasing power.

**People.** According to UN estimates, about two and a half billion people lived on Earth in 1950; by 1973, there were almost four billion. Humanity had never experienced such an increase in a quarter century. The reason was not that more people were being born, but that fewer were dying: antibiotics, vaccines, DDT against malaria-carrying mosquitoes, and clean water reached poor countries faster than incomes did. According to the same UN estimates, average life expectancy worldwide rose from roughly forty-six or forty-seven years in the early 1950s to just under sixty by the early 1970s. In Japan, it climbed from about sixty years to more than seventy-three—a country recently left in ruins had joined the ranks of the world’s longest-lived.

**Growth.** Maddison calculated that from 1950 to 1973, income per person worldwide grew by an average of almost three percent a year—twice as fast as in any of the earlier periods he measured. In Western Europe it grew by about four percent, and in Japan by about eight. At eight percent, income doubles every nine years. According to these tables, the average Western European received about four and a half thousand dollars in 1950 and about eleven and a half thousand in 1973—two and a half times as much. Growth in West Germany was steeper still: from just under four thousand to twelve thousand. A Japanese person began at about nineteen hundred dollars—less than a Mexican—and ended the period at roughly eleven and a half thousand, almost level with Europe. The United States, already the richest country, rose from nine and a half thousand to sixteen and a half thousand: the rest of the world’s gap with America narrowed rather than widened.

**Trade.** According to Maddison’s estimate, the volume of world exports grew by almost eight percent a year during these years—faster than production. A world that had walled itself off with tariffs in the 1930s became more closely connected in a quarter century than it had been on the eve of 1914. The dollar was pegged to gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce, and the other currencies to the dollar; this arrangement lasted until August 1971. For almost the entire period, a barrel of Middle Eastern oil cost about two dollars—less, after adjustment for inflation, than at any later time.

**Work and time.** Unemployment in West Germany fell to about one percent in the early 1960s—labor was so scarce that workers were recruited from Italy, Yugoslavia, and Turkey. According to Maddison’s estimates, average annual hours worked in West Germany fell from roughly twenty-three hundred in 1950 to eighteen hundred by 1973—this was the first time Keynes’s prediction, with which this book began, truly came to pass: people took part of the surplus not in money but in leisure. At the same time, the income gap within rich countries narrowed. According to calculations by Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez, the richest one percent of Americans received about one-tenth of all income, excluding capital gains, from the 1950s through the 1970s—roughly half their share in the late 1920s.

**Armaments.** The era’s wasted expenditure is also visible in the figures. According to official statistics, US military spending in the 1950s amounted to about ten percent of national product, and more at the height of the Korean War. In 1973, the United States had about twenty-nine thousand nuclear warheads and the Soviet Union about sixteen thousand, according to estimates by Robert Norris and Hans Kristensen in the *Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists*; by the mid-1980s, the global stockpile had grown to roughly seventy thousand.

**Diverging fortunes.** The chapter’s most important figure is the difference between regions. According to Maddison, income per person from 1950 to 1973 rose from about nine hundred to fourteen hundred dollars in Africa and from six hundred and twenty to eight hundred and fifty in India. There was growth, but it was slower than among the rich. In 1950, the average Western European was roughly five times richer than the average African; by 1973, roughly eight times. South Korea, whose income in 1950 was below one thousand dollars—poorer than many African colonies—had more than tripled it by 1973 and was already pulling ahead. Argentina, more than two and a half times richer than Japan in 1950, was markedly poorer by 1973. The Green Revolution roughly doubled India’s wheat production during the second half of the 1960s, but did not change the fact that most Indians remained below the poverty line.

The chapter in figures: rapid growth in incomes, life expectancy, and world trade; rising real wages and shorter working hours in many countries; and comparatively low income inequality in a number of rich countries. At the same time, the arms race, wars outside Western Europe, and damage from pollution persisted. Investment in development increased, but its share of all surplus has not been calculated. In the early 1970s, the conditions for growth changed: oil became more expensive, the fixed-exchange-rate system collapsed, and the opportunities for catch-up through copying gradually diminished.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate, and an error by a factor of one and a half or two is possible. The unit is an hour of work by a typical worker in the country and period concerned; a working life is seventy thousand hours; for reference, a sack of wheat weighs fifty kilograms.

### Concorde, 1962–1976

**Source data.** In 1962, the program was estimated at approximately seventy million pounds. By the beginning of scheduled service in 1976, development had cost more than one billion three hundred million, according to the British government, and as much as two billion by other estimates; Britain and France paid roughly equal shares. A British worker at the time earned between sixty pence and one pound an hour—an estimate by the author that requires verification.

**Hours and lives.** Development costs are measured in pounds, but the hourly wage given above has not been verified. I therefore do not establish a figure in labor hours or “working lives” for Concorde here.

**Alternative.** Comparison with a number of district hospitals would require a verified cost estimate for a hospital from a comparable period and with a comparable composition of costs. No such estimate is available here; the only reliable fact is that the program produced twenty aircraft, fourteen of which entered scheduled service.

### US Nuclear Arsenal, 1950–1973

**Source data.** The Brookings Institution study *Atomic Audit*, edited by Stephen Schwartz (1998), calculated that from 1940 to 1996 the United States spent at least five and a half trillion dollars in 1996 prices on nuclear weapons—about three-tenths of all military spending, with more than half going to delivery systems. The authors do not divide the sum by the years of the “Golden Age”; based on the course of the arsenal buildup, which peaked in 1967, I assume roughly half—two and a half to three trillion dollars, an estimate that requires verification. An hour of work by a typical worker in those years was worth eleven to thirteen 1996 dollars.

**Hours and lives.** The estimate of 5.5 trillion dollars covers the entire period from 1940 to 1996. Without isolating a verified sum for 1950–1973 and determining what part of it exceeded the requirements of deterrence, it cannot be converted into labor hours or lives.

**Alternative.** Comparison with the road network shows the scale of the total cost of the US nuclear program over the decades, but does not prove how many roads could have been built instead of the portion of spending required for deterrence from 1950 to 1973.

**Conclusion.** Many historians believe that mutual fear kept the Cold War from turning hot, and part of this expenditure was the price of peace. How many warheads were necessary for deterrence remains a disputed counterfactual question. The spending estimate given here shows the scale of the program, but by itself does not establish the size of its excessive portion.
