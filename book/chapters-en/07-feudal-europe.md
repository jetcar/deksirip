# Chapter 7. Feudal Europe

## Portrait of the Era

Winter, 1086, England. Royal commissioners ride into a village somewhere in Essex or Yorkshire. Twenty years earlier, the Norman duke William conquered this country, and now he wants to know exactly what he owns. Jurors are assembled—a priest, the village reeve, six local residents—and asked the same questions. What is the manor called? Who held it in King Edward’s time, before the conquest, and who holds it now? How many plow teams belong to the lord, and how many to the peasants? How many villeins—dependent peasants with holdings—how many cottars with scraps of land, how many slaves? How much woodland, meadow, pasture, how many fishponds? How many mills? And above all: what was it all worth then, what was it worth when the new owner received the land, and what is it worth now?

The answers were copied into two parchment volumes. The English soon nicknamed the survey the Domesday Book: like the verdict at the Last Judgment, what it recorded could not be appealed. The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* complained that the king omitted not a single ox, cow, or pig. The book covers around thirteen thousand settlements and is still read as a snapshot of the medieval economy—perhaps the most detailed the Middle Ages left us.

What does this snapshot show? Almost everyone is a farmer. About 270,000 heads of household were recorded; adding wives, children, and those omitted, historians estimate England’s population at one and a half to two and a half million. Around forty percent of those recorded were villeins, and roughly one in ten was a slave; slavery had not yet disappeared from England, though it was living through its final decades. The land was divided among the king, roughly two hundred great barons, bishops, and monasteries. The Church held about a quarter of the country’s total income. Towns were few and small: London is absent from the survey, and estimates of its population range from ten thousand to more than twenty thousand—less than a single district of ancient Rome.

One more entry recurs again and again: the mill. Historian Margaret Hodgen, who counted the records in the 1930s, found about 5,600 watermills—roughly one for every fifty families. The Romans knew the watermill; how widely they used it remains disputed, but no survey as dense as this survives for Rome. Here, in a poor, sparsely populated, fragmented country at the edge of Christendom, it stands in almost every valley. We will examine this puzzle in the next section: why did a poorer society, at least according to the documents, use a machine more widely than a richer one?

Let us step away from the English village and look across Europe. The period in this chapter spans roughly a thousand years—from the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the fifth century to the end of the fifteenth. Its first half was an age of decline. The cities Rome had boasted of contracted into neighborhoods around a bishop’s church. Roman roads became overgrown, coinage in the West almost ceased, and long-distance trade shrank to luxury goods. By most estimates, the population of Western Europe in the seventh century was markedly smaller than under the emperors. Vikings came from the north, Hungarians from the east, Arab fleets from the south. Anyone who could sought the protection of someone stronger.

From that search for protection grew what would later be called feudalism. Historians of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries coined the term, and modern specialists debate whether it oversimplifies a varied reality. But the essence is clear: land was granted in tenure in exchange for service. A king granted a baron land for military service, a baron granted it to a knight, and a peasant held a plot in return for labor and dues to the lord. Around 1030, Bishop Adalbero of Laon described society as a house in three parts: some prayed, some fought, and some worked. The scheme was more ideal than description, but it showed candidly who fed and who was fed.

The second half of the era was a time of expansion, called by historians either “medieval growth” or the “commercial revolution of the thirteenth century.” Between roughly 1000 and 1300, according to estimates by Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones, Europe’s population approximately doubled—from a little over thirty million to seventy or eighty million. Forests were cleared and marshes drained; German settlers founded hundreds of villages in the east; cities with tens of thousands of inhabitants grew in Flanders and northern Italy. Around 1300, Florence and Venice were among the largest cities in Christendom. Italian bankers and Flemish clothmakers met at the Champagne fairs, ships of Hanseatic merchants sailed the Baltic, and double-entry bookkeeping appeared in the ledgers of Tuscan companies. Thousands of churches and dozens of immense cathedrals were built.

Then catastrophe came. In 1347, chroniclers tell us, Genoese galleys brought plague from Crimea to Sicily, and over four years the Black Death killed between one-third and one-half of Europe’s people, depending on the estimate. It was one of the worst demographic catastrophes in human history. And at the same time, it was the event after which labor became expensive for the first time in centuries and the lives of survivors, paradoxically, improved markedly.

So this is not one era but three: decline, gradual expansion, and rupture. The Middle Ages are often called the “Dark Ages,” and this chapter will show how unfair that is. It was here that the heavy plow, the three-field system, the horse collar, the windmill, mechanical clocks, spectacles, universities, the bill of exchange, and accounting appeared—developments without which the chapters ahead are hard to imagine. But we will also see a society in which most people were bound to the land while the fruits of their labor were taken by those who prayed and fought.

Let us return to the question with which William’s commissioners ended every entry: what is it worth? This is a question about how much can be taken. Where did the surplus of the medieval village go? Some went to the lord as labor services, rent in kind, and payment for grinding grain at his mill. One-tenth of the harvest went to the Church. Another share went to the king in taxes and military service. How much of this returned as plows, mills, and bridges; how much as books and knowledge; and how much as castles, wars, tournaments, and bell towers rising higher than worship required? This question will guide us through a thousand years of the European Middle Ages.

## Energy and Technology

Let us begin with the puzzle left by the survey of 1086. Why did the watermill, known to the Romans but, judging by surviving texts, built only infrequently, stand in almost every valley of poor medieval England?

The French historian Marc Bloch was the first to pose the question seriously, in a 1935 article on the “advent and conquests of the watermill.” His answer sounds simple: the price of labor changed. In Rome, slaves and donkeys ground grain with hand querns and rotary mills; a machine requiring a dam, a channel, and a skilled miller offered the owner no clear advantage. After the empire collapsed, large slave estates disappeared, the population fell, and a peasant family grinding grain by hand spent several hours a day on it—usually women’s work. A waterwheel did the work of dozens of people in the same time. This answer was long considered standard, but it has formidable opponents. Archaeologists Örjan Wikander and Andrew Wilson assembled evidence from recent excavations: Roman watermills have been found in many provinces, from Britain and Gaul to North Africa, while the sixteen-wheel complex at Barbegal in southern France ground flour for an entire city. In their view, the apparent rarity of the Roman mill largely reflects how little ancient authors wrote about ordinary technology. The dispute is now not whether Rome knew the mill, but on what scale: England in 1086 offers a nationwide count; no such count exists for Rome and probably never will. Bloch’s explanation based on the price of labor remains a plausible hypothesis, not a proven fact.

But Bloch’s answer has a second, less pleasant half. A mill was expensive, and it was built not by the peasant but by the lord. Once it was built, he prohibited milling anywhere else: peasants had to bring their grain to the lord’s mill and surrender a share for the service—often one-sixteenth, sometimes more. This right was called a *banality*, from *ban*, the power to command. Peasants’ hand querns were confiscated. The chronicle of the English abbey of St Albans tells how, in the 1330s under Abbot Richard of Wallingford, the monks seized household querns from the townspeople and paved the floor of the abbey parlor with them as a warning. During the revolt of 1381, the townspeople’s first act was to tear up that floor.

Here is an ambiguous case in its purest form. The mill was a developmental technology: by a rough estimate, it freed several hundred to a thousand hours of labor per family each year—hand grinding took one to three hours a day. But it spread through an arrangement that allowed the lord to take a considerable share of the gain. The surplus created by the machine was divided not among those who did the grinding, but in favor of the person who owned the right to grind. Yet the machine was built, and that matters more than it may seem. Rent that can be collected only after investing in equipment is better than rent taken by naked force: at least it pushes the owner toward investment.

The mill was not the only innovation. Between roughly 600 and 1100, northern Europe acquired a cluster of technologies that the American historian Lynn White, in *Medieval Technology and Social Change* (1962), called the agricultural revolution of the early Middle Ages. White’s conclusions were later much criticized—we will come to that below—but the innovations themselves are beyond dispute.

The first was the heavy plow. The Roman plow, or ard, was light: it scratched the soil without turning the furrow and suited the dry, light soils of the Mediterranean. Northern European soils are heavy, clay-rich, and wet. The wheeled heavy plow, with an iron coulter, share, and moldboard, cut the turf, turned the furrow, and opened a channel through which excess water drained. It brought the richest valleys from England to Poland under cultivation. But only a team of six to eight oxen could pull it, and an individual family rarely owned such a team. Oxen were pooled, the land was plowed in long strips—it was difficult to turn the cumbersome team—and the strips of different owners alternated across common open fields. The technology demanded collective organization, and one theory, supported by Lynn White, holds that the village community and its shared rules grew partly from this need. Other historians derive open fields chiefly from the subdivision of holdings through inheritance and the desire to spread the risk of crop failure among different plots. It is no accident that the “plow team” was one of the units counted by William’s commissioners.

The second was the three-field system. In the Mediterranean, a field was divided in two: half was sown and half lay fallow. In northern Europe, where rain falls in summer, land came to be divided in three: one part was sown in autumn with winter crops such as wheat and rye; another in spring with oats, barley, peas, and beans; the third rested. Each year, two-thirds rather than half of the arable land was sown. Legumes enriched the soil with nitrogen and diversified the diet, while two sowings spread the work and reduced risk: if one harvest failed, the other remained. The three-field system is attested from the eighth and ninth centuries, but spread slowly and never became universal.

The third was the horse. According to an older theory advanced by the French historian Lefebvre des Noëttes in 1931, ancient harnesses encircled the animal’s neck with a strap that choked it under heavy pull. Jean Spruytte’s experiments in the 1970s showed this was exaggerated: a horse did not choke in an ancient harness, though it pulled less effectively. The rigid horse collar, resting on the shoulders, probably reached Europe from Asia and spread by the tenth and eleventh centuries. Nailed iron horseshoes came into use with it. A horse in a collar pulls with roughly the same force as an ox, but moves considerably faster. The three-field system supplied the oats it ate. Horses, however, required more feed and cost more, so in many regions the ox remained the principal draft animal for a long time; the transition lasted centuries and occurred earliest in Flanders, northern France, and parts of England.

What did all this yield in numbers? Medieval grain yields look negligible today. The Dutch historian Slicher van Bath, who assembled thousands of agricultural records, showed that in the thirteenth century one grain sown produced an average of three or four. One-quarter or one-third of the harvest had to be set aside immediately as seed. Yet even this was more than light plows produced on heavy northern soils, and, most importantly, new land was being cultivated. Medieval economic growth was mainly extensive: more fields, more people, more villages.

The mill did not stand still either. The waterwheel was adapted not only to grinding but to fulling cloth—wet woolen fabric was beaten with heavy wooden hammers, where once it had been trodden underfoot. The wheel powered forge bellows and hammers, saws, grindstones, crushers for ore and dyes, and later paper mills. In the 1180s, windmills appeared on the plains of East Anglia and northern France—the first securely dated reference is Weedley in Yorkshire around 1185. They were apparently a European invention, built differently from Persian windmills. Wind could grind where there were no swift rivers. Curiously, English documents preserve disputes over whether a lord’s rights extended to the wind: around 1191, Abbot Samson of Bury St Edmunds ordered a windmill erected without his permission by a local priest named Herbert to be demolished, according to the monastic chronicler Jocelin of Brakelond.

Any estimate of the energy available to a medieval person presents a modest picture. Energy historian Vaclav Smil shows in *Energy and Civilization* that almost all useful work was still done by human and animal muscle, while wood supplied heat. A waterwheel producing a few horsepower was the most powerful engine an average European saw in a lifetime. But the share of nonmuscular energy grew slowly and, more importantly, society became accustomed to seeking a mechanical solution to every heavy task. The Industrial Revolution would inherit this habit of looking at flowing water and asking what else it might turn.

There were technologies of another kind as well. Spectacles appeared in northern Italy at the end of the thirteenth century. Their inventor is unknown, but in 1306 the Dominican preacher Giordano da Pisa told an audience in Florence that the art of making spectacles had been invented less than twenty years earlier. Spectacles extended the working lives of scribes, notaries, jewelers, and weavers by years—a direct accumulation of human capital, meaning knowledge and skills embodied in people. Around the same time, in the closing decades of the thirteenth century, weight-driven mechanical clocks with escapements appeared; by the mid-fourteenth century, tower clocks struck the hours in many cities. Time was becoming urban and commercial rather than ecclesiastical: bells marked the beginning and end of work, appointments, and debt deadlines.

In metallurgy, the first blast furnaces appeared by the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries in Sweden and the Rhine valleys: bellows powered by waterwheels made it possible to melt iron instead of merely softening it into a bloom, a spongy mass of metal and slag. This made iron cheaper and enabled cast-iron production. In navigation, the Hanseatic cog—a broad-bellied ship with one mast and a sternpost rudder—made it possible to carry not only expensive goods but grain, timber, salt, and herring across the Baltic and North Seas. The magnetic compass is mentioned in Europe from the late twelfth century onward.

Now to the controversy. The boldest claim in Lynn White’s book concerned not the plow but the stirrup. In his account, the stirrup, which spread among the Franks in the eighth century, allowed a rider to strike with a lance using the force of the entire charging horse; heavy cavalry emerged, and to support expensive mounted warriors Frankish rulers began granting them land—thus feudalism grew from the stirrup. This elegant chain drew fierce criticism. Historians including Peter Sawyer and Bernard Bachrach showed that the stirrup spread more slowly than White believed, that land was granted for service without it, and that mounted combat changed gradually. Today, the claim that “the stirrup created feudalism” is a textbook example of technological determinism—the belief that technology by itself determines the structure of society. The lesson is useful for this book too: technology creates possibilities, but people and institutions decide who uses them and how.

The point is clear in comparison. Many of these devices—waterwheels, the horse collar, the compass, the blast furnace—were known in China earlier than in Europe; Song China, as we saw in the previous chapter, was in many respects ahead of the West. Medieval Europe’s distinction lay not in inventing first, but in the persistence with which it spread inventions and adapted them to new tasks. A mill profited a lord because he charged for grinding; it profited a monastery because monks were meant to pray, not grind; it profited a town because cloth from a fulling mill sold for more. In a society where labor was scarce and rights to income from machinery were clearly, if unjustly, assigned, the holder of the surplus had a reason to invest it in a mechanism rather than only in weapons and feasts. The next section will show how that surplus was divided among the plowman, the lord, and the Church.

## Organization of Labor

Imagine a typical English manor around 1250. Its land was divided into two unequal parts. One was the demesne—from the Latin *dominus*, “lord”: fields, meadows, and gardens cultivated for the lord, whose entire harvest went into his barns. The other consisted of peasant holdings. A dependent peasant, or villein, typically held about thirty acres of arable land—roughly twelve hectares scattered in narrow strips across the common fields—together with the right to graze livestock on the common pasture and gather brushwood in the lord’s forest. In return, he paid not so much with money as with his own time.

Obligations were recorded in manorial surveys with accounting precision. Two or three days a week, the villein or his adult son worked on the demesne: plowing with the lord’s plow, harrowing, carting manure, repairing hedges. At harvest time came additional boon-work—days when the whole family joined the lord’s harvest and received a meal in return. Beyond this, the peasant paid rent in grain, chickens, and eggs at feast days; surrendered a share for grinding at the lord’s mill; and, when the head of the household died, the heirs gave the lord their best animal. Marrying off a daughter, sending a son to a church school, leaving the village—all required permission and a fee. A serf was not a slave: he was rarely sold apart from the land and almost always together with his family; he had a family, a home, a household, and the protection of custom recorded in the manorial court rolls. But neither was he free: he was bound to the land, and the land to the lord.

Where did this organization of labor come from? It had two roots. One was the late Roman *coloni*, free tenants whom emperors bound to the land to ensure tax collection. The other was slaves whom great landowners of the early Middle Ages began to “settle on the land”: they gave a slave a plot and hut so he would feed himself and work part of the week for his lord. By the eleventh and twelfth centuries, the two groups had merged into one estate of dependent peasants, while slavery had almost vanished from Western Europe—the last references to slaves in England date from the early twelfth century. This was an important change: a worker who managed his own holding and kept its harvest had more reason to work well than a slave under supervision.

But only on his own holding. On the demesne, matters were different. An English agricultural treatise written around 1280 and attributed to a certain Walter of Henley is full of advice on supervising laborers who worked slowly and carelessly during labor service. Historians studying the estate accounts of the bishops of Winchester—one of the finest archives of the medieval economy—show that lords understood the problem well. As early as the twelfth century, many commuted labor services into money rents and hired wage workers for the demesne: hired laborers worked better because they could be dismissed. The trend was not linear. When English prices rose rapidly around 1200, lords again found it profitable to manage their estates directly and demand services in kind. But the direction for the centuries ahead was set: compulsory labor was gradually displaced by paid work.

How much did a peasant surrender? Precise calculation is difficult, and historians’ estimates differ. According to figures cited by Christopher Dyer in *Standards of Living in the Later Middle Ages* and by other scholars of the English countryside, a dependent peasant with a full holding gave up no less than one-third of his harvest in rent, labor services, dues, church tithes, and royal taxes—and in severe cases about half. Free tenants gave less. Remember that another quarter to a third of the crop had to be set aside immediately as seed. Little remained for the family, and in bad harvest years—during the Great Famine of 1315–1317, for example, when torrential rain fell for several years in succession—people died by the thousands.

Here lies the chapter’s central answer. The surplus of the medieval village was created chiefly by the labor of the peasant family on its own holding and on the demesne, and extracted through the force of law backed by the sword. The lord himself scarcely participated in production. Sometimes he invested part of his income in a mill, fishpond, or marsh drainage, but more often in horses, weapons, retainers, feasts, and wars. The historian Robert Brenner would later call this “political accumulation”: in the feudal world, the person who grew rich fastest was not the one who farmed best, but the one who seized more land and people.

There was, however, another kind of labor organization in the medieval world: the monastery. The Rule of Saint Benedict, composed in the sixth century, required monks not only to pray but to work with their hands and read every day: idleness, it says, is the enemy of the soul. A monastery was simultaneously a community, an economy, and a school; it endured for centuries, was not divided among heirs, and could plan in generations. This was especially vivid among the Cistercians, an order founded in 1098 at Cîteaux in Burgundy. Cistercians settled in remote, uncultivated places and created large farms, or “granges,” worked not by serfs but by *conversi*—lay brothers who took vows without becoming priests. Cistercian abbeys in Yorkshire became leading suppliers of wool to Flemish weavers; in Burgundy and Champagne, monks built canals, waterwheels, and forges. A twelfth-century description of Clairvaux Abbey admiringly recounts how a single diverted stream successively ground grain, fulled cloth, worked in the tannery, and carried away waste. The monastery was perhaps the closest thing in the early Middle Ages to an “enterprise”: much of its surplus was reinvested in land, technology, and books.

A third form of labor organization lived in the towns: the craft guild. Around 1268, the Paris provost Étienne Boileau compiled the statutes of roughly one hundred Parisian guilds—from bakers to women who spun silk—into the *Book of Trades*. Their structure was similar throughout. A boy was apprenticed to a master for several years—in London, seven years was standard; he lived in the master’s house and learned the craft. Then he became a paid journeyman, and only later, if successful, a master with his own workshop. The guild set the rules: what raw materials could be used, how many apprentices a master could keep, whether work by candlelight was permitted, and at what price goods could be sold. It aided sick members and their widows, buried the dead, and maintained an altar in the parish church.

What guilds meant for development remains disputed among economic historians. Stephan Epstein argued that guilds were primarily a system for transmitting skills: apprenticeship was a craft school, and guild oversight guaranteed quality for buyers unable to judge cloth or silver themselves. Sheilagh Ogilvie, drawing on a vast archival record in *The European Guilds* (2019), replies that guilds’ principal work was different: restricting the number of masters, excluding outsiders, women, and Jews, maintaining prices, and obstructing new methods—in other words, rent-seeking, or earning income through privilege rather than creation. Both are probably right, at different times. A young guild in a growing town taught skills and guarded quality; an old guild in a stagnant town protected its members from competition. The same institution can begin as developmental and end as wasteful—a pattern we will encounter repeatedly.

Thus three ways of organizing labor took shape: compulsory labor on the manor, communal and vowed labor in the monastery, and corporate labor in the town. All three were linked by the same question: who controlled the fruits of the work? On the manor, force supplied the answer; in the monastery, the rule; in the guild, privilege. But in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, money increasingly took control: rent instead of labor service, wages instead of compulsory work, the market instead of the lord’s barn. The next section examines how coin, credit, and long-distance trade returned to Europe.

## Exchange and Money

In the early Middle Ages, coin almost disappeared from everyday life in the West. Roman gold flowed east or settled in hoards, coinage dwindled, and most exchange took place in kind: grain for craftwork, labor for land, gifts for protection. Recall that peasants paid their lords with days of labor service and chickens at feast days. This was not barbarism but adaptation to a world where money was scarce, markets distant, and roads dangerous. But payment in time and chickens was inconvenient: difficult to accumulate, difficult to transport, and nearly impossible to invest in an enterprise at the other end of the country.

Charlemagne was the first to bring order to this chaos. Around 793–794, his monetary reform established a simple system: 240 denarii were struck from one pound of silver; twelve denarii made an accounting solidus, or shilling; twenty solidi made a pound. Only small silver coins were minted, while the solidus and pound were units of account. The system proved remarkably durable: the French *livre*, *sou*, and *denier*; the Italian *lira*, *soldo*, and *denaro*; and the English pound, shilling, and penny were its descendants. Britain abandoned the reckoning of “twelve pennies to a shilling, twenty shillings to a pound” only in 1971.

Silver remained scarce for a long time, and the growth of trade ran up against the shortage. Mines changed the situation. Mining began at Rammelsberg near Goslar in the Harz Mountains in the tenth century; silver was found near Freiberg in Saxony in 1168 and at Kutná Hora in Bohemia at the end of the thirteenth century. Coins became more numerous and larger. Around 1193–1194, Venice began minting the silver *grosso*, or “thick” denarius; in 1252 Florence issued the gold florin, and in 1284 Venice the gold ducat. The florin retained nearly constant weight and purity for a long time, and merchants from London to the Levant accepted it without inspection. This was trust between strangers cast in metal.

Where did merchants meet? At first, at fairs. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, Europe’s most important were the Champagne fairs: six fairs in four towns—Troyes, Provins, Lagny, and Bar-sur-Aube—followed one another for almost the entire year. Italians brought spices, silk, and alum, a salt used to fix dyes; Flemings brought cloth; Germans brought furs and metal. The counts of Champagne understood that moderate tolls were more profitable than robbing merchants: they provided protection on the road, a special fair guard, and a court that settled disputes quickly under mercantile custom. The fairs were markets not only for goods but for debts: during the final days of each fair, mutual obligations were offset and only the difference paid in money. When Champagne passed under the French crown at the end of the thirteenth century and wars and exactions made the route unsafe, trade moved—partly to Bruges, partly to sea routes around Spain. Merchants vote with their feet faster than subjects do.

In the north, another alliance arose: the Hanseatic League. It began as an association of German merchants trading abroad, and from the fourteenth century became a league of cities led by Lübeck, with about two hundred members at its height if small towns are included. The League maintained trading enclaves, or *kontors*, in Novgorod, Bergen, Bruges, and London, and carried not only expensive goods but bulk commodities: grain from Prussia, herring, salt, timber, wax, and furs. It had no king, permanent army, or common treasury, but possessed the power of collective action: if a city or ruler mistreated Hanseatic merchants, the League could impose a trade blockade. The Hanse shows that the rules of exchange can rest not only on a sovereign’s power, but on reputation and a community’s mutual responsibility.

The most interesting developments, however, occurred in Italy. Venetian and Genoese merchants made extensive use of a contract called the *colleganza* in Venice and the *commenda* elsewhere. One participant, usually a wealthy citizen, supplied the money; another, young and enterprising, carried the goods overseas and traded. If the voyage succeeded, they divided the profit: typically three-quarters to the provider of capital, one-quarter to the person who risked his life. If the ship sank, the investor bore the monetary loss. Historians who have studied Venetian archives, including Diego Puga and Daniel Trefler, show that such contracts allowed people without inheritances to enter trade and renewed the merchant elite itself in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. The surplus of some joined the energy of others—one of the clearest mechanisms of development in this chapter. We will see what became of this openness in Venice in the section on destruction.

Then came the bill of exchange. Carrying sacks of silver along roads infested with robbers was dangerous and expensive. A merchant in Florence could give money to a local banker and receive a letter under which the banker’s partner in Bruges would pay the sum in local coin. What moved was not metal but an obligation. The bill of exchange solved another problem too. The Church condemned usury—the charging of interest—as a sin, and twelfth-century church councils reaffirmed the ban. But exchange rates between cities could be arranged so that the investor earned a return during the journey, and theologians debated for centuries whether this was interest or legitimate payment for risk and effort. The ban did not stop credit, but made it more expensive and complicated and surrendered part of the market to people affected differently by church restrictions: Jewish moneylenders, whom kings alternately used, robbed, and expelled, as Edward I of England did in 1290.

Accounting came with credit. In 1202, Leonardo of Pisa, known to us as Fibonacci, wrote the *Book of Calculation*, teaching merchants to reckon with Hindu-Arabic numerals and zero: interest, proportions, currency exchange. Written calculation slowly displaced Roman numerals and the counting board, but it did displace them. Double-entry bookkeeping appeared in the ledgers of Tuscan and Genoese companies in the late thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries: every transaction was recorded twice, as a debit to one account and a credit to another, so an error or theft became visible in the broken balance. The Genoese treasury accounts of 1340 already used the method. Hundreds of ledgers and an immense business correspondence survive from the Prato merchant Francesco Datini, who died in 1410—an archive showing how an owner in one city managed offices in Avignon, Barcelona, and Mallorca. The monk Luca Pacioli described the method systematically only in a treatise of 1494.

But money also had a dark side directly connected to the question of where the surplus went. A sovereign who minted coin could reduce its silver content and pay with “lightened” money. This was debasement, a hidden tax: everyone who held coins or was owed money lost part of their wealth without seeing where it went. Philip IV of France needed money for wars so constantly and altered the coinage so often that he acquired the nickname “the Counterfeiter King.” Around 1360, the Norman scholar Nicole Oresme wrote a *Treatise on Money*, arguing that money belonged not to the sovereign but to the community that used it, and that debasement was in essence worse than usury. It was one of Europe’s first works to judge monetary policy by asking whom it benefited.

Credit could also become a channel of extraction. The Florentine companies of Bardi and Peruzzi, the largest merchant-banking houses of their age, lent money to the English king Edward III for his war with France. Both collapsed in the 1340s. The chronicler Giovanni Villani blamed the king’s refusal to pay; modern historians such as Edwin Hunt argue that the wider crisis also played a role and that chroniclers exaggerated the royal debt. But the lesson remains: the savings of Tuscan merchants and depositors went into a war that lasted more than a century and devastated both countries.

Thus by the fourteenth century Europe had learned to live with money again—and in some respects did so better than Rome. The bill of exchange, trading partnership, double-entry bookkeeping, and reliable gold coin lowered the cost of trust between people who had never met. These were developmental inventions in the most direct sense. Yet the same instruments also served extraction: money allowed a sovereign to debase coin invisibly, while loans drew merchants’ silver into the war treasury. Who set the rules determining which purpose money served is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

June 1215, the meadow at Runnymede on the Thames between Windsor and Staines. King John of England, known as Lackland, meets armed barons who have refused to obey him and already occupied London. John had lost a war in France and with it Normandy, and for years had squeezed money from his subjects in attempts to recover it: he demanded scutage—payment for exemption from military service—more often and in larger amounts than custom allowed, sold widows in marriage to the highest bidder, and seized the estates of those who fell from favor. The barons had come not to overthrow the king, but to write down what he could not do. The result was the charter later known as Magna Carta.

Most of its more than sixty clauses read today like a dull list of feudal grievances: inheritance dues, fish weirs in rivers, measures of wine and cloth. But among them were two ideas that outlived their authors. First: extraordinary levies were not to be imposed without the “common counsel of the realm.” Second: no free man could be arrested, dispossessed, or exiled except by the lawful judgment of his peers or the law of the land. Within weeks the pope annulled the charter and war resumed. But after John’s death it was reissued again and again, and by the end of the thirteenth century it had become the authority invoked whenever a king reached for another person’s purse. Curiously, the clause requiring the “common counsel” to consent to levies was removed from reissues as early as 1216. But the exchange itself became customary: in 1225, the king confirmed the charter in return for a tax; in 1297, he confirmed it again.

Why is this scene so important to our question? Because the greatest enemy of surplus in the medieval world was not crop failure but arbitrary power. If a ruler could seize a harvest, mill, or merchant’s goods at any moment, long-term investment made no sense: it was more rational to hide silver in a pot or spend it today. Economists call this the commitment problem: a powerful ruler must somehow persuade subjects that he will not rob them, yet they distrust his word precisely because he is powerful. The charter was one of Europe’s first attempts to solve this problem on parchment.

Medieval Europe was unusually fragmented in its power. Above a knight stood a baron, above a baron a king, but alongside them existed other forces: the Church with its own courts and tithes, towns with their own charters, merchant associations with their own rules. None could fully subordinate the others. The loudest clash was the Investiture Controversy—the struggle between popes and German emperors in the late eleventh and early twelfth centuries over the right to appoint bishops. In 1077, Emperor Henry IV stood for three days outside the gates of Canossa so Pope Gregory VII would lift his excommunication. The dispute ended in compromise with the Concordat of Worms in 1122, but its most important consequence lay elsewhere. In *Law and Revolution* (1983), the American legal historian Harold Berman argued that, in defending its independence from kings, the Church created Europe’s first systematic legal order. Around 1140, Gratian, a teacher of law in Bologna, gathered thousands of conflicting church rulings into a single compilation, the *Decretum*, organizing them by subject and explaining how contradictions could be resolved. Thus canon law took shape—a law with scholars, textbooks, and logic, not merely custom and the sovereign’s will.

At almost the same time, Bologna began rereading Justinian’s *Digest*, the immense collection of Roman jurists’ opinions that had been nearly forgotten in the West. Students from across Europe gathered around teachers of law; from this grew the University of Bologna, traditionally dated to 1088. Paris, Oxford, Salamanca, and Padua followed. The university was another medieval invention: a self-governing corporation of teachers or students that decided for itself whom to teach and whom to award a degree. University-trained jurists served kings, towns, and merchants. They brought concepts of contract, property, evidence, and appeal that made disputes more predictable.

Trade also required predictability. When a merchant from Genoa sold cloth to a merchant from Bruges, whose court would judge their dispute? Around fairs and ports arose what would later be called the *lex mercatoria*, or law merchant: customs governing contracts, bills of exchange, and surety, applied by merchants themselves in rapid fair courts. In England, such tribunals were known as “courts of piepowder,” or dusty feet, for visiting traders who had no time to wait. Historians debate how uniform this law was across Europe—probably less so than nineteenth-century jurists believed. But the principle is clear: many rules of exchange were created from below by the people who needed them.

In *Institutions and the Path to the Modern Economy* (2006), economist Avner Greif described another mechanism that enabled trade with strangers: communal responsibility. If a merchant from Florence failed to pay a debt to a merchant from London, the London authorities could seize the goods of any other Florentine found in the city. It sounds unjust, but it worked: the entire community had an interest in policing the honesty of its members, so an outsider could trust a Florentine without knowing him personally. As trade grew and communities became more diverse, the system began to fail, and in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries it was displaced by treaties between cities and courts that punished the debtor personally. Greif sees this as a step toward impersonal law—law protecting each person, rather than each member of one’s own group.

Towns secured rights for themselves as well. From the late eleventh century, communes arose in northern Italy—self-governing associations of citizens that elected consuls and later invited a *podestà*, an outside administrator appointed for a year so no local family could seize power. In Flanders, northern France, and Germany, towns bought or wrested charters from lords: the right to their own court, their own market, and a fixed annual payment instead of arbitrary exactions. Many towns accepted runaway serfs: if the lord did not find his man within a year and a day, the fugitive became free. Nineteenth-century German jurists compressed the principle into the saying “city air makes free.” The city became an island where surplus remained in the hands of those who created it—and therefore a place where it was invested in workshops, ships, and partnerships.

Finally, assemblies appeared with which a ruler had to negotiate over money. In 1188, King Alfonso IX of León summoned a council attended not only by nobles and bishops but representatives of towns; it is often called Europe’s first parliament. In England in 1265, the rebel baron Simon de Montfort invited knights from the shires and townsmen to his council, and from 1295 Edward I began summoning them regularly because he needed taxes for wars. Later came the Estates General in France, the Cortes in Aragon and Castile, and diets in Poland and Hungary. Economic historians Jan Luiten van Zanden, Eltjo Buringh, and Maarten Bosker counted how often European parliaments met between 1188 and 1789 and found that cities grew faster where assemblies convened regularly and genuinely constrained the king. Correlation does not prove causation—wealthy towns may themselves have secured stronger assemblies—but the direction of the relationship is revealing for our question.

We should not romanticize. Charters and parliaments protected above all those who sat in them: barons, knights, wealthy townsmen. A villein was still judged in his lord’s manorial court, and the clause concerning a “free man” did not apply to him. Town councils quickly became clubs of a few families, guilds became shields against competition, and the Church that created canon law also collected tithes, sold offices, and tried heretics. Rights in medieval Europe were privileges—the liberties of particular estates and corporations, not human rights.

Yet this arrangement had its own developmental logic. Power divided among kings, the Church, barons, and towns could not take everything at once. Each holder of a privilege defended its share of the surplus from the others and thereby limited arbitrary power as a whole. Merchants could leave Champagne for Bruges, artisans one town for another, scholars Paris for Oxford. Rulers who wanted revenue had to compete for it by offering order, courts, and moderate tolls. This competition among jurisdictions—not the wisdom of any single sovereign—distinguished Western Europe from the more unified empires discussed in the previous chapter. The next section asks what grew from this constraint and which mechanisms actually developed the medieval economy.

## What Fostered Development

Around 1106, Frederick, Archbishop of Hamburg-Bremen, concluded an agreement with a group of Dutch settlers who had come to him from near Utrecht. He gave them the marshy lowlands along the Weser that no one could use: the land stood under water, and local peasants kept away from it. The Dutch knew how to do what others did not—dig drainage ditches, build dikes, and divert water. The terms of the agreement have survived, and they are striking for an age of serfdom: the settlers received the land as a hereditary holding, paid a fixed and modest annual quitrent—a money rent—judged their own minor disputes, and were exempt from most obligations. The archbishop reasoned simply: the marsh would yield him nothing, while drained land would yield at least a little, but forever.

This is one of the earliest known charters of its kind, but far from the last. In the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, internal colonization swept across Europe—what historians call the “great clearances.” French lords founded “new villages” and *bastides*, luring settlers with privileges. German colonists moved east by the tens of thousands—and, over two centuries, by the hundreds of thousands according to historians’ estimates—beyond the Elbe and Oder into Poland, Bohemia, and the Baltic, almost everywhere receiving better terms than at home: a large holding, fixed rent, and the right to sell their farm and leave. Flemings and Dutchmen reclaimed land from the sea by creating polders, while Cistercian monks cleared forests in England and Germany. Europe’s map of arable land in 1300 had largely been drawn during those two centuries.

For our question, what matters here is who invested and why. Clearing land was hard, protracted work: years of grubbing out stumps, digging ditches, and waiting for a new field to begin yielding a harvest. Such labor, too, was an investment of surplus—not silver, but strength and time. And people undertook it where they had been promised that the fruits would remain theirs. A colonist on the Weser or in Silesia had a reason to dig: his rent would not rise if he harvested more, and the land could not be taken away at his lord’s whim. Freedom proved not the reward for growth, but its precondition. We will see this more than once in the chapters ahead.

The second developmental stream flowed not into the land, but into the mind. In 1085, the Castilian king Alfonso VI took Toledo, one of the largest cities in Muslim Spain, where Arabic translations of Greek scholars and original works by Arab mathematicians, astronomers, and physicians were preserved. In the twelfth century, scholars from all over Europe began making their way to Toledo. The most prolific of them, the Italian Gerard of Cremona, lived there for most of his life and, according to a list compiled by his pupils, translated more than seventy works from Arabic into Latin—among them Ptolemy’s *Almagest*, Euclid’s *Elements*, Avicenna’s medical *Canon*, and al-Khwarizmi’s algebra. Other translators worked in Sicily and southern Italy, where Latin, Greek, and Arabic learning met.

This was perhaps the most profitable import of the Middle Ages. In a few generations, Europe acquired what other civilizations had accumulated over centuries—and acquired it almost for nothing, at the cost of the labor of a few dozen translators and their episcopal patrons. The texts brought Hindu-Arabic numerals with zero; we have already seen how Leonardo of Pisa explained to merchants in 1202 how much easier they made calculation. Paper came with them: Muslim workshops in Xàtiva, Spain, were making it by the mid-twelfth century, and around 1276 paper mills began operating in Fabriano, Italy, where rags were pounded by water-powered hammers. Paper cost several times less than parchment, and this quietly changed everything: accounts, correspondence, and drafts could now be written without worrying about the price of every page.

Knowledge did not merely arrive—it began to spread. We have already discussed universities; by 1300 Europe had around twenty, and by 1500 more than sixty. Economists Eltjo Buringh and Jan Luiten van Zanden used surviving catalogues to estimate how many manuscript books were copied in different centuries. They showed that production in Western Europe rose severalfold in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, while reading spread beyond monks to lawyers, merchants, and town clerks. These estimates rest on incomplete data, but they show the direction persuasively: long before the printing press, Europe was already becoming a society in which knowledge was valued, copied, and bought. Gutenberg, whom we will meet later, answered a demand that had matured three hundred years before him.

The third stream was infrastructure. Roman roads were crumbling, but medieval societies built their own: bridges, dams, quays, and canals. Construction of a stone bridge across the Thames began in London in 1176; completed in 1209, it stood for more than six hundred years. Money for such bridges was raised in different ways: tolls, donations, and bequests—many towns had “bridge brotherhoods,” while the Church granted indulgences to those who donated to a bridge as a pious work. In Flanders and Lombardy, canals were dug to connect towns with the sea and rivers. Human-powered cranes were erected in ports. All this reduced transport costs—and thus expanded the market in which a peasant could sell grain and a clothmaker his cloth.

To this we should add what we have already discussed separately: watermills and windmills, the horse collar and harnessed horse, the three-field system, double-entry bookkeeping, bills of exchange and partnerships, and training in the guilds. Together they form a recognizable picture. Medieval development was rarely the work of one great inventor or one wise ruler. It consisted of thousands of small investments by many people: a peasant bought a horse instead of an ox, a miller installed a new wheel, a monastery drained a meadow, a town built a bridge, a merchant hired a bookkeeper, and a scribe copied a law textbook for a fee. And almost everywhere such investments proceeded rapidly, the investor had at least some assurance that the result would remain his: fixed rent for the colonist, a charter for the townsman, a contract for the merchant, corporate rights for the university.

Fairness requires us to acknowledge the limits of this growth. Much of it was extensive growth: more people, more land under the plow, more villages. Yields, as we have seen, remained low, and output per worker rose slowly. Historians Stephen Broadberry, Bruce Campbell, and their coauthors, who reconstructed English incomes from 1270 onward, estimate that by the end of the thirteenth century per capita income had almost ceased to grow: population had pressed against the limits of the land, new fields were increasingly poor, and peasant holdings were being subdivided. Following Michael Postan, many historians see this as a Malthusian trap—a situation in which growth in production is consumed by growth in population. Others, such as Robert Brenner, reply that the trap was social rather than natural: a peasant whose lord took the surplus could not invest in manure, livestock, and better tools, while the lord receiving the rent preferred to spend it on war and prestige. However blame is apportioned, by 1300 developmental investment had proved insufficient to break through the ceiling.

Even so, the outcome of those centuries should not be underestimated. Europe entered the Middle Ages as a devastated periphery of the ancient world and emerged with machines in every valley, universities, paper, numerals, bookkeeping, bills of exchange, and a body of law strangers knew how to use. Almost all of this was created not in palaces but at the points of contact—where surplus remained, however briefly, with those who produced it, and where knowledge could pass freely from hand to hand. But surplus flowed in other directions as well: into swords, sieges, and fire—and into places where destruction came not from people but from the flea and the bacterium. That is the subject of the next section.

## What Destroyed

In the spring of 1315, rain began falling across Northern Europe and did not stop all summer. Grain rotted in the fields, hay could not be dried, and salt made by evaporating seawater would not crystallize. The following year was the same, and then livestock began to die. In *The Great Famine*, historian William Chester Jordan describes grain prices rising severalfold in England and Flanders, seed grain being eaten, followed by draft animals, and—according to municipal accounts—about a tenth of the inhabitants of Ypres in Flanders being buried between May and November 1316. Jordan estimates that five to ten percent of the population died in affected regions, and in some places more. The Great Famine of 1315–1317 was a natural blow, but it struck a society with almost no margin of safety: holdings had been subdivided, surplus extracted, and the granaries of lords and monasteries were by no means always opened to the hungry.

This is a good place to begin a discussion of destruction because it shows that destruction is rarely purely natural. The rain cannot be blamed on people, but how many people it killed depended on where the previous century’s surplus had gone—into reserves and improving the land, or into rent and war.

The principal man-made destroyer of the Middle Ages was war—and not only great wars. For the knight, war was a profession and a source of income: ransom for a noble captive, plunder, captured lands. “Private wars” between lords were constant, and their customary method was not battle but devastation of an opponent’s estates: burning villages, driving off livestock, and trampling crops so the enemy lost his income. From the late tenth century, the Church tried to restrain this through the Peace of God and Truce of God movements—banning attacks on peasants, clerics, and merchants, and warfare on certain days of the week and feast days. The very need for such prohibitions speaks to the scale of the disaster.

The Hundred Years’ War between England and France (1337–1453) perfected this method. English armies did not so much besiege fortresses as conduct *chevauchées*—mounted raids through French lands. In 1355, Edward the Black Prince marched from Bordeaux almost to the Mediterranean in two months, burning towns and hundreds of villages along the way. The aim was explicitly economic: to undermine the French king’s tax base. Between campaigns, France was plundered by “companies”—bands of mercenaries left without pay. The war lasted more than a century, and throughout that time the surplus of both countries went to soldiers, ransoms, and fortifications, while taxes collected from peasants turned into their neighbors’ burned granaries.

The second destroyer was seizure from those who held capital and knowledge. We have already seen how Edward I expelled the Jews from England in 1290 after first burdening them with ruinous taxes. Philip IV of France did the same in 1306, confiscating their property and debt instruments for the treasury. Then, on October 13, 1307, on his orders, the Knights Templar were arrested throughout France—a military order that over two centuries had become one of Europe’s largest bankers and, in particular, a creditor of the king himself. Under torture the knights were forced to confess to heresy, the order was dissolved, and its grand master, Jacques de Molay, was burned in 1314. Historians still debate Philip’s motives, but the outcome for our question is clear: a ruler in debt solved the debt problem by destroying his creditor. Every such case taught the same lesson to anyone with money: do not lend to the powerful, and do not keep wealth in plain sight.

The third destroyer was quieter: closing the doors. Remember the Venetian *colleganza*, through which people without inherited wealth could enter trade? Diego Puga and Daniel Trefler, who studied thousands of Venetian documents, showed how it ended. The families that had grown rich through trade decided they no longer needed newcomers. In 1297, the Great Council—the republic’s principal governing body—was “closed”: access was reserved for families whose members had already sat on it. The event is still known as the *Serrata*, “the closure.” Trade was closed off next: the most profitable voyages of state galleys came to be allocated at auctions won by the same families, while the *colleganza* gradually fell out of use. Venice remained rich for a long time, but according to Puga and Trefler, renewal of the mercantile elite ceased and inequality grew. Nothing burned and no one died—the ladder by which some had climbed was simply pulled up behind them. This was the destruction not of capital but of opportunity, slower and less visible than war.

And finally, plague. In 1347–1351, the Black Death killed, by most historians’ estimates, between one-third and one-half of Europe’s population; Norwegian historian Ole Benedictow puts the figure as high as about sixty percent. Entire villages were abandoned forever—in England, archaeologists count thousands of “deserted medieval villages,” although most were not emptied immediately but over the following century. Craftsmen, teachers, and scribes died as well: many instructors and students perished, breaking countless chains by which skills and knowledge had been transmitted.

The plague also showed how society can multiply natural destruction with its own hands. Jews were accused of poisoning wells. In February 1349, Strasbourg’s authorities burned hundreds—and possibly around two thousand—Jews, according to chroniclers, before the disease had even reached the city. Pogroms swept dozens of cities in Germany, Switzerland, and France. Their households, credit networks, and crafts died with them; many survivors moved east to Poland, where kings welcomed them.

And when surviving peasants and laborers discovered that their hands had become scarce, the lords tried to hold them in place. England’s Ordinance of Labourers of 1349 and Statute of Labourers of 1351 prohibited paying or demanding more than before the plague and required every able-bodied landless person to hire himself out at the old rates. Similar laws were enacted in France, Castile, and elsewhere. This was an attempt to restore the old division of the surplus by force of law—and it failed. Wages rose anyway, while anger at attempts to freeze them, combined with a new poll tax, erupted in the English revolt of 1381. In France, an earlier peasant revolt—the Jacquerie of 1358—had been suppressed with immense brutality.

Let us sum up. Four forces destroyed in medieval Europe: war as the profession of the nobility, seizure of wealth from those who accumulated it and lent it out, the closing of doors to newcomers, and natural shocks magnified by the cruelty of a society living at the limit. Plague differed from the others in one respect: it destroyed people, but not the land, mills, or knowledge of how to use them. That is why, as we shall see in the section on the numbers, the survivors inherited more from its ashes. War and seizure, by contrast, destroyed precisely what had accumulated over centuries—and often spent the surplus on things that created nothing. Such spending is the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

On November 29, 1284, the vaults of the choir of the unfinished cathedral at Beauvais in northern France collapsed. Its builders had conceived the highest Gothic interior in the world—a stone ceiling almost forty-eight meters above the floor, higher than nearby Amiens, which was itself higher than Chartres. The vaults stood for twelve years. Reconstruction took another forty, with intermediate supports added, while the nave was never built: the money ran out. In the sixteenth century, a tower taller than any in Europe was raised over the unfinished cathedral—and in 1573 it, too, collapsed. Today Beauvais Cathedral is an enormous choir without a body, a monument both to engineering genius and to a competition that outgrew its purpose.

Cathedrals are the right place to begin because they offer the most honest example of a dual case—something that looks like waste but brings a developmental side effect. In *The Cathedral Builders*, historian of technology Jean Gimpel claimed that in the three centuries from 1050 to 1350 France quarried more stone than ancient Egypt had in any comparable period of its history. This is an enthusiast’s estimate and cannot be verified precisely, but it conveys the scale. Over those centuries, France built around eighty cathedrals and hundreds of large abbey churches. Construction of a single cathedral stretched over decades, even centuries, and absorbed a significant share of diocesan income, donations from townspeople, and collections from pilgrims.

What did the economy receive in return? Quite a lot. A cathedral site was a school: craftsmen learned geometry, load calculations, and the organization of hundreds of workers; hoisting cranes with treadwheels were refined there; stonemasons and carpenters passed their secrets to apprentices. A cathedral was also a public building—a place for assemblies, sometimes a market, a repository of civic memory; pilgrims visiting its relics brought money to innkeepers and shopkeepers. But the distinction we drew in the introduction applies here as well. A church that holds all its parishioners is functional. Vaults forty-eight meters high rather than thirty, a second tower, gilding, competition with the neighboring town for the tallest spire—that is prestige. In *Gold Was the Mortar*, American historian Henry Kraus showed how often construction stopped for lack of money, how bishops imposed special levies, and how in 1233 the townspeople of Reims rebelled against the exactions of the archbishop who was pressing ahead with the cathedral. A substantial share of cathedral funds came from people whose own homes remained cramped and wooden.

The second great stream of wasteful spending was the Crusades. From 1095, when Pope Urban II called for the liberation of Jerusalem—the campaigns began the following year—until the fall of Acre, the last Crusader stronghold, in 1291, Europe sent tens of thousands of knights and still more ordinary people to the East. Knights mortgaged their lands to equip themselves, while kings imposed special taxes—the “Saladin Tithe” of 1188 in England and France. Louis IX’s Seventh Crusade ended in 1250 with the king himself captured in Egypt and an enormous ransom. Historian William Chester Jordan, who studied Louis’s finances, showed that spending on the campaign exceeded the French crown’s ordinary annual revenues several times over: by his calculation, about one and a half million livres tournois, roughly six years of ordinary income. In return came a few decades of rule over a narrow strip of coast that was lost all the same.

It is sometimes said that the Crusades “opened” the East to Europe: spices, sugar, silk, and Arabic learning. Most historians consider this an exaggeration. As we have seen, knowledge came chiefly through Spain and Sicily, while trade with the East had passed through Italian merchants long before the Crusades and continued afterward. The real winners were Venice, Genoa, and Pisa, which transported Crusaders and received commercial quarters in Levantine ports. For them, a crusade was a business venture. And in 1204 the Fourth Crusade crossed the line from wasteful spending into destruction: the Crusaders, indebted to Venice for their passage, sacked Christian Constantinople—a city that for almost nine centuries had preserved ancient manuscripts and craftsmanship. The bronze horses now standing on St Mark’s Basilica in Venice came from there.

The third category was the nobility’s status consumption. Much of a lord’s income from the peasants was spent on looking like a lord: feasts that had to draw more guests than a neighbor’s; furs, silk, and spices brought from afar; retinues, horses, and tournaments. A twelfth- or thirteenth-century tournament was an expensive spectacle in which knights risked their lives and ruined one another—the Church even tried to ban it. Kings and towns issued “sumptuary laws” specifying who might wear ermine and who only sheepskin. What matters is that these laws did not prohibit spending as such, but a wealthy merchant dressing like a count. They protected not purses but hierarchy. The spending itself troubled no one when undertaken by the person entitled to do it.

The fourth was rent in its purest form. By the fourteenth century, according to various estimates, some sixty customs posts stood along the Rhine, Germany’s principal commercial river. Every owner of a castle on the bank—a prince, bishop, or count—charged passing vessels his own toll while giving almost nothing in return: no maintained channel, protection, or quay. The word *Raubritter*, “robber knight,” appeared later, but described precisely such proprietors. Merchants paid, prices rose, and trade shifted to other routes. This was wasteful spending in the exact sense: resources changed hands, production did not grow, while the cost of exchange did. The same category includes the monopoly rights encountered in the section on labor: the obligation to grind grain only at the lord’s mill and bake bread in his oven. The mill was a developmental investment, but the monopoly over it turned that investment into rent.

And finally, castles. In 1277–1295, Edward I conquered Wales and secured his victory with a ring of stone fortresses—Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech, and Beaumaris. Architectural historian Arnold Taylor calculated that these alone cost around eighty thousand pounds—a sum comparable to several years of the English crown’s revenue. Thousands of people conscripted from across England worked on the sites at the same time. Castles were not pure waste: they provided defense and order, and towns arose beneath their walls. But many were built not against an external enemy, but to keep the ruler’s own subjects obedient—and in that sense they were an investment not in production, but in control over extraction.

Taken together, these expenditures form a picture familiar from earlier chapters. In medieval Europe, the village surplus flowed upward in three streams—to the lord, the Church, and the king—and each recipient had his own ways of burning it: the lord had war, tournaments, and feasts; the Church, competition in spires and relics; the king, campaigns and fortresses. The difference from many other civilizations was that none of the streams held a monopoly. The Church contended with the king, towns with lords, and between them remained cracks where surplus could stay with a merchant, craftsman, or colonist. From those cracks—not from cathedrals and castles—grew what, a few centuries later, made Europe unlike its former self.

## The Lesson of the Era

Three centuries lie between William’s commissioners counting oxen and mills in 1086 and the townspeople of St Albans tearing up the floor of the abbey’s reception hall in 1381. In that time Europe doubled its population, cleared forests and marshes, learned to calculate with zero, translate Ptolemy, and send money by letter. Then it lost between a third and a half of its people to plague. Let us reduce the story to five propositions.

**First: a machine spreads where labor is expensive and the right to its proceeds is defined.** The Romans knew the waterwheel and, as excavations show, built it more often than historians of the last century believed—but Rome offers no documented density comparable to the English survey, with nearly a mill in every valley. Marc Bloch saw the difference not in knowledge but in calculation; this is a hypothesis, and archaeologists Wikander and Wilson believe he exaggerated Roman “backwardness,” but the logic of the calculation remains. Where slave labor is cheap, a machine does not pay. Where people are scarce and a lord or monastery knows for certain that it will receive the milling fees, a mill gets built. This right was not just: the banal monopoly took from the peasant a substantial share of the benefit of a machine that freed him from the hand quern. But even an unjust and unequal—yet secure—right to the proceeds of an investment encouraged investment. Rent that can be collected only after building a mill is better than rent taken at swordpoint. We will see this connection—expensive labor plus confidence that the gain from a machine will remain with the person who invested in it—again in Chapter 10, when we come to the steam engine.

**Second: freedom was not the reward for growth, but its precondition.** The Dutch on the Weser, German colonists beyond the Elbe, townspeople with charters, merchants with the *colleganza*—growth was fastest where surplus remained, at least in part, with the person who created it. Fixed rent instead of arbitrary exactions, judgment by law instead of whim, the right to leave—all proved not luxuries of prosperous times, but what made prosperity possible. Conversely, a labor-service peasant on the demesne worked slowly and carelessly, and lords themselves commuted his obligations to money rent or hired free laborers. Medieval Europe moved toward free labor not out of humanism, but because coerced labor proved a poor investment even for the coercer.

**Third: fragmented power governs worse, but takes less.** Neither king nor pope, barons nor towns could take everything. Each defended its share of the surplus from the others, leaving cracks between them—Champagne, from which merchants could depart for Bruges; a town where a fugitive became free after a year and a day; a university that moved from Paris to Oxford. No European king could have equipped Zheng He’s fleet, but neither could any king single-handedly close trade across the entire continent. Magna Carta and the first parliaments were attempts to write down what the powerful could not do to another person’s property without consent. At first they protected only a few, but the idea itself—that raising money required negotiation—proved the era’s most durable invention. Fragmentation had its dark side: the same hundreds of independent lords set up toll barriers along the Rhine and waged private wars. Competition among jurisdictions reduces arbitrary rule, but does not abolish rent.

**Fourth: an institution that begins by fostering development tends to end in waste.** A guild taught a craft and guaranteed quality—two centuries later it excluded outsiders and kept prices high. The Venetian *colleganza* lifted people without inherited wealth—then in 1297 the *Serrata* pulled up the ladder. The mill freed peasants’ hands—but the milling monopoly turned it into rent. A monastery drained marshes and copied books—then over time became one of the largest recipients of tithes and dues. In none of these cases did anyone decide to “stop developing.” Those who benefited from openness simply gained, sooner or later, the power to close it—and did. Hence the conclusion to which we will return in the synthesis: an institution cannot be judged once and for all; what matters is whether a mechanism exists to prevent it from becoming a privilege of those already inside.

**Fifth: a society without a margin of safety pays twice for a shock.** The Great Famine and the plague were natural disasters, but people determined how many they killed and what happened afterward. A peasant surrendering a third to a half of his harvest could neither build a reserve nor improve the land, and the rainy summer of 1315 turned into mass death. When the plague made labor expensive, the authorities’ first response was to use law to restore the old rates and blame the disease on the Jews. Destruction multiplied where surplus had flowed upward for years rather than into resilience.

Now for the answer to the book’s recurring question. Where did the surplus of feudal Europe go? As everywhere before the Industrial Revolution, most of it was consumed by population growth: by 1300 there were roughly twice as many people, and they lived little better than their great-grandparents. Of what was extracted upward beyond this, a substantial share went into waste and destruction: private wars and the Hundred Years’ War, the Crusades, tournaments and feasts, competition in spires, castles that kept rulers’ own subjects in submission, and toll barriers on rivers. Yet a noticeable share—and this distinguishes the period from many we have examined—went into development, not at the command of a single allocator but through thousands of small decisions: into mills and fulling mills, drained marshes, bridges, translations and manuscripts, universities, bills of exchange, and account books. What was new was not the amount of surplus—Europe was poorer than the Song and Abbasid worlds—but its distribution among many recipients, none of whom could monopolize it.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions honestly. Once again, we can measure almost nothing: the share of surplus spent on cathedrals or mills is an impression drawn from documents, not a calculation. The Postan–Brenner debate over what halted growth around 1300—nature or social organization—remains unresolved, and most likely both were right. It is easy to credit the Middle Ages with all of Europe’s future successes while forgetting that the same fragmentation and competition among jurisdictions existed in Poland and the Holy Roman Empire without producing rapid growth there. Finally, eastern Europe took the opposite path after the plague: where lords were stronger and towns weaker, peasants were bound to the land anew in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. The same shock can push a society toward free labor or renewed extraction—and the outcome depends on who holds more power at that moment.

What exactly the survivors inherited after the plague, how much their labor was worth, how many people lived in Europe at different stages of this thousand-year period, and how much their lifespans increased—all this is covered in the section on the numbers.

## The Numbers

Medieval Europe left more records than Rome, but fewer than Song China: estate surveys, monastic accounts, tax rolls, and market prices. England has been studied best, so many of the figures below refer to it. All are estimates, and disagreements among specialists are noted.

**Population.** Colin McEvedy and Richard Jones calculated that Europe had just over thirty million inhabitants around the year 1000, seventy to eighty million by 1300, and about sixty million by 1400, after the plague and subsequent epidemics. As we have seen, England had between one and a half and two and a half million people at the time of Domesday Book. Broadberry, Campbell, and their coauthors estimate that by the end of the thirteenth century this had risen to about four and a half to five million. Poll-tax rolls from 1377 suggest a population of only two and a half to three million. By most estimates, England did not regain its former level until the seventeenth century. The largest cities on the eve of the plague—Paris, with roughly two hundred thousand, though estimates vary widely, and Venice, Florence, and Milan, with about one hundred thousand each—remained islands: fewer than one European in ten lived in a town.

**Harvest and land.** According to Slicher van Bath, each grain sown yielded three or four. That was enough to feed the cultivator and his family and a few others—those who prayed, fought, traded, and built. The entire medieval pyramid rested on the difference between the four grains harvested and the one that had to be returned to the earth.

**Income per person.** Angus Maddison estimated average income in Western Europe around the year 1000 at roughly 430 “1990 international dollars”—less than in the Roman Empire at its peak, Song China, or the Abbasid Caliphate. By 1500, his figure rises to about 770, with Europe surpassing China for the first time. Broadberry and his coauthors put England at about 750 dollars in the 1270s and find growth almost completely halted by 1300. Then came the paradox discussed in this chapter: after the plague, by the same calculations, per capita income in England rose to roughly one thousand dollars by the beginning of the fifteenth century. The total pie shrank, but the number of mouths shrank still more.

**Payment for labor.** The classic series compiled by Henry Phelps Brown and Sheila Hopkins (1956) shows what a construction worker in southern England could buy with his daily wage. In the second half of the fifteenth century, his real wage was about twice its pre-plague level, and according to this series English workers did not attain that level again until the nineteenth century. In *A Farewell to Alms*, Gregory Clark recalculated the data and smoothed the picture, but did not change it: labor became more expensive after the plague despite the statutes of laborers.

**Machines and stone.** About 5,600 watermills in England alone in 1086, according to Margaret Hodgen’s count—roughly one for every fifty families. Jean Gimpel claimed that in the three centuries from 1050 to 1350 France quarried more stone than ancient Egypt had in any comparable period: by his count, eighty cathedrals, around five hundred large churches, and tens of thousands of parish churches. This is an author’s estimate, not a precise calculation. The expenditure on wheels and spires cannot be compared directly—it is an impression, not a count—but apparently more went into the stone of churches than into mill wheels; the orders of magnitude appear in “Accounting in Grain and Lives.”

**Knowledge.** Around 1200, Europe had only a few universities—Bologna, Paris, and Oxford; according to compilations by historians of universities, by 1500 there were more than sixty. Eltjo Buringh and Jan Luiten van Zanden (2009) showed that manuscript production per capita in Western Europe rose severalfold between the eleventh and fifteenth centuries—even before the advent of the printing press.

**Money and debt.** If Giovanni Villani is to be believed, by the 1340s King Edward III owed the Florentine banks of Bardi and Peruzzi around one and a half million florins. Edwin Hunt (1990) considers this figure greatly exaggerated, but both firms collapsed all the same: the era’s most advanced financial houses were ruined by loans made for war.

**Famine and pestilence.** The Great Famine of 1315–1317 killed, by William Chester Jordan’s estimate, between five and ten percent of the inhabitants of affected regions. The Black Death of 1347–1351 killed between a third and half of Europe’s population, or around sixty percent by Ole Benedictow’s estimate.

**Length of life.** Direct evidence is scarce. Reconstructions from estate surveys and monastic records yield a life expectancy at birth of twenty-five to thirty-five years; as everywhere before the nineteenth century, infant mortality pulled it down. John Hatcher, who studied records of fifteenth-century Canterbury monks, showed that even well-fed, literate men protected by monastery walls lived on average only another twenty-eight years once they reached the age of twenty—plague and other epidemics spared no one. Here lies the chapter’s most sobering figure: after three hundred years of mills, translations, and bills of exchange, the average European lived no longer than a Roman. What changed was something else—after 1350, a survivor had more bread, more meat, and more power to bargain over the price of his labor. Where this new surplus, rare for the age, went—into sails, silver, and new empires—is the story of the next chapter.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; errors by a factor of several are possible. With yields of three or four grains for every one sown and plowing done by oxen, I assume thirty to fifty hours of peasant labor for a fifty-kilogram sack of grain; a working life is about seventy thousand hours. For the first time, we can calculate in money: royal accounts survive, and daily wages are known. I assume two to three pence per person-day—one to one and a half pence for a laborer, three to four for a skilled craftsman; stone, lime, and transport are also someone’s days of work.

### Westminster Abbey

**Input data.** Henry III rebuilt the abbey from 1245 and by 1272 had spent, according to summaries based on his building accounts, which Howard Colvin edited, about forty-one thousand pounds. This was not even a complete cathedral: the nave remained unfinished.

**Labor, grain, lives.** Almost ten million pence—three to five million person-days, thirty to fifty million hours. Feeding the workers required sixty to one hundred thousand sacks, another two to five million hours of peasant labor. Total: 450 to 800 working lives. If each of the eighty French cathedrals cost no less, over three centuries they consumed forty to sixty thousand lives—between one and four Pyramids of Khufu. In fairness, that amounts to only one or two hundred lives per year in a kingdom with millions of workers, around one-tenth of one percent of all labor. What cathedrals consumed in large quantities was not total labor, but extracted surplus—tithes, levies, and donations.

**Alternative.** Here is the promised comparison of wheels and spires from “The Numbers.” A mill cost, by my estimate, ten to thirty pounds—this requires verification: Henry III’s money could have built 1,500 to 4,000 mills, between a quarter and three-quarters of all the mills recorded in Domesday Book. A hand quern took one or two hours a day from a family—this requires verification; a mill serving fifty families saved a quarter to half a working life every year, and thousands of mills saved hundreds of lives annually, more than the entire abbey cost. Even allowing for repairs, which according to John Langdon cost as much as construction over the course of a decade, the calculation favors the wheel.

### The Seventh Crusade

**Input data.** According to William Chester Jordan, about one and a half million livres tournois in 1248–1254. At an exchange rate of around four livres to the pound sterling—this requires verification—that is roughly 375,000 pounds, or nine Westminsters.

**Labor, grain, lives.** Thirty to forty-five million person-days; including grain to feed the workers, four and a half to seven thousand working lives. In addition came literal lives: of an army of fifteen to twenty-five thousand, thousands died at Mansurah and in captivity—the army’s size requires verification—and each lost twenty to thirty working years.

**Alternative.** At a wheat price of around five shillings per quarter (approximately 220 kilograms), the same money could have bought five to eight million sacks, a year’s bread for more than a million people: a reserve that, sixty years later in 1315–1317, might have saved many of those killed by the Great Famine.

**Conclusion.** The abbey left behind skills, a school of builders, and a building that still stands. The campaign left only debt, ransom, and graves. Both bills were paid from the same surplus, and both were smaller than what mill wheels could have given the village.
