# Introduction

## Keynes’s Prediction and the Missing Hours

In the summer of 1930, just as the world was beginning its slide into the Great Depression, the British economist John Maynard Keynes wrote a short essay with a title of unusual calm for the time: “Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren.” Banks were failing, breadlines were growing across America, and newspapers were announcing the end of capitalism. Keynes asked his readers to look past the panic and imagine the world a hundred years ahead.

His calculation was simple. If technology and capital accumulation continued to advance at roughly the pace of the preceding decades, then within a century living standards in the developed world would be four to eight times higher than they had been in 1930. If so, Keynes reasoned, humanity would solve the “economic problem”—the problem of securing life’s basic necessities—for the first time in its history. People would need to work only about three hours a day, fifteen hours a week, to meet their needs. Their chief concern would no longer be survival, but how to fill their leisure and live wisely and well.

Almost a hundred years have passed. Let us test the prediction.

The first half came true with room to spare. According to estimates compiled by the Maddison Project Database—begun by the British economic historian Angus Maddison and continued after his death by colleagues at the University of Groningen—output per person in Britain and the United States has risen roughly five- to sixfold since 1930. That is almost exactly the midpoint of Keynes’s range. The average resident of a rich country now enjoys things that were available to only a few in 1930: a private car, a refrigerator, antibiotics, long-distance air travel, and the whole world’s library in a pocket.

The second half of the prediction did not come true at all. The working week in developed countries did shrink, but not to fifteen hours—only to roughly thirty-five or forty—and nearly all of that reduction took place in the first few decades after the war. Since then, the curve of working time has been almost flat. Many people in the 2020s work no less than their parents did and feel as if they work more: they must keep track of email, messages, reports, and endless rounds of approval. Surveys in different countries show that a substantial share of workers doubt whether their jobs are useful in any way. The anthropologist David Graeber, who collected such testimonies in his book *Bullshit Jobs*, regarded this as one of the great mysteries of our time.

The result is a strange picture. Machines, energy, and knowledge now allow us to produce many times more in an hour of labor than they did in Keynes’s day. Yet the promised liberation never came. Where did the difference go?

One answer is that people simply wanted more. Keynes himself partly anticipated this: he distinguished between “absolute” needs, which can be satisfied, and “relative” needs—the desire to stand above one’s neighbor—which are insatiable by definition. There is some truth in that. We live in larger homes, travel farther, and receive longer and more expensive medical treatment. But on closer inspection, “people wanted more” explains far from everything. A substantial part of the increased wealth has gone not toward what people wanted, but toward things over which their wishes have little influence: housing costs that outpace wages in many cities; debt payments; the expansion of industries that create nothing new but merely redistribute what already exists; armaments; and work whose purpose cannot be explained even by those who perform it.

This book grew out of the simple question behind Keynes’s prediction: **what happens to the surplus?**

### What Is Surplus?

The word *surplus* will appear throughout this book, so let us agree at the outset on what it means. Surplus is everything a society produces beyond what it needs merely to go on living as it did before. If a village harvests exactly enough grain to feed itself until the next harvest and sow its fields again, there is no surplus. If it harvests more, a surplus appears—and immediately raises the question of what to do with it.

There are, in essence, only a few options. The extra grain can feed people while they dig a canal, allowing the fields to yield more in future years. It can support craftspeople who make better sickles and plows. It can maintain scribes and teachers who preserve and pass on knowledge. It can be stored against a bad harvest. Or it can feed warriors sent to plunder the neighboring village; builders raising a mountain-high tomb for a ruler; or tax collectors making sure that no one conceals a share of what is owed.

Ever since people learned, more than ten thousand years ago, to grow more than they consumed, every society has faced this decision anew each year, whether consciously or not. Sometimes the decision is made by a chief, sometimes by priests, a king, a parliament, a market, a planning committee, a board of directors, or millions of people each controlling a tiny share. But the decision is always made. And, as we shall see, it determines whether a society will be richer a century later, poorer, or exactly where it was before—only with a few new monuments.

### Why History?

We could discuss all this in the abstract, using models and graphs. But abstract arguments have a weakness: it is easy to believe them when they are elegant and hard to determine whether they are true. History is the only laboratory in which experiments with surplus have been conducted on living societies for thousands of years, with real consequences.

This laboratory contains everything. There are societies that stored grain in temple granaries for centuries and invented writing in the process, simply because they needed to keep accounts. There are empires that built roads used by merchants for the next thousand years, and empires that spent their strength on palaces of which only ruins remain. There is a civilization that built the finest fleet of its age and then burned it itself. There are countries that discovered mountains of silver and ended up poorer, and a marshy strip of land beside the North Sea that possessed almost nothing but confidence in its own promissory notes—and became Europe’s richest country for a century. Some societies tried to allocate surplus according to a single plan issued from the center; others entrusted the task to the market. Both produced successes and catastrophes.

This book will travel the whole distance from hunter-gatherer camps to the world of artificial intelligence. Its aim is not to list dates and rulers, but to ask the same set of questions in every era. What did people produce with? How did they divide their labor? How did they exchange? Who decided where the surplus went? And above all, what did that surplus ultimately become: new tools, knowledge, and trust—or war, prestige, and extraction?

### A Few Things to Say Up Front

Before we begin, I should be candid about three things.

First, this book has a thesis. I will not pretend to be an impartial chronicler: I believe that economic history is, to a large extent, the history of where societies directed their surplus, and that these directions can be distinguished and compared. The thesis will be stated in full in the next section and tested in every chapter that follows. Where the facts contradict it, or where serious scholars disagree with it, I will say so. Readers have every right to retain their own view; my task is to give them the material they need to do so.

Second, many of the figures in this book are estimates. No one measured the GDP of ancient Egypt or conducted a census of Stone Age hunters. When I give per capita income for the Roman Empire or the world’s population in the year 1000, I am citing reconstructions by historians, built from tax rolls, skeletal remains, grain prices, and hundreds of other indirect clues. Such estimates may be wrong by a factor of one and a half or two. But they remain useful if we do not demand single-unit precision: they show orders of magnitude and directions of change well. I will always identify whose estimate I am using.

Third, I am not an economist proposing a new theory, nor a historian who has discovered new archives. Almost everything on which this book rests has long been known to specialists and set out in works I will name along the way. What is new is only the angle of view: an attempt to look at ten thousand years of economic history through a single lens and see what that lens reveals—and what it does not.

One request to the reader. As you read about Sumerian scribes, Roman legions, Hanseatic merchants, and Soviet planners, keep Keynes’s prediction and the missing hours in mind. We live in an age when machines are poised to take over an even greater share of human work. That means the question of where the surplus goes will become more important in the coming decades, not less. History offers no ready-made answer. But it does show us clearly which answers have already been tried and how they ended.

## The Thesis: Where the Surplus Goes

I will state the book’s central idea at once, in a single paragraph, so that we can return to it throughout:

> An economy advances when most people gain a greater capacity to produce, learn, and exchange, and it stalls when resources are absorbed by extraction, prestige, and control. Wealth is created not by accumulation in itself, but by what the surplus is invested in: tools, knowledge, and trust—or palaces, wars, and rents.

This claim has three parts, and each deserves to be examined separately.

### Not How Much, but What It Becomes

The first part may seem obvious, but it challenges a very old and persistent habit of thought. For centuries, wealth was equated with stocks: gold in a treasury, grain in granaries, cattle in herds, land in one’s possession. The rulers of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries seriously believed that a country grew richer if it imported more precious metal than it exported. Later we will see how this worked out for Spain, through whose hands mountains of American silver passed—and why, by the end of that story, it had fallen behind neighbors that possessed no silver at all.

A stock produces nothing by itself. Mice will eat grain in a granary if it is never put to use; gold in a cellar will not plow a field. Wealth in the sense that matters to living people is the ability to produce more tomorrow than today with the same amount of effort. That ability arises only when part of today’s surplus becomes something that makes tomorrow’s work easier: a better tool, a trained craftsperson, a road, recorded knowledge, or a rule people can rely on.

That is why, in this book, the question “How rich is this society?” will almost always yield to another: “What does it invest its output beyond bare necessity in?” Two societies with identical harvests may find themselves in entirely different places a century later, depending on whether their surplus feeds the builders of canals or the builders of tombs.

### The Capabilities of the Majority

The second part of the thesis concerns *whose* capabilities expand. Monuments make it easy to misjudge. The Egyptian pyramids, Roman villas, and palaces of Versailles convey an impression of extraordinary wealth—and they truly were wealth, but wealth concentrated among a very narrow circle. The peasant who fed the builders of Versailles lived much as his ancestors had several centuries before.

Economic historians have long noted an uncomfortable fact: for most of history after the advent of agriculture, the living standards of the *ordinary* person rose very slowly or not at all. In 1798, the English clergyman and economist Thomas Malthus explained this as follows: any improvement in food production is soon “consumed” by population growth, and society returns to its former poverty line, only with more people. This pattern is known as the Malthusian trap. The American economist Gregory Clark went further in *A Farewell to Alms*. Drawing on the wage and price data he had assembled, he estimated that the average English person around 1800 was materially not much better off than the average hunter-gatherer. That estimate is disputed, and we will return to the debate in the opening chapters. But the broader picture—millennia during which living standards for the majority scarcely moved—is accepted by almost everyone.

Yet a surplus existed throughout that time. There was enough for temples, armies, courts, and aristocratic luxury. The problem, then, was not only that the surplus was small, but *where* it went and *who* decided. I will test the following conclusion in every chapter: sustained growth began when the surplus ceased to be concentrated in the hands of a few who spent it on prestige and control, and began to spread in forms that expanded the capabilities of many—literacy, affordable tools, secure rights to the fruits of one’s labor, and inexpensive exchange.

### Extraction, Prestige, and Control

The third part of the thesis names the three main “drains” through which surplus disappears without creating future growth.

**Extraction** occurs when surplus is taken from those who produced it in exchange for nothing, or for far less than its value. Plunder, tribute, arbitrary taxation, confiscation, and—in subtler forms—rent: income received not for creating something, but for controlling something others cannot do without. Land, a river crossing, a license, a monopoly. Economists call the pursuit of such income *rent-seeking*. Extraction has an important property: it does not merely transfer wealth from one pair of hands to another; it also undermines incentives. If the harvest will be taken anyway, why struggle to grow more?

**Prestige** means spending whose main purpose is to display status—one’s own, a ruler’s, or a state’s. Monuments far beyond any practical need, luxury, competition in the display of wealth. As early as 1899, the American economist Thorstein Veblen called this “conspicuous consumption.” Prestige spending is not always useless: it may sometimes strengthen cohesion or accidentally give birth to new technologies, and the next section will consider such ambiguous cases. As a rule, however, it consumes resources without adding to a society’s productive capacity.

**Control** means the resources spent to keep people subordinate and monitor them: overseers, guards, tax collectors, expanding systems of inspection and approval. Some control is necessary. Without rules and enforcement there is no trust, and without trust there is no exchange. But a point comes when the apparatus of control begins to serve itself rather than society, and every additional unit of resources invested in it produces not more order, but more friction.

### A Measure That Runs Through the Book

If the thesis is correct, then every era permits a rough but useful question: what share of its surplus went toward development, and what share toward extraction, prestige, and control? An exact calculation will almost never be possible. We do not possess the complete ledgers of Babylon or Rome, while modern statistics are organized quite differently from what such a calculation would require. But even an approximate answer—“most of it,” “a substantial share,” “a negligible fraction”—can explain a great deal. This share will serve as a measure running through the book. In every chapter I will attempt at least a rough estimate, marking honestly where the data ends and judgment begins.

### The Objections

A thesis that cannot be challenged is worthless. I will therefore set out the main objections now; we will return to each of them later.

*First objection: consumption is itself the goal.* Why invest the surplus in future growth at all if people can simply live better today? Good food, a celebration, a beautiful home—are these “waste”? No. This book does not advocate asceticism. Consumption by the majority is precisely what makes growth worthwhile. I will call spending wasteful not when people enjoy life, but when resources go toward something that improves life neither now nor later—or improves life for a very few at the expense of the many.

*Second objection: geography and chance decide, not people.* In *Guns, Germs, and Steel*, the American geographer and biologist Jared Diamond made a compelling case that the orientation of continents, the availability of domesticable plants and animals, climate, and disease powerfully shaped which societies developed first. This is true, and I will take it into account. But geography sets the starting conditions, not the outcome: under the same natural conditions, some societies grew rich and others did not, while the same countries rose in one century and declined in another.

*Third objection: there is no way to know in advance what will foster development.* This is perhaps the most serious objection. Many discoveries emerged from spending that seemed pointless to contemporaries: astronomy grew from astrology, precision mechanics from mechanical toys at court, and computers and the internet from military programs. If societies had spent their surplus only on what was “obviously useful,” they might have missed what mattered most. I will not pretend that this problem has an easy solution. But it does not follow that all spending is equal. It means that diversity and openness matter: society benefits when many people can try many different things, rather than when a single ruler decides for everyone what deserves resources.

*Fourth objection: “what the surplus is invested in” is too simple.* Economists explain growth through complex combinations of causes: institutions—the rules by which a society lives, emphasized by Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson in *Why Nations Fail*; culture and ideas, in the work of Joel Mokyr and Deirdre McCloskey; the prices of labor and energy, in Robert Allen’s work; geography and access to resources, in Kenneth Pomeranz’s. My thesis does not displace these explanations. It proposes a common question to which many of them can be reduced: each of these causes, in one way or another, affects where the surplus is ultimately directed. By the end of the book, readers can judge for themselves how fair that reduction is and where it breaks down.

Now that the thesis has been stated, we need a tool for applying it to particular eras. That tool will consist of three categories: developmental, destructive, and wasteful.

## Three Categories: Developmental, Destructive, and Wasteful

Imagine an accountant asked to audit not one company, but an entire civilization over several centuries. There are no income statements, no statistics, not even an accurate census. What the accountant does have is what time has left behind: canals and ruins, tax tablets and promissory notes, skeletons in cemeteries, traces of fire in layers of soil. How can all of this be entered in a ledger?

I propose a simple table with three columns. We can try to place every major act of a society—a construction project, a war, a reform, an invention, a new tax—in one of them.

### Developmental

I call developmental anything that increases a society’s ability to live better tomorrow than it does today. There are several signs of such an action, and any one of them is sufficient.

First, output per hour of labor rises. A heavy plow, a watermill, a steam engine, a tractor, a computer: a person equipped with one produces more in the same amount of time. Second, knowledge accumulates in a form that can be passed on to others: writing, a calendar, a recipe for smelting iron, the scientific method, a textbook. Third, access to benefits expands: something once available to a few becomes available to many, as books did after the invention of the printing press and clean water after cities built public water systems. Fourth, resilience increases: society becomes better able to survive a failed harvest, an epidemic, or a war because it has reserves, roads along which grain can be delivered, hospitals, and insurance mechanisms.

This category also includes something less visible but perhaps most important of all: trust between strangers. When a merchant in one city can provide goods on credit to a merchant in another and be confident that the debt will be repaid, exchange becomes cheaper and therefore more abundant. Economists call the costs of finding a partner, negotiating an agreement, and ensuring that it is honored *transaction costs*. Law, courts, standard weights and measures, reliable money, and reputation are all ways of reducing those costs. They are developmental investments even if they leave not a single building behind.

### Destructive

I call destructive anything that destroys what has already been accumulated: capital, knowledge, trust, or people themselves.

The most obvious example is war. It burns cities, tramples fields, and kills those who knew how to work and teach. But destruction can be quiet too. When a ruler “debases” the coinage—mixing ever more copper into silver in order to mint more coins from the same quantity of precious metal—no one dies, but people lose trust in money and exchange begins to seize up. When prices in Germany in 1923 rose so quickly that wages were paid several times a week or even daily, and people ran out to spend them immediately, it was not buildings that were destroyed but the savings of an entire middle class and its faith in the state—with consequences the world would feel ten years later.

Expelling people can also be destructive. In 1685, King Louis XIV of France revoked the Edict of Nantes, which had guaranteed rights to Huguenot Protestants. According to widely cited estimates, about two hundred thousand people left the country, among them large numbers of craftspeople, merchants, silk weavers, and watchmakers. Their skills traveled with them—to England, the Netherlands, Prussia, and Switzerland—and benefited other economies instead. France did not lose a single field or workshop. It lost the people who knew how to work in them.

One qualification is needed here. The Austrian economist Joseph Schumpeter popularized the phrase “creative destruction”: new technologies and companies displace old ones, a painful process through which the economy renews itself. When automobiles displaced coachmen and digital photography displaced film, entire occupations and firms disappeared. But in the terms of this book, that is not destruction: society’s productive capacity ultimately increased rather than diminished. I will use *destructive* only for changes that leave society with *fewer* capabilities than before—fewer people, fewer skills, less trust, fewer functioning machines and fields. The pain of those whose trades lose their value is entirely real, however, and we will repeatedly encounter the question of who pays for renewal, from the English Luddite weavers to today’s debates about artificial intelligence.

### Wasteful

The third category is the most interesting and the most contentious. I call spending wasteful when it consumes resources without increasing either productivity or the well-being of the majority. Nothing has been destroyed, but nothing has been created either. It is like an engine idling: fuel burns while the vehicle stands still.

The classic example is an arms race that ends with roughly the same balance of power with which it began. In the first decade of the twentieth century, Britain and Germany competed to build a new kind of battleship, the dreadnought. Each side built more because the other side was building more. Enormous sums went into these steel giants, yet in 1914 the British fleet remained, as before, substantially stronger than the German one. In the great war that followed, the main battle fleets met in a full-scale engagement only once—at Jutland in 1916—and the battle did not alter the overall situation at sea. Biologists call such situations the “Red Queen effect,” after the Lewis Carroll character who had to run as fast as she could merely to stay in the same place.

Another example is rent-seeking, discussed in the previous section. The American economist Anne Krueger, who brought the term into wide use in 1974, studied countries where importing goods required licenses. She observed that firms spent enormous amounts of effort and money not on production but on obtaining those licenses: cultivating connections, offering gifts, hiring lawyers and intermediaries. From the perspective of an individual firm, this was rational—a license generated income. From society’s perspective, the effort was a pure loss: it did not create more licenses; it merely awarded them to whoever was best at obtaining them. The labor of intelligent and energetic people burned up in a struggle to divide the pie rather than bake it.

The category also includes prestige construction beyond any useful function, bureaucracy that exists for its own sake, status consumption, and redistribution in which resources merely travel in circles while paying numerous intermediaries along the way. The distinction between waste and destruction matters. After wasteful spending, society remains where it was; only an opportunity has been lost. Economists call this an *opportunity cost*: the value of what the same resources could have accomplished if used differently. A pyramid cannot be accused of breaking anything. It can only be judged against what those same millions of person-days of labor might have become.

### Ambiguous Cases

If history fitted neatly into three columns, this book would be shorter. In reality, many of its most important events lie along the boundaries between them.

Consider an example to which we will return in Chapter 3. More than five thousand years ago, temples in the Sumerian city of Uruk collected enormous quantities of grain, livestock, and textiles. This was partly extraction from peasants and partly a means of conferring prestige on the gods and their servants. But to keep track of all those goods, temple officials needed a recording system. First came clay tokens and impressions in clay, then signs representing objects and numbers. From this accounting, writing gradually emerged—one of the most developmental inventions in human history. A system that was largely extractive and prestige-driven produced a by-product whose value surpassed everything stored in its granaries.

There are many similar cases. A number of the twentieth century’s key technologies—the jet engine, radar, the first electronic computers, rockets, ARPANET, from which the internet grew, and satellite navigation—were born in military programs or through their direct funding. The Soviet-American space race was largely a prestige contest: its primary aim was to demonstrate the superiority of one system to the world. But it also gave us satellite communications, weather satellites, new materials, and an entire generation of engineers.

Does that mean war and prestige “pay for themselves”? We must be careful. To call a military program developmental, it is not enough to show that it produced something. We must ask what the same money and the same minds would have produced if directed toward those goals from the outset. It is impossible to answer with complete confidence—history cannot be replayed—but the question must always be asked. Otherwise, any expenditure can be justified by pointing to something invented along the way.

The reverse can happen too: what appeared to be unambiguous development may end in destruction. Beginning in the 1960s, Soviet planners sharply expanded cotton cultivation in Central Asia and diverted water from the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to the fields. New canals, newly irrigated land—by every sign, a classic developmental investment, the same irrigation with which civilization began. But the rivers surrendered to the fields almost ceased to reach the Aral Sea. Over several decades, what had once been one of the world’s largest lakes broke apart into separate saline bodies of water. The fishing industry died, winds carried salt and dust from the exposed lakebed, and public health in the region deteriorated sharply. An investment that increased production in the short term destroyed the natural capital of an entire region in the long term.

### How to Keep the Categories Straight

These examples suggest three rules that I will follow from this point on.

First, the judgment depends on the time horizon. What looks developmental over five years may prove destructive over fifty, and vice versa. I will therefore try to follow consequences at least two or three generations into the future—something easier for a historian than for a contemporary observer.

Second, the judgment depends on whose capabilities we count. A raid that enriched one people impoverished another. A monopoly that enriched one company made a product more expensive for millions of buyers. I will measure the overall result and, above all, the position of the majority rather than the success of the victors.

Third, the same action may belong to different categories at different doses. A little control creates order; too much suffocates. A little monumental construction unites society around common symbols; too much exhausts it. The question is therefore almost never “Is this good?” It is almost always “How much, and at whose expense?”

The three columns are a crude instrument, and I will not pretend that every event in history can be assigned to one of them without ambiguity. But a crude instrument is better than none. To make it more precise, we need measures that allow us to compare eras. Those are the subject of the final section of this introductory part.

## Five Measures and How to Read This Book

When a doctor examines a patient, no single measurement is enough. A seriously ill person may have a normal temperature, while a healthy person who has just climbed a flight of stairs may have a rapid pulse. The doctor therefore considers several signs together and judges their combination. The same is true of societies. The best-known measure of an economy—gross domestic product, or GDP, the market value of everything a country produces in a year—is useful but deceptive when viewed in isolation. GDP rises when a country builds hospitals, and it rises when the country builds tanks. It rises when people buy books, and it rises when they pay lawyers to pursue endless litigation against one another. A fire followed by the reconstruction of a city will temporarily raise GDP too. For the question this book asks—what did the surplus become?—that single number is not enough.

I will therefore use five measures. None is exact, especially for the distant past, but taken together they provide a reasonably reliable picture.

### Measure One: How Much Can One Person Produce?

The first question is productivity: how many goods can one hour of human labor create, and how much energy can one person put to work?

Here, energy is not a metaphor. A hunter-gatherer had essentially the strength of their own muscles and the heat of a fire. A farmer with an ox gained the aid of an animal many times stronger than a human. A miller with a waterwheel commanded the force of a river. A worker beside a steam engine used the energy of coal accumulated over millions of years. In *Energy and Civilization*, the Czech-Canadian scholar Vaclav Smil traces this staircase and shows that a resident of a rich country today consumes—through transportation, heating, electricity, and the industries that make their possessions—dozens of times more energy than they receive from food. Without noticing it, each of us commands the equivalent of an entire crew of invisible workers.

This measure captures developmental investment well: the plow, the mill, the locomotive, and the power plant all appear as steps on the staircase. But it has a blind spot. It says nothing about who enjoys the fruits of higher productivity, and it does not distinguish energy spent on a tractor from energy spent on a battleship.

### Measure Two: What Does a Society Know, and Can It Pass That Knowledge On?

The second question is the accumulation of knowledge. How much do people know about the world, and above all, how easily does that knowledge pass from one person to another and from one generation to the next?

Knowledge is a peculiar form of capital. It does not diminish when shared: if I teach you to smelt iron, I do not forget how. Yet knowledge is very easy to lose. A few craftspeople die or flee, a library burns, or the language in which books were written is banned—and a skill accumulated over centuries disappears. After the Romans left Britain in the early fifth century, the inhabitants of the former province largely stopped making wheel-thrown pottery and building masonry with mortar for almost two hundred years, although both had recently been commonplace.

Useful proxies here include literacy, the number of books, and the number of people engaged in research and education. According to historical reconstructions compiled by projects such as the University of Oxford’s Our World in Data, at the beginning of the nineteenth century roughly one adult in eight worldwide could read and write. Today, more than eight in ten can. This is one of the most profound changes in all of human history, and I will follow closely what helped it and what hindered it.

### Measure Three: How Does the Majority Live?

The third question is the quality of life of the broad majority, not merely the elite. The splendor of palaces, temples, and tombs is easily mistaken for the prosperity of an era, even when their builders lived on the edge of hunger.

For this measure, historians have unexpectedly reliable witnesses: human bones. Skeletons reveal height, age at death, traces of childhood starvation, disease, and heavy labor. Average height is an especially sensitive indicator. It falls when children are poorly fed and rises as nutrition improves. Bones revealed that in many regions the first farmers were shorter and suffered more disease than the hunters who preceded them—a paradox to which we will return in detail in Chapter 2.

Another indicator is life expectancy at birth. For almost all of history, it remained around thirty years. This does not mean that people died at thirty: extremely high child mortality dragged down the average, while those who survived childhood often lived to sixty or beyond. It does mean that until roughly the nineteenth century, a substantial share of children in every country and under every ruler failed to reach adulthood. Today, according to the United Nations, average global life expectancy exceeds seventy years. Whenever I describe an era in this book as “rich,” I will try to ask: rich for whom?

### Measure Four: How Does a Society Withstand Shocks?

The fourth question is resilience. Failed harvests, epidemics, wars, and floods happen in every age. What differs is how societies endure them.

Fragility often hides behind visible prosperity. In the first half of the nineteenth century, millions of poor people in Ireland subsisted almost entirely on potatoes. Potatoes produced a great deal of food from a small plot, and the island’s population grew rapidly. When potato blight arrived in 1845, harvests failed for several years in succession. About a million people died from hunger and disease, and roughly another million emigrated. The disaster was not caused by plant disease alone: grain and livestock continued to be exported during the same years, while relief came late and in meager quantities. Famine is almost never only a shortage of food. It is also a failure in the way society distributes it.

Resilience is created by reserves, roads and supply routes, diverse sources of food and income, insurance, and the ability of government and neighbors to provide aid. It costs money, and in tranquil years such spending can easily look wasteful: why keep the granaries full when the harvest is good? This is one of the traps to which I will repeatedly return. Not every unused reserve is a waste.

### Measure Five: How Much Does It Cost to Reach an Agreement?

The fifth question concerns the costs of coordination and trust—the transaction costs discussed in the previous section. How much effort is required to find a partner, verify their honesty, negotiate, ensure that an agreement is honored, and resolve a dispute?

This measure is the hardest to calculate but perhaps the most important for understanding the long sweep of history. A vast share of economic progress consists not of new machines but of new ways to make agreements: standard weights, coinage, written contracts, bills of exchange, double-entry bookkeeping, courts that settle disputes by the same rules for everyone, joint-stock companies, exchanges. Each innovation enabled people who had never met to cooperate more cheaply. Conversely, whenever trust in money, safety on the roads, or predictability in law disappeared, exchange contracted—and with it the division of labor and everything that depended on it.

### Combining the Measures

The five measures do not always point in the same direction, and their divergences often conceal the most interesting part of the story. The early Industrial Revolution in England sharply increased productivity and energy use per person, but for several decades it did almost nothing to improve workers’ lives and made conditions worse in rapidly growing cities. Soviet industrialization accelerated growth in output and literacy, but cost millions of lives and undermined trust within society. For centuries, imperial China was a model of resilience, yet by the nineteenth century it had fallen behind others in productivity. When the measures diverge, I will not combine them into a single score; that would create false precision. I will show the divergence and explain where it came from.

### How the Book Is Organized

From here, the book proceeds chronologically, from the first humans to the present. Eight parts cover hunter-gatherers and the first farmers; early states; antiquity and other great civilizations; the Middle Ages and the emergence from them; the industrial world; the contest between economic systems in the twentieth century; the present; and, finally, a synthesis.

Each historical chapter follows the same structure so that eras can be compared like entries in a single medical chart. A chapter opens with a *portrait of the era*—a scene, its people, and its scale. Sections on *energy and technology* explain what and how people produced; *the organization of labor* describes who worked and for whom; *exchange and money* examines how the result was distributed; and *power and institutions* asks who set the rules and how they were enforced. Next come the three sections for which the book was written: *what fostered development*, *what destroyed*, and *what was waste*. Each chapter closes with *the lesson of the era* in several propositions and a section of *figures*: population, life expectancy, income per person. After the figures comes a short section, *“The Cost in Grain and Lives,”* in which the era’s most conspicuous expenditures are converted into sacks of grain, hours of peasant labor, and human lives. At the end of this introduction, I explain how the calculation works.

One more word about the figures, because this matters. For per capita income in past eras, I will rely mainly on the Maddison Project, a research group at the University of Groningen that continues the work of the British economist Angus Maddison, who devoted his life to reconstructing the world’s economic history. The figures are expressed in notional dollars at constant purchasing power so that different eras can be compared. For the distant past, they depend on many assumptions, and different scholars may produce estimates for the same period that vary by a factor of one and a half or two. Treat them like a map drawn from travelers’ accounts: the shapes of the continents are right, but distances between cities are best checked.

The book can be read straight through or selectively. The chapters are self-contained enough to open at any point, but the question “where did the surplus go?” and the three columns—developmental, destructive, wasteful—run through them all. In the penultimate chapter, I will bring the observations from every era together in a single table and try to identify mechanisms that recur across millennia. In the final chapter, I will ask the question that prompted this entire inquiry: what do these lessons imply for a world in which machines are taking over an ever greater share of work? There I will offer my own hypothesis—explicitly a hypothesis, with all its weaknesses, which I will try to identify honestly.

## How I Calculate the Cost: Grain, Hours, and Lives

To say that a pyramid, a palace, or a military campaign “consumed enormous resources” is to say almost nothing. How enormous? Money from different eras cannot be compared directly, and even Maddison dollars do a poor job of conveying what a society actually paid. At the end of every historical chapter, I therefore do something simple: I take one or two characteristic expenditures of the era—wasteful or destructive—and convert them into terms intelligible in any century: bread and human time.

The chain has four links.

The first is *person-days*. How many people worked on a construction project or served in an army, and for how many days? The calculation also includes those who quarried and transported stone, timber, or metal: a quarry and a barge are as much a part of a building project as the scaffolding around a wall. This is the most reliable link, because historians and archaeologists know at least something about the size of workforces and the duration of projects.

The second is *grain*. A person who builds, fights, or serves at court does not plow, and someone must feed them. An adult performing heavy labor needs about one kilogram of grain a day—or other food with an equivalent calorie content. I use a fifty-kilogram sack as the unit: roughly fifty days of food for one person. A thousand workers consume about seven thousand such sacks in a year. If the staple food of an era was meat, fish, or tubers rather than grain, I convert it into a grain equivalent by calories. That puts hunter-gatherers and temple builders on the same scale.

The third is *hours of peasant labor*. Grain does not appear from nowhere: someone plowed the field, sowed, reaped, threshed, and transported the harvest. The number of hours embodied in one sack depends on the era—crop yields, tools, draft animals. For preindustrial agriculture, I begin with a rough assumption of fifteen to forty hours per sack beyond what a peasant grew to feed their own family. In each chapter, I refine the number using evidence specific to that era.

The fourth is *lives*. The hours are divided by the number of working hours in one lifetime. This requires an important qualification that is easy to miss.

### Why a “Life” Means an Adult Working Life

As noted earlier, average life expectancy at birth remained near thirty years, and sometimes lower, through most of history. But that figure does not describe an adult. It is the average for everyone born, including infants who died in their first year. If we use it as the denominator, a “life” becomes thirty years, a substantial part of which was spent in childhood without working at all. Such a measure both undervalues the life of someone who reached adulthood and conflates two different questions.

My primary unit is therefore an *adult working life*: approximately age fifteen to fifty, or about thirty-five working years. A peasant’s year was uneven—the harvest season alternated with the winter lull—so I assume an average of roughly two thousand working hours a year. That yields about seventy thousand hours in one working life, with a range of roughly sixty to ninety thousand. When I write that an expenditure “cost twenty lives,” I mean that twenty adults would have had to devote their entire working lives to it. For comparison, I sometimes also calculate a version based on life expectancy at birth and explain why it gives a different result.

Another distinction matters. In wasteful expenditure, a “life” is time devoted to work that created nothing; the workers themselves remain alive. In destructive expenditure, above all in war, this is joined by lives in the most literal sense—people killed or dead from hunger and disease. I count those separately and do not mix them with hours.

### A Short Example

Consider an imaginary ruler who orders a stone tomb built for himself. The example is invented, but the figures are of the order found in ancient building projects. One thousand people work year-round on the site and in the quarries. Construction lasts ten years, at about two hundred fifty working days per year.

That produces roughly two and a half million working days. But people must be fed every day, not only on workdays: over ten years, one thousand people consume about three and a half million kilograms of grain, or roughly seventy thousand sacks. Growing that grain beyond their own families’ needs would require peasants to work between one and three million hours. That is fifteen to forty working lives merely to feed the builders.

And the builders themselves? Two and a half million working days at ten hours each comes to about twenty-five million hours, or roughly three hundred fifty working lives. In all, almost four hundred adults laboring from youth to old age for a single tomb.

Now consider the alternative. Seventy thousand sacks would allow a town of five thousand people to survive two failed harvests in succession. Twenty-five million hours of labor could dig an irrigation canal that increased harvests for decades. This is the book’s question in its most tangible form: what else might have been done with the same sacks and hours?

### What This Calculation Cannot Do

This is a rough estimate, and I identify it as my own in every chapter. Each link depends on assumptions—how many workers there were, how much they ate, what the crop yield was—and each may be wrong by tens of percent; combined, the error may be severalfold. I therefore round the figures, provide ranges where possible, and state every assumption so readers can redo the calculation themselves. I take the underlying data—workforce, duration, yields—from historians and archaeologists and identify whose estimate I use. Uncertainty is especially great in the earliest chapters, where there are neither palaces nor armies, and I say so explicitly.

The calculation says nothing about the meaning people attached to their spending. A tomb might unite a community; a temple might also serve as a storehouse and school for scribes. I examine such ambiguous cases in the main sections of each chapter. Here, I calculate only the cost.

From the industrial era onward, grain ceases to be the principal measure because people no longer spend most of their income on bread. For those periods, I convert expenditure into hours of work at the median wage—the wage above and below which exactly half of workers earn—and then back into working lives. To retain a link with the earlier chapters, I also provide an indicative equivalent in sacks of grain at prices from the period.

Why do any of this? Because the thesis of the book concerns choice. When we say “the court spent a great deal,” the expense remains abstract. When we say “the court consumed as much as thousands of peasants grew over their entire lives,” we can see what society gave up. In the penultimate chapter, these estimates will come together in a single table, allowing us to compare how the cost of wasteful and destructive expenditure changed from tombs to the arms race.

But first, we must return to the beginning. Long before temples, money, and states, people were already facing the same question we face: what should be done with the little that remains beyond necessity? In truth, they had almost nothing “beyond”—and that explains a great deal.
