# Chapter 11. The Second Industrial Revolution and the First Globalization

## Portrait of the Era

Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, May 10, 1869. On a bare plateau north of the Great Salt Lake, two tracks meet. From the west, the Central Pacific has spent six years forcing its way here from California through the Sierra Nevada; from the east, the Union Pacific has driven rails across the prairies from the Missouri River. A symbolic golden spike is hammered into the final tie, while wires run from spike and hammer to a telegraph key: across the entire country, the telegrapher taps out a single word—“Done.” In New York, Chicago, and San Francisco, the signal is greeted with cannon fire and bells. A journey from one ocean to the other, which only recently took months by wagon or by ship around South America, can now be made in about a week.

In the famous photograph from that day, engineers and workers from the two companies embrace. Almost none of those who did the hardest work appear in it. The western section, blasted through the Sierra granite, was built chiefly by Chinese workers—between ten and fifteen thousand people by varying estimates, most of the Central Pacific’s entire workforce. They were paid less than white workers, and no one knows exactly how many died from explosions, avalanches, and cold. Thirteen years later, the United States Congress would prohibit Chinese immigration. This scene is the entire era in miniature: an unprecedentedly connected world built by the hands of people whom that very connectedness did not recognize as its own.

Six months later, in November 1869, the Suez Canal opened on the other side of the world. The journey from London to Bombay shortened by roughly eight thousand kilometers—almost by half—and the sailing ship, barred from this route by the windless Red Sea, began yielding to the steamship. A working telegraph cable had already lain beneath the Atlantic for three years, since 1866: news from London reached New York not in ten days, but in minutes. The price of wheat in Chicago and the price of wheat in Liverpool began moving almost in unison—for the first time in history, grain had a single world market.

Now let us move to Chicago. A city that had been a settlement of a few hundred people in the 1830s passed one million inhabitants by 1890. On its southwestern edge, the Union Stock Yards had operated since 1865—more than a hundred hectares of pens into which trains brought livestock from across the West. In the slaughterhouses nearby, a carcass moved on an overhead hook past a line of workers, each performing the same operation: one cut the throat, another removed the hide, a third severed a leg. This was not yet assembly, but disassembly; the principle was the same one that would become the symbol of the age half a century later: the worker remained in place while the work traveled to him. Henry Ford later wrote that he had borrowed the idea of the assembly line from Chicago’s meatpackers. Meat traveled east in refrigerated railcars, and a New York housewife ate beef raised two thousand kilometers from her kitchen.

Another scene: Braddock, outside Pittsburgh, 1875. The Scottish immigrant Andrew Carnegie, who had begun as a boy in a textile mill earning a little over a dollar a week and then worked as a telegrapher, opened a steelworks built around the Bessemer converter—an enormous pear-shaped vessel through which air was blown into molten pig iron. What had once been an expensive material for blades and springs became a cheap material for rails, bridges, ships, and the skeletons of skyscrapers. Within a quarter-century, the price of a ton of steel rails in the United States fell severalfold. Carnegie would become one of the richest people in history, while in 1892 a strike at his Homestead works would be suppressed by armed guards and state troops—a story for the section on power.

And now, night. New York, September 1882: Thomas Edison’s Pearl Street power station supplied electricity to several dozen buildings in the financial district, and incandescent lamps burned in offices with a steady light, free of gaslight’s soot and smell. Eleven years later, the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago would be illuminated by the alternating-current system of Nikola Tesla and George Westinghouse, and visitors would call its white pavilions glowing at night the “White City.” Another twenty years later, in 1913, the moving assembly line would begin operating at Ford’s Highland Park plant, cutting the time needed to assemble a chassis from twelve and a half hours to about one and a half. In January 1914, Ford would announce the unheard-of wage of five dollars a day.

Such was the scale. If the First Industrial Revolution was a British phenomenon that spread slowly to its neighbors, the second was multipolar from the outset: its leaders were Germany in chemicals and electrical engineering and the United States in mass production, followed by France, Belgium, Sweden, Russia, and, from the 1870s, Japan. By 1913, the world’s railways exceeded one million kilometers by widely cited estimates. In the century before the First World War, around fifty-five to sixty million people left Europe, according to Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson—the largest voluntary migration in history. The share of exports in world output, Angus Maddison calculated, rose from about five percent in 1870 to almost eight percent in 1913—a level the world would not regain until the early 1970s.

But this picture has a reverse side, and without it the picture lies. The same steamships and telegraph that connected Chicago with Liverpool also connected Bengal with London. During the drought of 1876–1878, while millions died in India, grain continued to leave by rail for export and the colonial administration cut relief to avoid “demoralizing” the starving. In *Late Victorian Holocausts* (2001), the American historian Mike Davis estimates that famines of the period in India, China, and Brazil killed tens of millions; his figures are disputed, but no one disputes the fact of mass starvation in the colonies amid global abundance. During the same decades, King Leopold II’s Belgian Congo became a rubber plantation for the new century’s tires, where collection quotas were enforced by severed hands.

This was an era when, for the first time, humanity produced more than it could eat, burn, and wear out in a single year—and did so on a global scale. The question of this book sounds louder here than ever: where did the surplus go? Into rails, power stations, schools, and waterworks? Into the palaces of the “Gilded Age” and the race of battleships? Or into extraction from those at the other end of the telegraph wire? The answer, as we shall see, is all of these at once, and in 1914 it would become clear which item had outweighed the others.

## Energy and Technology

The First Industrial Revolution was the work of practitioners. Hargreaves, Arkwright, Newcomen, Watt, and their fellow craftspeople proceeded from the workshop: they tried, broke, and rebuilt. Science helped them, but from a distance—as the general atmosphere of the age, not as a set of instructions. The Second Revolution was different. Its key inventions could not be discovered by feel: to obtain dye from coal tar, transmit electricity across tens of kilometers, or bind nitrogen from the air, one had to understand what was happening inside matter. Historian Joel Mokyr describes this shift as a transition from knowing “how” to knowing “why”: as long as you know only the recipe, you can repeat it; once you understand the cause, you can improve it systematically. In these very decades, science turned from a gentleman’s pastime into a branch of the economy.

Let us begin with steel. Steel is iron containing a small, precisely measured admixture of carbon: strong and resilient, neither brittle like cast iron nor soft like wrought iron. It had been made before, but in tiny batches—crucibles holding a few dozen kilograms—and cost as much as a luxury good. In 1856, the Englishman Henry Bessemer patented a process in which air was blown through molten pig iron: oxygen burned away the excess carbon, and rather than cooling, the metal heated itself still further. A heat that had once taken a day now took around twenty minutes. At almost the same time, the Siemens brothers in Britain and Pierre Martin in France developed the open-hearth furnace—slower, but allowing better quality control. One obstacle remained: the Bessemer process could not tolerate phosphorus, yet Europe’s largest ores, in Lorraine and Sweden, were phosphoric. In 1878, two young Englishmen, Sidney Gilchrist Thomas and his cousin Percy Gilchrist, a works chemist, found a way to remove the phosphorus by lining the converter with lime. The invention was British, but Germany benefited most: its ores became usable overnight. By the end of the century, both the United States and Germany had overtaken Britain in steel output—the birthplace of the steam engine had lost its lead in the material from which the new world was built.

Cheap steel transformed everything it touched. A steel rail lasted many times longer than an iron one and carried heavier trains. A steel hull made it possible to build steamships both larger and lighter. A steel frame lifted buildings upward: the first skyscrapers appeared in Chicago in the 1880s, with walls that no longer held up the building but merely hung from its skeleton. Steel wire meant bridges, elevator cables, telegraph lines, and the barbed wire that enclosed the American prairies. By the criteria of this book, this was a classic developmental invention: it made the era’s essential material cheaper and thereby reduced the price of everything made from it.

The second branch was chemistry. In 1856, while trying to synthesize quinine for malaria, the eighteen-year-old London student William Perkin obtained a purple dye—mauveine—from coal tar. He failed to make quinine and made fashion instead: mauve dresses became a symbol of the decade. But what mattered was not the color, but the principle. The black, sticky waste of gasworks proved to be a storehouse of raw materials from which substances once obtainable only from plants could be assembled. In 1869, the German chemists Carl Graebe and Carl Liebermann synthesized alizarin, the red pigment in madder, and within a few years the madder fields of France and the Netherlands lost their purpose. In 1897, BASF brought synthetic indigo to market, and over the following decade and a half, India’s indigo exports collapsed—exports on which hundreds of thousands of peasants in Bihar and Bengal depended. Thus the technology of the Second Revolution first revealed its double nature on a global scale: a discovery in a Ludwigshafen laboratory meant ruin for a village seven thousand kilometers away.

Why did chemistry become German when the first dye was English? The answer lay in the organization of knowledge. German universities and technical colleges graduated chemists by the hundred, while firms such as BASF, Bayer, and Hoechst were among the first in the world to establish research laboratories with permanent staffs of scientists paid not for a product, but for searching. By varying estimates, German firms accounted for between four-fifths and nine-tenths of global synthetic-dye production by 1913. Edison embodied the same idea in America: his workshop at Menlo Park, founded in 1876, was designed as an “invention factory,” where a team of mechanics and chemists produced innovations on schedule. Invention ceased to be a fortunate accident and became a budget item. This was perhaps the era’s most important technological development: the method of inventing was itself invented.

The summit of this chemistry was the synthesis of ammonia. Plants require nitrogen to grow, and although it makes up almost four-fifths of the air, plants cannot use it in that form. For centuries, nitrogen was returned to the soil through manure and legumes; from the mid-nineteenth century, Chilean nitrate and Peruvian guano—bird droppings shipped from Pacific islands—were used as well. Supplies were finite, and in 1898 the British chemist William Crookes publicly warned that without a new source of nitrogen, the world faced a shortage of bread. The answer came from Germany: in 1909, Fritz Haber produced ammonia in the laboratory from atmospheric nitrogen and hydrogen; over the next four years, BASF engineer Carl Bosch turned the tabletop experiment into a plant at Oppau operating under immense pressure and temperature. In *Enriching the Earth* (2001), Vaclav Smil estimates that without synthetic nitrogen it would be impossible to feed roughly two-fifths of the world’s present population; later calculations by Jan Willem Erisman and his coauthors (2008) put the share at around one-half. Yet during the First World War, the same process allowed Germany, cut off from Chilean nitrate by the blockade, to produce explosives. This is a double-edged case in its purest form: the same factory feeds and kills, and chemistry does not decide which it will do—the channel into which surplus is directed does.

The third branch was electricity. Its laws had been discovered in the 1830s, but for a long time it remained a scientific curiosity: batteries were expensive and weak. Everything changed with the generator—the dynamo, brought into practical form in 1866–1867 by Werner Siemens and his contemporaries: the rotation of a shaft could now be turned into current in any quantity. Events then moved quickly. Bell’s telephone in 1876; Edison’s incandescent lamp in 1879 and power station in 1882; the electric tram; alternating current, which could be transmitted over long distances by raising its voltage; and the first large hydroelectric station at Niagara in 1895. Electricity was not a new source of energy—it was still generated from coal or falling water. It was a means of delivering energy: dividing it, moving it, and switching it on where and when needed with the flick of a switch.

There is a lesson here to which the book will return. In his famous article “The Dynamo and the Computer” (1990), economic historian Paul David showed that for a long time the electric motor scarcely raised factory productivity. Around 1900, electricity accounted for only a small share of mechanical power in American industry, and where it was introduced, a large motor simply replaced the steam engine and drove the same shafts and belts across the entire workshop. The real gain came only in the 1920s, when factories were redesigned from the ground up: a motor for every machine, a bright single-story shop, and machines arranged in the sequence of production rather than around a shaft. It took the technology around forty years and a change of generation among engineers and managers to become wealth. An invention is not yet surplus; surplus appears when the organization is rebuilt around the invention.

The fourth branch was the internal-combustion engine and oil. In 1859, Edwin Drake drilled the first commercially successful oil well in Pennsylvania; at first, oil was made into kerosene for lamps, while gasoline was a waste product to be discarded. In 1876, Nikolaus Otto built a reliable four-stroke engine; in 1886, Karl Benz patented the automobile; Gottlieb Daimler and Wilhelm Maybach built a light, high-speed gasoline engine; and in 1897, Rudolf Diesel demonstrated an engine in which fuel ignited under compression without a spark. The steam engine was tied to a boiler, a stoker, and a heap of coal; the new engine was compact, started at once, and fit inside a carriage, a boat, and, from 1903, an airplane. Before the First World War, its economic importance remained modest: in 1913, the world traveled by train and horse. But the direction was already clear.

Finally came communications and transport—the forces that made the era one of “globalization.” Steamships acquired compound engines, in which steam performed work successively in several cylinders, as well as steel hulls and propellers; ever less coal was needed per ton of cargo, and ocean freight became cheap enough to carry not only spices and silk across the sea, but grain, ore, and meat. In 1882, the refrigerated ship *Dunedin* delivered frozen mutton intact from New Zealand to London—and a new export economy grew on the far side of the world. In *Globalization and History* (1999), Kevin O’Rourke and Jeffrey Williamson calculated that the difference between wheat prices in Chicago and Liverpool fell from nearly sixty percent in 1870 to about fifteen percent by 1913. This is the birth of the world market expressed in a single figure: distance ceased to protect local prices.

What united all these branches? If the First Revolution connected humanity to coal, the Second learned to turn coal—and then oil—into materials, chemicals, light, and motion with unprecedented precision. Energy per person in industrial countries grew severalfold compared with the beginning of the century, but something else mattered more: people learned to deliver it where it was needed—to a machine, a railway car, a lamp in the kitchen. In *The Rise and Fall of American Growth* (2016), the American economist Robert Gordon calls the inventions of 1870–1900—electricity, the internal-combustion engine, water and sewer systems, chemistry, the telephone—the “great inventions” that defined life throughout the twentieth century. His conclusion, controversial but serious, is that humanity never received such a collection again, and that later technologies, including the computer, changed everyday life less. Gordon’s opponents reply that the effect of new technologies is always visible only after a delay—recall the electric motor.

Where, then, did the surplus generated by these machines go? A substantial share went into the next cycle: laboratories, power stations, rails, pipes. But along the same technological branches it flowed into another channel as well. Krupp steel became guns, armor, and dreadnoughts; Alfred Nobel’s dynamite, invented in 1867 for tunnels and mines, exploded in bombs too; Haber and Bosch’s nitrogen fed fields and filled shells. The technology of the Second Revolution was neutral in the most literal sense: it multiplied the power of whoever wielded it. What societies chose to do with that power was decided not in the laboratory, but in corporate offices, ministries, and workers’ meetings. The following sections take us there.

## Organization of Labor

In 1898, an engineer with a stopwatch appeared at Bethlehem Steel’s plant in Bethlehem, Pennsylvania. Frederick Winslow Taylor, born into a prosperous Philadelphia family, had himself begun as a patternmaker and foreman, and from his youth had been obsessed with one question: how much work could a person truly do in a day? In the plant yard, laborers loaded pig-iron bars weighing more than forty kilograms apiece into railcars. Taylor broke their movements into elements, timed each one, calculated when and how long they should rest, and selected a single laborer whom he called Schmidt in his book *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911). Taylor claimed that by following his instructions—lift, walk, lower, and rest on command—Schmidt increased his load from roughly twelve and a half tons a day to about forty-seven, while his earnings rose by sixty percent. Historians have long doubted the story’s accuracy: Taylor was a gifted storyteller, and in 1974 historians Charles Wrege and Amadeo Perroni, comparing his assistants’ reports, found that the account had been massaged. “Schmidt’s” real name was Henry Noll. But the idea proved stronger than any corrections to the figures.

The idea was this: knowledge of how work should be done must pass from the worker to the office. Throughout the history we have traced, skill had lived in the hands and minds of workers themselves—the blacksmith, the weaver, the guild master. An apprentice spent years learning to “feel” the metal. Taylor proposed bringing this knowledge outside: study it, record it, break it into operations, and issue it to the worker as instructions on a card. The planning department thinks; the shop floor executes. Taylor sincerely believed this would benefit everyone: output would rise, wages with it, and the cause of hostility between employer and worker would disappear. Workers saw something else. When the system was introduced at the government arsenal in Watertown in 1911, molders went on strike, Congress held an investigation, and from 1915 time-and-motion studies were banned in government armories—a ban that lasted more than thirty years. Trade unions called Taylorism a sweatshop system with a scientific face.

Here lies one of the era’s principal fault lines. Scientific management genuinely increased output per hour of labor—development by the first criterion of this book. But at the same time it took from the worker what had been his capital—knowledge of his own trade—and with it his bargaining power. If anyone can be trained for an operation in a day, anyone can also be replaced in a day. In *Labor and Monopoly Capital* (1974), the American sociologist Harry Braverman called this process “deskilling”—the loss of skill—and saw it as the defining feature of the new capitalism. His opponents reply that new skilled occupations were growing at the same time: machine setters, electricians, draftsmen, engineers. Both sides are half right: knowledge did not disappear; it moved—from the shop floor to the office and laboratory.

The logical extension of Taylorism was Ford’s assembly line. In 1913, the assembly of the magneto, a small ignition component, was divided into twenty-nine operations at Highland Park, cutting the time per unit from about twenty minutes to a little over thirteen; the same was then done with the engine and the entire automobile. The Model T, which cost about $850 in 1908, sold for less than $300 by the mid-1920s. But the machine served by a human being revealed an unexpected weakness: people did not want to stand beside it. Ford’s labor turnover in 1913 reached around 370 percent a year, according to figures frequently cited by company historians: to maintain a workforce of roughly fourteen thousand, more than fifty thousand people had to be hired over the year. The answer was the “five-dollar day” of January 1914—about twice the usual rate, combined with a reduction of the shift to eight hours. Ford himself later called it one of his best cost-cutting decisions: turnover collapsed and output rose. The full rate was not available to everyone, however. The company’s “Sociological Department” sent inspectors into workers’ homes to check whether they drank, lived tidily, or took in lodgers.

This story matters for the book’s question. The assembly line created an immense surplus—cheap cars for millions. Part went to buyers through the falling price, part to workers through high pay, and part to Ford. The high wage was not a gift: it was the price that had to be paid for monotonous and exhausting work, and at the same time a way to turn the company’s own workers into customers. This union of mass production and mass consumption would later be called “Fordism.”

But to understand the era, it is not enough to look at the shop floor. The Second Industrial Revolution’s principal innovation in the organization of labor was made not in the workshop, but in the office. In *The Visible Hand* (1977), the American business historian Alfred Chandler showed that railways became the first truly large private organizations. An Arkwright-era textile mill was managed by its owner and a pair of overseers. A railway thousands of kilometers long, with hundreds of stations, thousands of cars, and trains approaching one another on a single track could not be managed that way: a scheduling error meant a collision. After a head-on crash on Massachusetts’s Western Railroad in 1841, the company introduced clear rules, lines of authority, and reporting; by the 1870s, the Pennsylvania Railroad had become a model hierarchy, with departments, division superintendents, and an accounting office that combined thousands of reports into a single picture. Thus appeared a new kind of person: the salaried executive, the manager who did not own the company but ran it for a salary. Chandler called this the “visible hand” of management, which in large industries displaced Adam Smith’s “invisible hand” of the market: thousands of actions within the firm were now coordinated not by prices, but by orders and timetables.

Armies of clerks followed managers into the office. The Remington typewriter of 1874, the telephone, the calculating machine, the card file—all this technology created a new mass occupation: the office worker. Women filled it to a large degree. Typists, stenographers, telephone operators—there were almost none in the United States in 1870, but hundreds of thousands by 1910. For many young women, this was the first path to earnings of their own outside the factory, field, or someone else’s kitchen. They were paid less than men in the same positions and usually dismissed upon marriage—but the shift had begun.

Who worked at all was changing as well. Child labor, normal during the First Industrial Revolution, was now being pushed out from two directions at once—law and school. In England, the Education Act of 1870 created a network of elementary schools, and attendance became compulsory in 1880; in France, the Jules Ferry laws of 1881–1882 introduced free and compulsory primary education; in the German lands, compulsory schooling had existed even earlier. Complex production required workers who could read a drawing and instructions, and states began investing surplus in the literacy of the entire population—one of the era’s most indisputably developmental choices. In the United States, where child-labor laws remained a matter for the states, the process moved more slowly: census figures show that in 1900, roughly one child in six between the ages of ten and fifteen worked.

The working day was shrinking as well. Exact figures vary by country and industry, but economic historians estimate that the average working week in American manufacturing fell from a little over sixty hours in the 1870s to about fifty-five by 1914, according to Robert Whaples; Britain followed a similar path. For the first time in the industrial age, people began receiving part of the surplus not only in money, but in time—the very theme with which this book began.

Finally, labor became global. Steamships and railways moved not only grain, but people. The tens of millions of Europeans who crossed the ocean were mostly young people from poor rural communities—Irish, Italians, Poles, Jews from the Pale of Settlement, Swedes. They traveled to where wages were higher and in doing so changed the world themselves: O’Rourke and Williamson calculate that mass emigration was one of the chief reasons real wages in Europe and the New World converged markedly during these decades. Ireland and Italy, which people left, gained higher wages for those who remained; America and Argentina gained hands for their factories and fields. From 1892, Ellis Island in New York Harbor became America’s principal gateway, and millions passed through it.

But the global labor market had a lower tier as well. After slavery was abolished in the British Empire in 1833–1834, planters in the Caribbean, Mauritius, Natal, and Fiji needed a replacement and found one in “indentured labor”: poor people from India and China signed contracts for five years or more at meager wages, without the right to leave and subject to punishment for “desertion.” By widely cited estimates, more than one million people left India this way between 1834 and 1917. Formally, it was a free transaction; in practice, it was debt bondage at best and at worst something close to the slavery it replaced. The colonial government of India ended recruitment only in 1917.

So who worked, and for whom? More people than ever before worked for wages in large organizations managed not by owners, but by salaried executives. Knowledge left the worker’s hands for the drafting room and laboratory; in return, the worker received more money and more free time—but less control over his own labor. The countryside lost people: in the United States, agriculture’s share of employment fell from roughly one-half in 1880 to one-third by 1910. And this entire mechanism led to the question that will dominate the following sections: who divides the result, and by what rules—the market, the corporation, the state, or organized workers?

## Exchange and Money

Chicago, July 1896. In the stifling hall of the Democratic National Convention, William Jennings Bryan, a thirty-six-year-old former congressman from Nebraska, takes the podium. He speaks not of war or slavery, but of something seemingly dull: which metal money should be made of. Bryan demands the free coinage of silver alongside gold and ends with an image the whole country will remember: mankind must not be crucified upon a cross of gold. Farmer-delegates from the South and West carry him on their shoulders; the next day he becomes the party’s presidential nominee. He will lose in November, and four years later Congress will put the dollar on gold by law. But the scene itself shows how deeply the monetary question touched people’s lives in this era: the choice of metal was debated at rallies as faith had been debated in other centuries.

### Gold Standard: Trust Cast in Metal

To understand the passion, one must understand the gold standard. Under it, each national currency—the pound, mark, franc, or dollar—was defined as a fixed weight of gold, and the central bank undertook to exchange paper money for metal at that price at any time. Since the pound and the dollar were each equal to a particular weight of gold, they were rigidly tied to one another as well. A merchant in Hamburg selling machinery in Buenos Aires did not have to fear that the value of money would drift during his cargo’s six-month journey. For overseas trade and credit, this was the equivalent of a common railway gauge.

Britain, as we saw in the previous chapter, had been on gold since 1821. The rest of the world joined it in the final third of the century, almost always on a wave of political success. Unified Germany adopted gold in 1871–1873, using the five billion francs in indemnity paid by defeated France. The United States stopped minting silver dollars in 1873—farmers would later call this the “Crime of ’73”—resumed redeeming the Civil War’s paper “greenbacks” for gold in 1879, and established the standard in law in 1900. In 1897, Japan built its gold reserve with the indemnity received after its war with China. That same year, Finance Minister Sergei Witte put the ruble on gold. By the 1900s, almost every major economy belonged to the gold club; the only notable exception was silver-based China.

Why were countries so eager to join? In *Golden Fetters* (1992), the American economist Barry Eichengreen explains that gold was a badge of reliability. A country that maintained convertibility promised investors that it would not use the printing press to devalue their holdings. In return, lenders were more willing to extend credit and charged less interest. Recall the Bank of England from Chapter Nine: trust in a promise had once again become capital, now on a global scale.

But that trust came at a price, and the burden was not shared equally. The world’s supply of gold grew more slowly than the output of goods, and from 1873 to the mid-1890s prices in industrial countries fell almost continuously—by a third or more, depending on the series. Contemporary observers called this period the “Long Depression”; historians today dispute whether it was a depression at all, since output continued to grow. For a worker on a fixed wage, falling prices were more like a raise. But for a Kansas farmer who had borrowed to buy land and a threshing machine, they were a trap: his debt remained unchanged in dollars while wheat grew cheaper, so each year it took more grain to repay. Hence Bryan’s fury. Silver would mean more money, rising prices, and relief for debtors. In the end, however, the problem was solved not by politicians but by geologists: in 1886, the world’s largest gold deposits were discovered on South Africa’s Witwatersrand; new methods of extraction followed, then came the Klondike Gold Rush, and by the late 1890s prices were rising again.

Eichengreen makes another important observation. The gold standard rested not only on metal, but on politics: when defending convertibility required a bank to raise interest rates and contract credit, workers paid the price through lost jobs and wages. Before 1914, the franchise was restricted in many countries, women could not vote in the major powers, workers’ parties and trade unions were only beginning to gain strength, and unemployment was not yet regarded as the government’s concern—so central banks could afford to ignore it. This was creditors’ trust, purchased at the expense of those whose voices were weak. When those voices grew stronger after the First World War, the system, as we shall see in the next chapter, ceased to work.

### London: Banker to the World

If the gold standard had a capital, it was the City—the square mile of London banks, insurance offices, and exchanges. Much of world trade was settled with “bills on London”: a Peruvian exporter and a Swedish machinery buyer balanced their accounts through London merchant banks—the Rothschilds, Barings, and Schroders. Lloyd’s underwriters insured ships under every flag. The British government did not manage all this; the City itself was an institution of trust, heir to the Amsterdam exchange of Chapter Eight.

Above all, London exported capital. In some years before the war, Britons invested abroad an estimated 5–9 percent of their national income, according to Maurice Obstfeld and Alan Taylor in *Global Capital Markets* (2004)—a share no major country has matched since. By 1913, British overseas investments are generally estimated at about four billion pounds, roughly two-fifths of all foreign investment in the world. Where did the money go? Mostly not into factories, but into railways, ports, tramways, and municipal water systems in the United States, Canada, Argentina, Australia, and India. The Argentine pampas became a granary for the world largely because British capital laid railways across them and British refrigerated steamships carried away their meat.

Did this flow foster development? For the recipients, largely yes: the infrastructure remained after the dividends had departed for London. For Britain itself, the answer is disputed. Some historians, such as Michael Edelstein, argue that returns abroad were simply higher and investors acted rationally. Others, including William Kennedy, contend that the London market readily financed an Argentine railway but hesitated to finance a young British electrical-engineering firm, and that this was one reason chemistry and electricity became German and American industries. If Kennedy is right, this is a familiar story: the surplus flowed to where returns were familiar and dependable, not to where something new was being born.

The flow of capital had a dark side as well. Isma’il, the khedive of Egypt, was both a modernizer and a spendthrift; he amassed European debts to pay for canals, palaces, and an army. In 1875, he sold the British government Egypt’s stake in the Suez Canal; in 1876, he suspended payments, and the administration of Egyptian finances passed under the supervision of a commission of creditors. In 1882, British troops occupied the country. Much the same happened in 1881, when the Ottoman Empire, after defaulting on its debts, handed a substantial share of its tax revenues to the Ottoman Public Debt Administration, whose council included representatives of foreign bondholders. Debt, which we first encountered in the chapter on temple economies, had become in the age of steamships a means of taking control of a country’s taxes without declaring war.

### Panics and Firefighters

Global credit made the world more closely connected—and more vulnerable. In 1890, Baring Brothers, one of London’s oldest banks, stood on the verge of collapse: it had loaded up on Argentine securities, and Argentina, in the midst of a speculative boom, had stopped paying. The fall of Barings could have brought down the City. Within days, Bank of England governor William Lidderdale assembled a guarantee fund from London banks—about seventeen million pounds, according to the figures usually cited—and the panic never began. Bagehot’s rule from the previous chapter was now working as deliberate policy: the system had acquired a firefighter.

The United States had no firefighter. In October 1907, after a failed speculation in copper shares, depositors rushed to withdraw their money from New York trust companies, and panic swept across the country. It was stopped by a private citizen—the banker John Pierpont Morgan, a seventy-year-old man who, according to the famous account, locked the heads of New York’s trusts in his library and refused to let them out until they had agreed on a joint rescue. The country that produced more steel than any other in the world depended on the goodwill of a single financier. The lesson was quickly absorbed: the National Monetary Commission was created in 1908, and in December 1913 an act of Congress established the Federal Reserve System, America’s central bank.

### New Routes from Factory to Consumer

While financiers argued over gold, ordinary exchange was changing too—the way a product reached a person. In 1872, Aaron Montgomery Ward, a traveling salesman in Chicago, mailed farmers a one-page list of goods and prices: order by post, receive by rail. Two decades later, his catalog and that of Sears, Roebuck and Co. ran to hundreds of pages, offering everything from plows to wedding dresses. For an Iowa farmer, this ended the power of the only local storekeeper, who had set prices as he pleased. In cities, the same role was played by department stores—Le Bon Marché in Paris, Macy’s in New York, Harrods in London—with fixed prices on their tags and no haggling. A fixed price may seem a small thing, but it represented the same decline in the cost of exchange: buyers no longer had to know how to bargain to avoid being cheated.

Consumer credit appeared as well. As early as the 1850s, Singer began selling sewing machines on installment: a few dollars a month, and an expensive machine stood in the home of a seamstress who used it to earn her living. Installment plans spread to pianos, furniture, and eventually automobiles. Here the surplus of the future—earnings that did not yet exist—was for the first time converted on a mass scale into goods for the broad majority. Development or trap? Both: the same installment plan that gave a seamstress a tool of her trade would later fill homes with things people could have done without.

People also built their own institutions of exchange from below. By the end of the century, cooperative stores modeled on the Rochdale weavers’ society of 1844 had more than a million members in Britain and distributed their profits among customers. In Germany, Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen began creating rural credit cooperatives in the 1860s, in which the farmers of a village jointly guaranteed one another and extended loans, freeing themselves from the moneylender. Such cooperatives spread throughout Europe. Trust among neighbors proved to be collateral as good as gold.

### Free Trade and Its Retreat

Finally, there was trade between countries itself. The victory over Britain’s Corn Laws in 1846 opened a brief era of freedom: the Cobden–Chevalier Treaty of 1860 sharply reduced tariffs between Britain and France, and a network of similar agreements followed across Europe. But in the 1870s, cheap grain from America and Russia poured into Europe, and landowners demanded protection. In 1879, Bismarck imposed tariffs on both grain and iron—an alliance dubbed the “marriage of iron and rye”: Prussian landowners and Ruhr industrialists received protection together, while townspeople paid for it in dearer bread. France followed with the Méline tariff in 1892, and the United States raised duties with the McKinley Tariff in 1890. Britain, the Netherlands, and Denmark remained committed to free trade. Danish farmers, ruined by cheap grain, switched to butter and bacon and joined together in cooperative dairies—and prospered. Two responses to the same wave: some protected rent, while others restructured production.

So what became of the surplus generated by exchange in this era? It flowed through three channels at once. The first fostered development: Argentina’s railways, municipal water systems, cooperatives, installment plans for sewing machines, and common rules that made trade across oceans possible. The second extracted wealth: colonial debts converted into control over foreign tax revenues, tariffs that protected landowners’ incomes, and deflation that shifted the burden onto debtors. The third concentrated power in a few hands: in 1901, Morgan merged Carnegie’s mills with those of its competitors to form United States Steel, the world’s first corporation valued at more than a billion dollars. How such giants used their power, and who tried to restrain them, is the subject of the next section.

## Power and Institutions

Homestead, Pennsylvania, dawn on July 6, 1892. Two barges move slowly up the Monongahela River toward Carnegie’s steelworks. Aboard are some three hundred armed men from the Pinkerton private detective agency, hired by plant manager Henry Clay Frick. A week earlier, after refusing to renew its contract with the union of skilled steelworkers, the company had locked the gates and surrounded the plant with a barbed-wire fence; the workers responded by taking control of the town. Thousands meet the barges on the riverbank—workers, their wives, and teenagers. Who fired first remains unknown. The battle lasts until evening; according to the figures generally cited, seven workers and three guards are killed. The Pinkertons surrender and run a gauntlet of the enraged crowd. A few days later, the governor sends some eight thousand state militia troops to Homestead. The plant reopens with replacement workers, the union is crushed, and Carnegie’s mills will remain nonunion for more than forty years.

Carnegie himself is vacationing in Scotland. Three years earlier, he had published the essay that became known as “The Gospel of Wealth”: a rich man, he wrote, was merely the trustee of his fortune and had a duty to give it away for the public good during his lifetime. And give he would—hundreds of millions of dollars for libraries, universities, concert halls, and peace foundations. This combination contains the central question of the section. The immense surplus created by steel and rails had come into the hands of a few. Who should decide where it went: the owner himself, according to his own will and taste; the market; the state; or those who stood before the furnaces?

### Trusts and “Robber Barons”

First, let us see how power became concentrated. A railway and a steelworks require enormous investment at the outset, but afterward each additional ton or journey costs very little. In such an industry, competition quickly becomes a war of annihilation: everyone cuts prices almost to zero simply to keep capacity in use, and all of them go bankrupt together. Entrepreneurs sought a way out—and found it in collusion and merger.

John D. Rockefeller’s Standard Oil became the model. Starting with a single Cleveland refinery in the 1860s, by the end of the 1870s he controlled, by the generally accepted estimate, about nine-tenths of all oil refining in the United States. He achieved this not only through efficiency—although his refineries really were the best and kerosene did grow cheaper—but also through secret rebates wrung from the railroads: his company paid less than its competitors to ship oil and sometimes even received a share of what they paid. Rivals who refused to yield were ruined or forced to sell. In 1882, Rockefeller and his partners devised the legal structure that gave an entire era its name—the trust: shareholders in dozens of formally independent companies handed their shares to a board of trustees, which managed them all as a single enterprise. Oil was followed by sugar, whiskey, tobacco, and lead. Journalists and politicians called the owners of these empires “robber barons,” likening them to medieval knights who exacted a toll from everyone passing their castles.

Was that fair? Historians still disagree. Rockefeller’s and Carnegie’s defenders point out that kerosene and steel became several times cheaper under them, meaning that much of the gain went to buyers. Critics reply that once a monopoly has crushed its rivals, it is no longer compelled to lower prices and can keep the surplus for itself. Within this book’s framework, the distinction is fundamental: as long as a giant grows by making things cheaper, it fosters development; once it begins earning income by barring others from the market, it becomes a rent recipient, deriving income from its position rather than from creation. Most often, the same company followed both paths: first one, then the other.

### How to Rein In a Giant

America responded first—because its giants were the largest and its farmers, dependent on the railroads, were the most numerous and the angriest. In 1887, Congress passed the Interstate Commerce Act, banning railroads from granting secret rebates to favored customers and creating the first federal regulatory commission. The Sherman Act followed in 1890, declaring every conspiracy “in restraint of trade” and every attempt to monopolize a market illegal. At first, the law barely worked, and the courts were readier to use it against trade unions than against trusts: a strike, too, could be construed as a conspiracy. Everything changed in the early twentieth century. In 1902–1904, the journalist Ida Tarbell published her years-long investigation into the history of Standard Oil, and it became a bestseller; President Theodore Roosevelt made trust-busting his rallying cry. In 1911, the United States Supreme Court ordered Standard Oil broken into more than three dozen independent companies. The Clayton Act and the Federal Trade Commission followed in 1914.

Germany chose the opposite path. There, combinations of producers in the same industry that agreed on prices and production quotas—cartels—were not merely permitted but protected: in 1897, the German Empire’s highest court ruled that a cartel agreement was lawful and enforceable. By 1914, Germany had hundreds of cartels in coal, steel, chemicals, and cement. German economists saw them as a way to smooth crises and bring order to the chaos of competition; the state saw a convenient partner for industrial policy and armament. A comparison of the two countries shows that the “rules of the market” are not given by nature: they are chosen, and the choice reflects who holds greater power.

### Voice and Union

While some fought the monopoly of capital, others built a countervailing power—the organization of labor. This was the era’s principal institutional innovation from below. Britain recognized trade unions as legal in 1871 and peaceful picketing in 1875. When the court in the 1901 Taff Vale case ordered a railway workers’ union to compensate the company for losses caused by a strike, it threatened the very existence of labor organizations—and in response, the unions built a party of their own. In the 1906 election, Labour sent about thirty members to Parliament, and that same year a new law shielded trade unions from such claims. In Germany, the Social Democratic Party, after surviving twelve years of prohibition, became the largest faction in the Reichstag in 1912. In America, the path was bloodier: the Haymarket affair in Chicago (1886), Homestead (1892), and the Pullman Strike (1894), suppressed by federal troops. American trade unions of the period organized only a small share of workers, most of them skilled.

Behind many of these movements, explicitly or otherwise, stood one man. Karl Marx, a German émigré who spent years working in the reading room of the British Museum, published the first volume of *Capital* in 1867. His central idea is especially important to this book because Marx asked the same question we do: where does the surplus go? His answer was that a worker creates more value in a day than he receives in wages, and that the difference—“surplus value”—is appropriated by the owner of the means of production: the machines, buildings, and land. From this, Marx argued, followed growing inequality, crises, and ultimately revolution. Marx was both scholar and prophet, and those two roles have had different fates. Many economists far removed from Marxism acknowledge the depth of his analysis of how capital becomes concentrated, how machinery transforms labor, and how crises arise from the very logic of accumulation. But if he is read as a prophet of absolute immiseration—as many followers read him, although Marx scholars dispute whether he meant only a decline in labor’s share of output—the prediction did not come true in the industrial countries of this era: real wages, as we have seen, rose. As early as 1899, the German Social Democrat Eduard Bernstein said so openly and proposed pursuing change through elections and reform rather than revolution. The dispute between “reformists” and “revolutionaries” that began then would split the labor movement in the next chapter—and divide the world in Chapter Thirteen.

### The State Insures

The most unexpected answer to the “labor question” came from the era’s most conservative politician. Otto von Bismarck hated the socialists and banned their organizations in 1878. At the same time, however, he resolved to deprive them of their strongest argument. Germany introduced compulsory health insurance in 1883, workplace accident insurance in 1884, and old-age and disability insurance in 1889. The costs were divided in different proportions: workers themselves paid most of the health-insurance contributions, employers paid for accident insurance, and the treasury contributed to pensions. This was the world’s first state mechanism that systematically directed part of the surplus toward protecting workers from the chief calamities of industrial life: injury, illness, and old age without the strength to work. The motive was openly political—to bind workers to the monarchy—but the mechanism outlived both the monarchy and Bismarck. Austria, Denmark, and Sweden followed Germany; in Britain, the Liberal government introduced old-age pensions in 1908 and state insurance against sickness and unemployment in 1911. To pay for it, Chancellor of the Exchequer David Lloyd George proposed the “People’s Budget” in 1909, with higher taxes on large incomes and land; the House of Lords rejected it, and the conflict ended in 1911 with the Lords losing their power to veto financial legislation. In the United States, a constitutional amendment authorized a federal income tax in 1913.

From that point on, the state had a new category of expenditure. Previously, the treasury had spent almost everything on the army, the court, and interest on the debt. Now, though still in small proportions, it began to spend on schools, insurance, and pensions—that is, on the capabilities of the majority. According to economist Peter Lindert’s estimates in *Growing Public Sector* (2004), even in the most advanced countries social transfers amounted to only about one percent of national income by 1910. But the direction had been set.

### Empire as an Institution of Extraction

There remains the face of power turned outward. In 1884–1885, representatives of the European powers met in Berlin, without a single African at the table, and agreed on the rules for partitioning Africa; by 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent on the continent. The institutions the metropoles built in their colonies were predominantly extractive—in the terminology of Acemoglu and Robinson: a poll tax or hut tax that forced peasants to seek work in a mine or on a plantation; monopolies over the purchase of crops; forced labor. Colonial railways were built chiefly from mine and plantation to port, not between the country’s own cities. These institutions outlived the empires: where colonizers had built machinery for extraction, local elites often simply seized its levers after independence. This book will return to that legacy.

So who decided, and where did the surplus go? The era began by giving owners of capital almost complete power over it—and spent half a century creating counterweights: antitrust law, trade unions, the franchise, social insurance, and income tax. Every one of them was won through struggle and paid for in strikes, blood, and elections. This transfer of power over the surplus from those who owned to those who voted is perhaps the chief institutional lesson of the Second Industrial Revolution. Yet at the same time, those same states devoted more and more of the surplus to armies and navies, while beyond Europe they built machines of extraction. Which of these trajectories would prove stronger was decided in August 1914.

## What Fostered Development

London, June 1858. The heat has lasted for weeks, and the Thames, into which two and a half million people empty their sewage, has become an open cesspit. The stench is so powerful that the curtains in the new Houses of Parliament on the riverbank are soaked in chloride of lime, and members seriously discuss moving somewhere farther away. Newspapers call it the “Great Stink.” What decades of cholera epidemics could not achieve, the noses of legislators do: within weeks Parliament grants the money, and engineer Joseph Bazalgette begins building a system of intercepting sewers—more than 130 kilometers of brick-lined underground channels carrying waste downstream, away from the city. The main lines enter service in the mid-1860s. Cholera, which had killed Londoners in waves since 1832, returns to the city for the last time in 1866—and strikes chiefly in an eastern district not yet connected to the new system.

This scene is a useful place to begin a discussion of what truly fostered development in this era. For its most important achievement was not steel or electricity in themselves, but the fact that, for the first time on a mass scale, the surplus flowed into things used by everyone, rich or poor.

### Clean Water: The Century’s Best Investment

Bazalgette’s sewers were still built on a mistaken theory: people believed that disease traveled through foul air, or “miasmas.” But in those same decades, the correct explanation emerged. As early as 1854, the London physician John Snow had shown that cholera spread through water from a particular pump on Broad Street; in the 1860s–1880s, Louis Pasteur in France and Robert Koch in Germany proved that many diseases were caused by microorganisms, and in 1883 Koch identified the bacterium that causes cholera. The knowledge of “why” discussed in the section on technology had reached medicine too.

Hamburg provided the clearest lesson. In the summer of 1892, cholera broke out in the city and killed about eight and a half thousand people within weeks, according to official figures. Hamburg drew water from the Elbe with almost no treatment: the city authorities, dominated by merchants and property owners, had postponed an expensive filtration project for years. Neighboring Altona, effectively part of the same urban area but under Prussian administration, drank water from the same Elbe, taken even farther downstream—but passed through sand filters. The epidemic almost stopped at the boundary between the two. Koch, who came to investigate the outbreak, made the contrast part of his proof. In *Death in Hamburg* (1987), historian Richard J. Evans showed that the disaster arose not from a lack of knowledge, but from politics: a city governed to keep taxes low for property owners had economized on something that concerned everyone.

Afterward, Europe and America began building waterworks, filters, and sewers on a scale rivaled only by the railways. In a 2005 study, economists David Cutler and Grant Miller estimated that water filtration and chlorination accounted for almost half the decline in mortality in large American cities during the first third of the twentieth century and about three-quarters of the decline in infant mortality. That estimate is debated: a recalculation by D. Mark Anderson, Kerwin Charles, and Daniel Rees in 2022 found that filtration had a much smaller effect on overall mortality, for example—but no one disputes the direction of the effect. Life expectancy in England, which had remained around forty years in the middle of the century, exceeded fifty by the 1910s. The chief contributors were not medicines, of which there were almost none, but clean water, sewerage, better nutrition, and an understanding of why an obstetrician should wash his hands.

By this book’s criteria, this was developmental investment with almost no qualifications. It raised the quality of life of the majority, not an elite; it preserved the principal capital of any society—people who had been raised and educated but had once died as children; and it made society more resilient to shocks. It was difficult to sell as a private good: clean water from a common pipe is drunk even by those who did not pay for it, and an epidemic does not distinguish between those who paid and those who did not. So it was built by city councils and states—where those who stood to benefit had enough of a voice to make it happen.

### School and Laboratory

We have already seen the second developmental mechanism in the section on labor: mass schooling. By 1914, almost every adult in Germany, Britain, Scandinavia, and the northeastern United States could read. This was more than culture—it was productive capacity. A worker reading a blueprint, a farmer reading a pamphlet about crop varieties and fertilizers, a female clerk handling correspondence: all were built upon primary school. Above it rose technical colleges—the Technische Hochschule in Charlottenburg near Berlin, the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (1861), and American agricultural colleges established on federal land under the Morrill Act of 1862, with experiment stations that carried new knowledge out to farms.

Most important, the process of discovery itself was systematized. By the beginning of the twentieth century, the industrial laboratory we saw at BASF and Edison had appeared at General Electric (1900), DuPont, and the Bell Telephone Company. Science was transformed from the chance gift of isolated individuals into an industry in which part of the profits was regularly invested. This may have been the era’s most enduring investment: it continued to bear fruit throughout the following century.

### Common Rules for the Whole World

The third mechanism is almost invisible, yet globalization would have been impossible without it. In this era, the world agreed on common standards. The International Telegraph Union was created in Paris in 1865, establishing tariffs and rules for transmitting messages across borders. The Universal Postal Union followed in Bern in 1874: a letter paid for with the stamp of one country could be delivered in another without additional charges. In 1875, seventeen states signed the Metre Convention, creating common standards for the meter and kilogram. In November 1883, the railways of the United States and Canada replaced dozens of local times with uniform time zones; a year later, an international conference in Washington adopted Greenwich as the prime meridian. To these we may add the Paris Convention for the Protection of Industrial Property (1883), which allowed inventors to protect their rights in many countries at once.

Not one of these agreements produced a single ton of steel. But each reduced the costs of exchange and trust—the book’s fifth criterion—for millions of transactions at once. A standard is trust that need not be built afresh in every transaction. And, importantly, it was constructed without a single empire and without war, through agreements among countries.

### Knowledge That Can Be Transferred

At the end of 1871, a Japanese government mission led by Iwakura Tomomi sailed from Yokohama—nearly fifty officials, from ministers to secretaries, accompanied by several dozen students. For almost two years, they traveled through America and Europe studying everything from constitutions and schools to factories and postal systems. Japan’s new government invited foreign engineers and teachers, sent students abroad, proclaimed universal primary education in 1872, and that same year opened a model silk-reeling mill at Tomioka with French equipment. Within a few decades, Japan was building its own ships and railways. It was the first country outside Europe and its settler colonies to enter the industrial world deliberately and rapidly—and it did so above all by investing in knowledge and people. In fairness, Japan soon invested in its army as well and became a colonial power in its own right; that trajectory will return in the sections that follow.

This was the distinctive feature of the Second Industrial Revolution: its technologies rested on science, and science is published. A chemistry textbook, a patent specification, or an engineering journal could be read in Tokyo, St. Petersburg, or Buenos Aires. Knowledge, in the language of economists, is “non-rival”: my use of it leaves no less for you. Societies that invested their surplus in the ability to absorb knowledge from elsewhere—in schools, universities, translators, and engineers—could reap the fruits of other people’s discoveries almost for free.

### Protecting the Worker

Finally, the institutions discussed in the previous section—social insurance, trade unions, cooperatives, and a shorter working day—also fostered development. They are often described as pure redistribution: taking from some and giving to others. But they had a productive effect as well. A family that does not lose everything when its breadwinner is injured or falls ill is less compelled to send its children out to work and can invest more in their education. Danish cooperative dairies, which distributed their income among member-farmers, enabled a small country to prosper in the global butter market. Here, redistribution did not consume the surplus; it converted it into resilience and human capital. In fairness, economists still dispute where the boundary lies beyond which generous benefits begin to weaken incentives—but in an era when social expenditure amounted to about one percent of national income, that boundary was still very far away.

And all of this rested on the falling cost of essentials. The price of steel, grain, transport, light, and fertilizer declined, and every such price reduction was part of the surplus that went not to the factory owner, but to everyone who ate bread or lit a lamp.

The section can be reduced to one thought: the era developed when the surplus went into what was shared—pipes, schools, laboratories, standards, insurance—into things that expanded the capabilities of the majority and could be used without depriving anyone else. Almost none of these investments was made by the market alone, and almost none was a gift from above: they were won by city councils, scientists, workers’ organizations, and reformers, often after a catastrophe such as London’s Great Stink or Hamburg’s cholera epidemic. But in those same decades there were very different channels through which the surplus flowed toward destruction. They are the subject of the next section.

## What Destroyed

Upper Congo, Congo Free State, summer 1903. The British consul Roger Casement, an Irishman in the service of the Crown, spends several months traveling upriver and visiting villages from which companies working for Belgium’s King Leopold II extract rubber. Global demand for rubber has soared: pneumatic tires for bicycles and then automobiles, insulation for electrical and telegraph wires. Wild rubber vines grow in the forest, and only human hands can gather their sap. The system is simple: each village is assigned a quota; to ensure it is met, soldiers of the Force Publique, the colonial army, hold women hostage. Failure brings flogging with a whip made of hippopotamus hide, burned homes, and executions. Soldiers have to account for every cartridge issued to them, and to prove that a bullet has not been wasted, they bring back severed hands. Casement records the testimony, and his 1904 report, together with journalist E. D. Morel’s campaign, turns the Congo into the first international human-rights scandal. In 1908, Belgium takes the colony from its own king. Exactly how many people died—from murder, starvation, disease, and falling birth rates—is unknown: there were no censuses. In *King Leopold’s Ghost* (1998), the American journalist Adam Hochschild cites an estimate that the Congo’s population may have fallen by roughly half, or some ten million people. Many historians consider that figure exaggerated or impossible to prove, but no one disputes the scale of the catastrophe.

The previous section showed where the era’s surplus went to beneficial ends. This one is about what it destroyed. And the first striking fact is that the worst destruction occurred not despite the new technologies and markets, but through them. Steamships linked the Congo to Antwerp, the exchange linked it to the tire buyer, and the telegraph linked it to Brussels. The global market connected a cyclist in Ohio to the forests of the upper Congo, yet transmitted not a single signal along that chain about the price paid to obtain the product.

### Colony as a Machine of Extraction

The Congo was an extreme, but it was not an exception. In German South West Africa, present-day Namibia, the Herero people rose against colonists who were taking their land and cattle in 1904. The commander of the German forces, General Lothar von Trotha, issued an order declaring that every Herero within the colony was to be put to death; those who retreated into the Omaheke Desert were cut off from the wells. The Nama then rebelled as well. Survivors were sent to camps for forced labor. According to the estimates most often cited, about three-quarters of the Herero and half of the Nama died—tens of thousands of people. In 2021, the German government officially recognized the events as genocide.

Let us also return to India, discussed at the beginning of the chapter. During the famine of 1876–1878, Viceroy Lord Lytton, a committed believer in nonintervention in the market, refused to regulate the grain trade, while the ration on public works for the starving was cut so severely that, in Mike Davis’s account, Madras sanitary commissioner William Cornish argued it was smaller than a prisoner’s ration. Estimates of the deaths in that famine range from roughly five and a half to ten million. It would be easy to lapse into oversimplification here, so let us state honestly what historians dispute. The famine was caused by drought associated with El Niño, not by railways in themselves; later research, including a 2010 study by economists Dave Donaldson and Robin Burgess, shows that by the beginning of the twentieth century railways had, on the contrary, substantially reduced mortality from crop failures by allowing grain to be carried where it was scarce. But in the 1870s, policy was decisive: the market moved grain to those who could pay for it, not to those who were dying, and the administration considered intervention harmful. The same steam locomotive could bring either rescue or removal—and the difference lay in whose lives the authorities considered their responsibility.

Within this book’s framework, such institutions destroy twice over. First, directly: through the people killed and the capital burned. Then for the long term: they destroy trust and teach both rulers and subjects that wealth is obtained by taking it from others. In a 2011 study, economists Nathan Nunn and Leonard Wantchekon showed that in those parts of Africa from which more slaves had been taken in earlier centuries, people today have less trust in their neighbors and local authorities. Colonial extraction in the nineteenth century left a similar legacy—states built to take rather than to serve.

### Wars of the Industrial Age

It may seem that the world lived in peace between Napoleon and 1914. That was true only of Western Europe, and even there only in part. The American Civil War (1861–1865) was the first war to demonstrate on a mass scale what railways, telegraphs, and mass-produced rifled weapons could do to human beings. The traditional estimate put the death toll at about 620,000; in 2011, demographer David Hacker recalculated the census data and raised the estimate to around 750,000, with a range of 650,000 to 850,000. Even the old figure was roughly equal to all the people the United States lost in all its other wars combined through Vietnam; the new estimate is higher. The South, where the war destroyed railways, cities, and farms, lagged behind the North in income per person for many decades.

Still more terrible in proportion to population was the Paraguayan War of 1864–1870, in which Paraguay fought an alliance of Brazil, Argentina, and Uruguay. Estimates of Paraguay’s losses vary enormously: Vera Reber put them much lower, at roughly one-tenth to one-fifth of the population; Jan Kleinpenning estimated about half; Thomas Whigham and Barbara Potthast, using a newly discovered 1870 census, put them between three-fifths and two-thirds, with most of the country’s men dead. Paraguay dropped out of history for decades. At almost the same time, the Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) raged in China—a civil war whose death toll was comparable to those of the next century’s world wars. Estimates of twenty to thirty million dead are commonly cited, most from famine and epidemics. The provinces of the lower Yangtze, the richest and best educated in the empire, were devastated. The Qing Empire never recovered enough strength afterward to carry out a modernization of its own—and this was one reason the Second Industrial Revolution passed China by.

What these wars taught also mattered. The Franco-Prussian War of 1870–1871 showed that a war could be won within six months through the General Staff’s railway timetables—and that the victor could collect an indemnity of five billion francs. European staffs drew a dangerous lesson: the next war would be short and profitable. The American Civil War, fought for four years and won through attrition, taught the opposite lesson, but Europe paid little attention. In 1914, millions of people would leave for the front expecting to be home by Christmas.

### Destruction of Natural Capital

There was a kind of destruction the era did not regard as destruction at all. At the beginning of the nineteenth century, tens of millions of bison roamed the Great Plains of North America, according to various estimates. The railroad split their range in two, and a new tanning process made bison hide a popular industrial commodity, used, for example, to make drive belts for factory machinery. Hunters with long-range rifles killed the animals by the thousand, stripped off their hides, and left the carcasses to rot. The army looked on approvingly: without the bison, the nomadic peoples of the Plains lost the basis of their way of life and became more tractable. When naturalist William Hornaday tried to count the survivors in 1889, he arrived at a figure of about one thousand. Still more vivid was the fate of the passenger pigeon, whose flocks had darkened the sky for hours at a time as late as the middle of the century. The telegraph told hunters where the birds were nesting, and the railway carried their carcasses by the barrel to urban markets. The last passenger pigeon, a female named Martha, died in the Cincinnati Zoo on September 1, 1914—a month after the world war began.

In both cases, the market worked perfectly from the perspective of the individual hunter: a hide brought money, while no one paid him to leave an animal alive. Ecologists and economists would later call this the “tragedy of the commons”: everyone exploits a resource that belongs to all and to no one until it is exhausted, because whatever one person conserves will be taken by the next. By this book’s criteria, it was the direct destruction of capital: a herd that had fed people for thousands of years was converted into a one-time income within two decades.

### The Price Paid at Work

Finally, destruction occurred within the industrial countries themselves—in mines and workshops. On the morning of March 10, 1906, an explosion of coal dust swept through the workings of the Courrières mines in northern France and killed some eleven hundred miners, the worst disaster in the history of European coal mining. On March 25, 1911, fire broke out on the upper floors of the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory in New York; one of the doors to the staircase had been locked—according to the commonly accepted account, to prevent workers from carrying out fabric or leaving without being searched. One hundred and forty-six people died, most of them young Jewish and Italian immigrant women; many leapt from the windows before the eyes of the crowd, while the fire engines’ ladders reached only to the sixth floor. On American railroads in the early 1900s, thousands of workers and passengers were killed each year.

Every such death was capital invested in a human being, burned to save money on a door, ventilation, or a brake. And here the same pattern appears as with Hamburg’s water: safety costs money now, while its benefits are diffuse and accrue to someone other than the person who pays. It was secured not by owners, but by legislators, trade unions, and public outrage. In the years after the Triangle fire, New York State passed dozens of laws on fire safety and working conditions—and once again, the law came after the catastrophe, not before it.

The section can be reduced to one conclusion: the connected world multiplied everyone’s power—both the power of those who built waterworks and that of those who wrung out rubber. The destruction of this era was not a remnant of backwardness that progress had not yet had time to correct; it was another application of that same progress. Colonies, wars, exterminated herds, and burned factories show that markets and technology do not by themselves distinguish creation from extraction. People must make that distinction—people with a voice strong enough to say, “This must not be allowed.” The inhabitants of the Congo, the Herero, and the starving villages of Madras had no such voice. And between destruction and development lay another category, the era’s costliest expenditure: battleships, palaces, and the race for prestige, the subject of the next section.

## What Was Wasteful Spending

Portsmouth, February 10, 1906. At the Royal Dockyard, King Edward VII smashes a bottle against the bow of a new battleship. The keel of HMS *Dreadnought* had been laid only four months earlier—the yard worked almost around the clock to show the world what British industry could do. The ship was revolutionary: ten heavy twelve-inch guns instead of the usual four, and steam turbines instead of reciprocating engines, allowing it to outrun any battleship in the world. It cost about £1.8 million, according to the figures generally cited. And its most important property was unexpected: merely by appearing, it rendered every earlier battleship in the world obsolete, including Britain’s own. Fleets that had taken decades to build became outdated within a year. The race began again—and now every ship cost more.

For comparison, the main lines of Bazalgette’s London sewer system, which saved the city from cholera, cost about four million pounds according to the figures generally cited—the price of a little more than two dreadnoughts, albeit in prices from forty years earlier. The sewers still serve London today. Most dreadnoughts were scrapped ten or twenty years after being launched, and many never fired a shot at an enemy.

### A Race No One Can Win

The naval race between Britain and Germany is almost a textbook example of what this book’s framework calls wasteful spending. Since 1889, Britain had followed the “two-power standard”: its fleet was to be stronger than the world’s next two fleets combined. At the urging of Admiral Alfred von Tirpitz, Germany passed naval laws in 1898 and 1900 designed eventually to challenge British command of the sea. Britain responded, and Germany responded to the response. In 1909, when London debated whether to build four dreadnoughts that year or six, newspapers and the opposition took up the slogan “We want eight and we won’t wait”; in the end, eight were laid down. By August 1914, Britain had about twenty dreadnoughts and Germany about fifteen.

What made this spending wasteful? Not that a fleet is entirely useless: a trading power must protect its sea lanes. Rather, an arms race is a treadmill. Each ship on one side devalued a ship on the other, and after a decade of enormous expenditure, the balance of power was much as it had been at the beginning—except that both countries had spent hundreds of millions and become more suspicious of one another. The only major clash between the two fleets, the Battle of Jutland in 1916, ended without a decisive result, after which the German battle fleet remained in harbor for almost the rest of the war. In June 1919, German crews scuttled their own interned ships at the British anchorage of Scapa Flow to keep them from falling into the victors’ hands. A decade’s surplus sank to the bottom of a Scottish bay.

In fairness, this spending also had a developmental side effect—the very kind of ambiguous case discussed in the introduction. Naval orders accelerated the development of Charles Parsons’s steam turbine—invented for power stations, but brought to maturity through shipbuilding contracts—as well as armor plate, radio communications, and fire-control instruments, the ancestors of analog computers. Yet the question remains the same as with the pyramids: would the same money and the same engineers not have produced more if they had been directed toward power stations from the outset rather than battleships?

### The Gilded Age

On land, the surplus flowed in another direction—into display. Mark Twain and Charles Dudley Warner titled their 1873 satirical novel *The Gilded Age*, and the name stuck to the era: glitter on the surface, cheap metal beneath. Its symbol became the resort town of Newport, Rhode Island, where the families of the new millionaires built summer residences that American humor called “cottages.” The Breakers, built in 1893–1895 for Cornelius Vanderbilt II, grandson of the railroad magnate, is an Italianate palazzo with about seventy rooms. The neighboring Marble House of William Vanderbilt cost eleven million dollars according to widely cited figures, most of it spent on marble. These palaces were occupied for only a few weeks each year.

The era’s most famous scene was the ball given by Mr. and Mrs. Bradley-Martin of New York at the Waldorf Hotel in February 1897. Guests came dressed as French and English kings and courtiers; newspapers counted the diamonds. At the time, the country had still not recovered from the depression that began in 1893—the same depression during which the Pullman workers struck. The outrage was such that, according to contemporary accounts, city authorities raised the assessed value of the hosts’ property for tax purposes, and the couple soon moved to England.

In *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899), economist Thorstein Veblen gave this phenomenon the name still used today: “conspicuous consumption.” His point was that much of the rich man’s spending was undertaken not for comfort, but to show everyone how much he could afford to waste. The value of such a thing lay precisely in its uselessness: gold on a cornice, a room no one entered, a ball for a single night. By this book’s framework, that is wasteful spending: resources—marble, the labor of carvers, years of architects’ work—were consumed, while productivity and well-being rose for no one except the owner’s vanity.

Here we should be fair to both sides of the argument. Defenders will point out that marble workers and cooks received wages, while Newport’s “cottages,” like Neuschwanstein Castle, begun in 1869 by Bavaria’s King Ludwig II and burdened with enormous debts when the king died in 1886, support a tourist industry today. That is true, but it belongs to the same class of argument as saying that war is justified by the demand for cloth. Builders would also have been paid to construct a school or waterworks; the difference lies in what remains when construction is finished. And proportion matters: compared with Roman or Versailles-scale expenditure, the share of the surplus spent on display in this era was probably smaller—the greater part of the largest companies’ profits was reinvested in new factories and railways. The Gilded Age was more the visible foam on the surface than the principal current.

### Money Spent Capturing Advantage

Quieter than the balls, but perhaps more costly, was a third category: expenditure aimed not at creation, but at securing a privileged position. In 1872, the *New York Sun* exposed the Crédit Mobilier affair—a construction company through which the directors of the Union Pacific Railroad paid themselves inflated sums to build the transcontinental line, financed with generous government support. To keep Congress from asking inconvenient questions, company shares were sold at favorable prices to several influential members of Congress. The transcontinental railroad was built and brought immense benefits, but along the way a substantial part of the surplus settled not in the rails, but in the pockets of those who controlled its distribution.

The struggle for customs tariffs discussed in the section on exchange worked in much the same way. When an industry spends effort and money obtaining a tariff against its competitors, it makes its product neither better nor cheaper—it wins the right to charge the buyer more. Economists would later call such activity rent-seeking: resources are spent not on making the pie larger, but on cutting oneself a bigger slice. Lobbyists, lawyers, gifts to legislators—all of this is work, often highly skilled work, but its social product is zero or less than zero.

### Failed Construction Projects

Finally, part of the surplus burned up in speculative projects. American railroads were built with such fervor that two or three parallel lines were often laid where one would have been enough—and afterward none of them could pay for itself. The banking house of Jay Cooke, deeply invested in the Northern Pacific Railway, collapsed in September 1873 and triggered the panic that began the “Long Depression.” During the next crisis, in 1893–1894, about a quarter of the entire American rail network passed into receivership, according to widely cited estimates. In the 1880s, the French company of Ferdinand de Lesseps, builder of the Suez Canal, attempted to dig a canal across the Isthmus of Panama, but could master neither tropical disease nor the terrain. It went bankrupt in 1889, ruining many small investors; the works cost about twenty thousand lives, according to the estimates generally cited, and the investigation that followed revealed that the company had bribed members of parliament.

Yet speculative expenditure is an ambiguous case and should be distinguished from a palace or a battleship. Bankrupt railways remained in place: they were bought for next to nothing and continued to carry grain. When the United States began work on Panama again in 1904, it used some of the French excavations and, more importantly, new knowledge: by then it had been proved that yellow fever and malaria were spread by mosquitoes, and army doctor William Gorgas made combating them part of the project. The canal opened in August 1914. Ruined investors lost their money, but society gained rails and knowledge. Once scrapped, a dreadnought left almost nothing but scrap metal.

The section can be reduced to one thought: the era that knew how to invest its surplus in pipes and laboratories also spent it on three kinds of emptiness. On a race in which every step taken by a rival nullified one’s own. On display, in which a thing’s value lay in its uselessness. And on a struggle for advantage, in which one person’s gain was everyone else’s loss. Of the three, the first proved the most dangerous: battleships did not merely consume money—they created the expectation of war that came true in August 1914.

## The Lesson of the Era

Half a century passed between the golden spike in Utah and the battleships at Scapa Flow, and in that half-century the world became one as never before. Grain, money, news, and people crossed oceans faster than they had once crossed a single province. The First Industrial Revolution proved that growth need not come to an end. The second showed that it could encompass the entire world—and that this made the question of who received its fruits not simpler, but more urgent. Let us reduce the chapter to five propositions.

**First: the ability to invent became the principal form of capital—but invention is not yet wealth.** BASF’s laboratories, Menlo Park, and General Electric’s research departments turned discovery from a lucky accident into a budget item. Knowledge of “why”—chemistry, electrical engineering, microbiology—made it possible to improve things systematically rather than by feel. This is probably the era’s most enduring legacy. But Paul David’s account of the electric motor adds a sobering qualification: the technology became wealth only when factories, management, and habits were reorganized around it, and that took about forty years. Invention opens the door; the surplus appears when society walks through it. This lesson is worth remembering whenever a new technology promises to change everything at once.

**Second: no one could sell the era’s most profitable investments one piece at a time.** Waterworks, sewerage, primary schools, and common standards for time, measures, and postal rates—all returned more to society than they cost, and all were difficult to convert into private income: clean water from a common pipe is also drunk by someone who did not pay. They were therefore built neither by the market alone nor by a ruler’s benevolence, but by city councils, states, and international agreements. As a rule, they were built after catastrophe—the Great Stink, Hamburg’s cholera epidemic, the Triangle fire. Hamburg and Altona, drinking from the same river, demonstrated the point more clearly than any theory: the difference between life and death was determined not by knowledge, which both cities possessed, but by whose interests governed the municipal treasury.

**Third: scale lowers costs, but the same position also makes it possible to extract rent.** Rockefeller and Carnegie really did make kerosene and steel cheap. But a giant that has crushed its rivals can stop lowering prices and begin collecting tribute from those who have nowhere else to go. The boundary between development and rent runs not between good and bad companies, but through the history of a single company. And the era showed that the “rules of the market” are a choice: America outlawed trusts and broke up Standard Oil; Germany legalized cartels. Neither choice was dictated by economics; both reflected who held greater power. In fairness, the other side of the argument must also be acknowledged: German cartels did not prevent Germany from becoming a leader in chemicals and electrical engineering, so this era does not prove a simple relationship of “less concentration means more development.” It proves only that without a counterweight, concentration sooner or later begins to serve itself.

**Fourth: power over the surplus began to pass from those who owned to those who voted.** Fifty years earlier, a worker had neither a union, insurance, nor a vote. By 1914, industrial countries had legal trade unions, workers’ parties, Bismarck’s and Lloyd George’s social insurance, income taxes, and antitrust laws. Marx asked the same question as this book—where does the surplus go?—and answered that it was inevitably taken by the owners of the machines until revolution put an end to it. The era disproved the inevitability, but not the question itself: labor’s share rose precisely where and when workers acquired organized power. The other side of the lesson can be seen in the history of the gold standard. Its stability rested on the ability to shift the burden of defending convertibility onto the voiceless—the unemployed and indebted farmers. Creditors’ trust was purchased at the expense of those who were never asked. A system stable only while the majority remains silent is stable only until the day the majority speaks.

**Fifth: a connected world multiplies every kind of power—and does not choose which kind.** Steamships carried both Argentine meat and Congolese rubber to London. The telegraph brought the prices of Chicago and Liverpool together and told hunters where passenger pigeons were nesting. Haber’s nitrogen fed fields and filled shells. The era learned to reach agreements on things that benefited everyone at once—post, telegraphy, the meter, the prime meridian. But it failed to agree on the most dangerous issue: the Hague Conferences of 1899 and 1907, the first of them convened at the suggestion of Nicholas II with the aim of limiting armaments, adopted rules for the conduct of war but did not halt the naval race. In *The Great Illusion* (1910), the British journalist Norman Angell argued that in a world linked by trade, war could not profit the victor. He is often remembered as the man who was proved wrong on the eve of catastrophe, but that is unfair: he wrote not that war was impossible, but that it was ruinous—and in that he was right. The error lay elsewhere: in believing that mutual benefit alone would keep states from destruction. Interdependence reduces the gains from war, but does not abolish it; institutions are needed for that, and the world had none.

Now for the answer to the book’s recurring question. Where did the surplus of the second industrial age go? More than ever before, it went into what was shared: pipes, schools, laboratories, standards, and the first forms of insurance. This was the best investment in all the history we have covered, and most people in industrial countries still feel its benefits today. An enormous part went into new factories, rails, and steamships that generated surplus themselves. A significant part went into the rents of trusts and tariffs, Newport palaces, and speculative construction projects. Some was extracted where people had no voice—in the Congo, Namibia, and India. And an ever-growing share went into battleships and guns. The era’s chief lesson is that all these channels existed at the same time and within the same countries: Britain built both Bazalgette’s sewers and dreadnoughts; Germany built both social insurance and Tirpitz’s fleet. A society does not choose between development and destruction once and for all; it chooses every year, in every budget, and the balance can reverse very quickly.

Let us also state the limits of these conclusions. Many of the chapter’s figures are estimates, and every one is disputed: the number of victims in the Congo and India, clean water’s contribution to increased life expectancy, whether the “pessimists” are right about British investment abroad. The chapter looks mainly from the perspective of Europe and North America, while in 1914 most of the world’s people still lived in the villages of Asia and Africa, which the Second Industrial Revolution had barely reached. And in hindsight it is easy to see battleships as the road to war; to contemporaries, the arms race seemed a prudent form of insurance, and peace among the great powers of Western Europe had lasted for more than forty years.

How many people lived in the world in 1914; how far income, height, and life expectancy had risen; how far Germany and the United States had pulled ahead of the rest of the world; and how much trade had grown—these questions belong in the section of figures.

## The Numbers

For these decades, historians stand on noticeably firmer ground than for the age of Watt: almost every major country conducted censuses, customs houses counted every bale and ton, and statistical offices published yearbooks. But income per person, especially outside Europe and America, must still be reconstructed after the fact, and most of the figures below are disputed estimates.

**Population.** Angus Maddison estimated that about 1.26 billion people lived on Earth in 1850 and about 1.8 billion in 1913. The world added roughly half a billion people in a little over sixty years—faster than ever before. At the same time came the largest voluntary migration in history: Timothy Hatton and Jeffrey Williamson estimate that some 55–60 million people left Europe during the century before 1914, about three-fifths of them for the United States.

**Income per person.** In Maddison’s tables, measured in “1990 international dollars,” GDP per person in Britain rose from about $3,200 in 1870 to $4,900 in 1913; in Germany, from $1,800 to $3,600; and in the United States, from $2,400 to $5,300. By the beginning of the war, the average American was already richer than the average Briton. Japan nearly doubled its income over the same years, from $700 to $1,400, becoming the first non-Western country to join this acceleration. India and China barely moved: at about $550–670 in 1913, they stood where they had forty years earlier. The gap between the richest and poorest large countries, roughly sixfold in 1870, had become eight- to tenfold by 1913. The Second Industrial Revolution deepened the Great Divergence begun by the first.

**Steel, rails, electricity.** In 1870, the world produced less than one million tons of steel; in 1913, it produced about seventy-five million. The United States supplied more than two-fifths of the total, around thirty-one million tons; Germany supplied almost a quarter; and Britain, birthplace of the Bessemer process, about a tenth. The world’s railways extended beyond one million kilometers, about four hundred thousand of them in the United States. Paul Bairoch estimated that in 1913 the United States accounted for almost one-third of world industrial output, while Germany and Britain each accounted for roughly one-seventh. Britain, which had still produced more than a fifth in 1880, was now one of three leaders rather than the leader. Electricity was only beginning to enter homes by 1914: even in the United States, historical statistics show that fewer than one in five dwellings had been electrified.

**The world became smaller.** Crossing the Atlantic under sail in the middle of the century took a month to six weeks; in 1907, the liner *Mauretania* crossed the ocean in less than five days. A telegram from London to Bombay that would have taken a month by post in 1850 arrived within a few hours after the cable was laid in 1870. As noted earlier, exports’ share of world production rose from roughly five to eight percent—a level not reached again until the early 1970s.

**Where the money flowed.** By 1914, British investment abroad stood at about four billion pounds according to widely accepted estimates; in some prewar years, calculations by Michael Edelstein, Maurice Obstfeld, and Alan Taylor show that between five and nine percent of national income went overseas—more in the final prewar years than was invested in new factories and houses at home. And as for distribution: Thomas Piketty estimated in *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (2013) that around 1910 the richest one percent of Britons owned about seventy percent of all private wealth in the country; in France, about sixty percent. This is the high point in the series available to us for the twentieth century. Nineteenth-century data are less reliable, but they do not show a higher level either.

**Wages and working time.** Charles Feinstein estimated that between the middle of the century and 1900, the British worker’s real wage rose by roughly one-half to twofold—after the “Engels pause” described in the previous chapter, the curves finally began moving together. Working time fell as well: according to Michael Huberman and Chris Minns, the typical factory worker’s week in industrial countries declined from sixty hours or more around 1870 to about fifty-five by 1913. This was the first significant return of the surplus to people in the form of free time—the very development Keynes hoped would continue.

**Length of life.** Life expectancy at birth in England and Wales rose from about forty-one years in 1850 to roughly fifty-three in 1911; in the United States, it was about forty-seven around 1900. Most of the increase came in the era’s final three decades and coincided with pipes, filters, and vaccines—although, as noted above, the precise contribution of each cause is disputed. In India, calculations based on early-twentieth-century censuses put life expectancy at only twenty-three to twenty-five years. James Riley estimated the world average in 1913 at just over thirty—meaning that for most of humanity, the age of steam and steel had still changed almost nothing.

The chapter’s balance sheet in numbers: the world’s population grew by almost half, steel output by many dozens of times, income per person in industrial countries by one-half to twofold, and life expectancy there by a decade. Yet the gap between the industrial core and the rest of the world became wider than ever before, while ownership within the core itself became more concentrated than ever. The next chapter tells how this surplus—factories, fleets, accumulated wealth, and a web of trade—was almost entirely thrown into the furnace of two world wars and one Great Depression within thirty years.

## Accounting in Grain and Lives

Everything below is the author’s estimate; an error of one and a half to two times is possible. The measure is the same as in the previous chapter: an hour of a typical worker’s labor. I use a rough estimate based on the series of Arthur Bowley and Charles Feinstein: around 1910, a British worker earned twenty-five to thirty shillings for a fifty-five-hour week, or five to six pence an hour (requires verification); one pound sterling equaled forty to fifty hours. An American worker in the mid-1890s earned fifteen to twenty cents an hour, according to Stanley Lebergott’s series (requires verification). A working life is seventy thousand hours. For comparison in sacks of grain: before the war, a fifty-kilogram sack of wheat cost about seven shillings in Britain and about one dollar in 1890s America.

### The Naval Race, 1898–1914

**Source data.** I count as wasteful not the entire naval budget—a trading power needs to protect its sea lanes—but the increase spent running in place. British naval expenditure rose from about twenty-four million pounds in 1898 to fifty-three million in 1913–1914; German spending, converted into pounds, rose from roughly six million to twenty-four million, according to parliamentary reports and newspapers of the period (requires verification). By my estimate, the increase above the 1898 level over sixteen years totaled between 250 and 400 million pounds for the two countries. I use 300 million.

**Hours and lives.** Thirteen to fifteen billion hours—about two hundred thousand working lives, with a range of 150,000 to 300,000. For simplicity, I value the German share at British wages; at lower German wages, the number of hours would be greater. In grain, about nine hundred million sacks—enough bread for all of Britain for fifteen years.

**Alternative.** Bazalgette’s sewer system cost about four million pounds: the increase caused by the race equaled more than seventy such systems. More to the point is the 1909 debate: Lloyd George’s “People’s Budget” had to pay both for eight dreadnoughts and for the newly introduced old-age pensions, which cost the treasury eight to twelve million pounds a year at first (requires verification). The increase caused by the race would have paid pensions to every elderly Briton for twenty-five years. The balance between the fleets ultimately remained almost unchanged, while the surplus sank to the bottom of Scapa Flow.

### Marble House, Newport, 1888–1892

**Source data.** William Vanderbilt’s summer residence cost eleven million dollars according to widely cited figures, about seven million of it for marble. It was occupied for only a few weeks a year.

**Hours and lives.** Fifty-five to seventy-five million hours, or about one thousand working lives, with a range of eight hundred to eleven hundred. In grain, about ten million sacks.

**Alternative.** Andrew Carnegie funded about seventeen hundred public libraries in the United States for roughly forty million dollars according to widely cited figures—twenty to twenty-five thousand dollars per building (requires verification). One summer home equaled four to five hundred municipal libraries. The money belonged to people from the same social circle: the boundary between wasteful and developmental spending ran not between classes, but between decisions.

**Conclusion.** A palace cost a thousand lives; the naval race, two hundred thousand. The Gilded Age was foam, while battleships became the era’s principal wasteful current. Yet both expenditures worked in the same way: a surplus that could have become pipes, libraries, and pensions was spent on displays of superiority—whether in marble cornices or the number of dreadnoughts. After 1914, the accounting would be measured in millions of literal lives.
