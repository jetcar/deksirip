# 1. About China and Brezhnev

There is a real fact here: after the break with China in the 1960s, the USSR had to maintain a large army on the Far Eastern border, and this took resources away from civilian production. It's true, and it added to the load.

But that doesn't explain everything. The priority of heavy industry over goods for people - not bread from the factory, but steel - was established under Stalin, in the first five-year plans of the late 1920s, more than thirty years before the quarrel with China. And the fact that factories did not go bankrupt and received money regardless of the result was also a rule from the very beginning, not an invention of the Brezhnev era.

So China is a real addition to the load, and not the reason for the design itself. She was built that way from the very beginning.

# 2. About “the same source, a specific regime, and not socialism in general”

Here you are right, and I admit it directly: Kornai studied specific Hungary and similar countries at a specific time, and not “socialism in general” as an idea. Generalizing from one historical case to any system without private property is a stretch, and I will not pretend that this is ironclad proof.

But the very idea that he described - that money is given to those who do not risk theirs, and therefore he does not keep track - is not tied to communism as an ideology. The same person later wrote about banks under capitalism, which are saved when they go bankrupt (2008). The mechanism is general: **if the one who gives the money does not lose anything personally if it fails, he does not keep track of where it goes.** This is not about the Reds and not about the capitalists, this is about whose skin is at stake.

#3. Workers finance their own plant.

This is a good, concrete thought, and I will answer it directly, without “everything is falling apart.”

**Where it works, it’s the same as with teapots.** The machine is broken, the workers see it every day, their salary depends on it—they will fix it. Here transparency and personal interest really come into play, I already said this about ordinary goods and the same thing here.

**Where it doesn’t work is a specific and narrow thing, and not “everything is bad.”** Imagine: a plant needs to replace equipment that will last thirty years. Today's workers must pay for it now, at full cost. And most of the time it will be used by other people - those who will come to the plant in ten to fifteen years, when the current ones have already retired or changed jobs. The question is: why should today’s people pay for a machine, the benefits from which will not accrue to them?

This is not a hypothesis. This was actually observed in Yugoslavia, where the factories were run by the workers themselves - they systematically preferred to pay themselves now, rather than invest in long-term things. And even today in Mondragon - the largest working association of worker cooperatives in Spain - they have their own bank precisely in order to resolve this issue not by the workers themselves, but by a separate structure, and still enterprises within the association are sometimes closed (Fagor plant in 2013).

So it’s not “transparency ruins everything.” It ruins a specific thing: **when one person pays and another uses it**, it is not profitable for the payer to pay. For dummies this is not a problem, because today’s worker pays and uses it. For a thirty-year-old machine, this is a problem, because one person pays, another uses it.

# 4. “It doesn’t come in - we stopped quickly”

There is an inconvenient point here that should be stated directly: if the workers themselves decide whether to finance their own plant, they are simultaneously those who decide and those who are affected by the decision. It's like giving yourself a bonus. Stopping funding for your workplace means being left without a job. People on average are in no hurry to make such decisions about themselves, even if objectively it is time.

#5. Titanium: Your message is key.

Here, in my opinion, you yourself have come to the most interesting question of the entire conversation, and it is worth analyzing it in detail, in simple words.

Your idea: titanium goes where more people use it - in airplanes and space, and not in sports cars, because more people use airplanes.

The problem is that “how many people use the entire category” and “how much does this particular kilogram of titanium cost here” are two different things, and the second is more important for deciding where to send it.

Let's take an example. You have one kilogram of titanium. It can go to:
- a part for a prosthetic leg for one specific person;
- a bicycle frame that will be bought by one of the millions of buyers of inexpensive bicycles.

According to your rule “more people use it” - bicycles win dry, there are millions of buyers, but the prosthesis is one person. But I think you yourself feel that this is the wrong answer: for that one person, this kilogram means the ability to walk, but for a cyclist, the frame will be a little lighter, but not critical.

**It's not about how many people use the category. The point is how badly this particular piece of resource is needed right here, in this one case.** The satellite and alloy for a specific bearing in a specific engine is not “aerospace category versus sports category”, it is thousands of individual solutions, and each must be compared separately with thousands of other individual solutions, and not one general category with another general category.

Next is the same question that we have already discussed. **Who counts “how many people use” each category, and who decides where to draw the line between categories** - are passenger planes, military aircraft, satellites and helicopters one category or four different ones, each with its own priority? This is exactly the same power to formulate a question that File 17 was about: as soon as someone decides what is considered “one category,” he has already determined by this decision who will win.

A regular market with prices solves this problem not because it is smart, but because there is no need to compare entire categories. The satellite manufacturer offers a price per kilogram of titanium for this particular satellite; the prosthesis manufacturer offers a price for this particular prosthesis; sports car manufacturer - for its frame. The winner is the one who needs that particular kilogram the most - without anyone comparing the total number of airplane users against the total number of sports car enthusiasts.

If you remove the price and leave the vote “what is more important - aerospace or sports”, you have not solved the problem of comparison, you have only coarsened it to the level at which it is solved incorrectly: important, but small needs (the same prosthesis) lose out to massive, but not particularly important ones (the same bicycle), simply because they vote according to their heads, and not according to who specifically needs it more.

# 6. Summary briefly

- About China - a real addition to the load, but not the reason for the system itself: the priority of heavy industry and soft financing of factories were laid down thirty years earlier.
- About the sources - I agree, one historical case is not proof for any system; but the mechanism itself (“if you don’t take risks, you don’t watch”) was also observed under capitalism, which means it’s not a matter of ideology.
- Workers finance their plant - they work for the equipment that they themselves use now; does not work for equipment whose benefit will accrue to other people in the future - this is a specific, narrow, rather than general problem, and it is documented in Yugoslavia and visible even in the living Mondragon.
- Titan is the main idea here: “more people use it” is not the same as “more important.” As soon as you go from one kilogram per consumer to voting on entire categories, you lose exactly the information you need to make a good decision, and you get a new question - who determines the boundaries of the categories - that does not answer itself.
