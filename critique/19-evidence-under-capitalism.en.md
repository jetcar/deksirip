# Part 19. “All your examples from capitalism” - analysis of the methodological objection

---

# I. Yucca Mountain: I’m filming the example, it was assigned incorrectly

You're right. Yucca Mountain was not rejected by the majority. The platform was appointed by Congress in 1987, but was buried by political opposition - primarily the position of the senator from Nevada, who had a leadership position and, therefore, power over the agenda and budget lines. There was no plebiscite. The billions spent are the result of the work of a completely capitalist political machine.

That is, the example illustrates the **power of the agent-setter** (my blocker C6), and not the failure of the majority rule. I put it in the wrong section. It is removed from the placement argument.

---

#II. The methodological objection is serious - and it has a limit

The general form of your objection: all my evidence is collected in systems with capital, so it measures capitalism, not the proposed construct. Turnout is low because the vote doesn't decide anything; experts were bought because there was someone to buy; people don’t donate because they are forced to save for their own safety.

**This is a legitimate objection, and it attacks a significant part of my evidence base.** I admit frankly: the systematic limitation of all criticism is that there are almost no untainted observations - no one has attempted an experiment with a society where basic needs are unconditionally provided and decisions are made by citizens.

**But there is a limit to the objection, and it must be drawn, otherwise it destroys the very possibility of discussion.** If any contradictory observation is dismissed with the words “this was under capitalism,” then no observation can say anything about the design. This is not protecting an idea - it is taking it beyond what is being tested. Your original goal was to find fundamental problems, and to do this the evidence must be admissible.

A useful move is not to argue about the principle, but to organize the evidence into three columns.

#IV. Charity: a check inside capitalism that partially removes the confusion

Your counter-argument to the 2% figure is clear: people don’t donate because they have to save for their own safety; with unconditional provision, they will donate more.

Verification exists within the current system. **Take those who already have complete security - the upper income groups.** They do not save for a rainy day, their basic needs are satisfied many times over, the free balance is huge.

The share of income they donate to charity is not 15%. According to available data, the share of donations in income is weakly related to income level, and by some measures it is even lower for the wealthiest than for the middle class. This is not about greed: it is about the fact that the removal of material needs does not in itself transform behavior into a mode of mass gratuitous financing of general needs.

**What this proves and what it doesn't prove.** Doesn't prove that with your design there will be exactly 2%: culture, status incentives and the lack of alternative ways to use funds can raise the figure. It proves something more narrow: **the hypothesis “it’s only a matter of lack of security” is testable and not confirmed**. This means that the gap between 2% and the required 15-16% cannot be closed by introducing unconditional security alone - an additional mechanism is needed, and it must be named.

The C1 blocker stands after this, although with the caveat that the exact figure for your system is unknown.

# V. Switzerland - a point in your favor that is worth taking away

You are more right about turnout than my numbers suggest, and that’s worth saying.

The average turnout for Swiss elections is about 45%, but the composition of participants varies from vote to vote. The proportion of citizens who **never** participate is small; the share of participants is **always** also small; the majority participates selectively - in those issues that concern or interest them.

**This is exactly the model that you describe:** a person does not understand everything, but gets involved where it is important to him. So the number “45%” does not mean “55% are passive.” Cumulative participation is significantly higher than the average turnout for a single issue.

This weakens my argument about passivity and is worth adding to the master post as a response to the objection rather than waiting for someone to raise it against you.

What remains of the argument: selective participation means that each specific issue is decided by a **self-selected interest group**, not society. For issues where interest coincides with concern, this is good. For issues where no one is interested - maintenance, hidden infrastructure - no one solves it. This brings back C3, but without relying on the thesis of universal passivity.

#VI. Surfside does not reject your objection

This is worth highlighting because there is no confusion here.

Florida apartment owners were not poor or dependent. They are financially independent—exactly the kind of independence that, according to your argument, makes bribery pointless and decisions free. No capitalist interfered with them: they themselves were the owners, they decided by majority on their own house, the defect was described by the engineers and visible to them personally, and the consequences threatened them and their families.

And they refused to approve the contribution for three years until the building collapsed.

**Not a single element of your objection is applicable here.** This is not poverty, not capital, not the meaninglessness of the voice, not bought experts - the experts wrote everything in the 2018 report. This is a pure majority decision of financially independent people about their own safety, with fast and personal feedback, that is, in the most favorable conditions.

Blocker C3 rests on this example, not on American infrastructure or Yucca Mountain.

#VII. “Don’t buy or force, but motivate, sell the idea”

You say: this is how it works now, so if it’s a minus, then it’s the same minus.

**Not the same, and here's why.** Belief has an economy: resources are invested in it until the marginal return equals the return from other uses. The payoff depends on what can be won by persuasion.

- Today, persuasion fights mainly for **consumer spending**. In this case, the distribution of capital is carried out by the professional layer, and advertising has little effect on them: they watch reports, not videos.
- In your design, conviction fights **directly for capital**. The prize has increased by an order of magnitude.

Hence the prediction, independent of anyone's greed: the share of public resources spent on persuasion grows until the marginal return equals. Today, global advertising spending is about 1% of global GDP, despite the fact that the prize is limited by the consumer wallet. You raise the prize to the entire investment flow.

And second: today there is a **professional skeptic who makes money from debunking** - short selling, credit analysis, investigation. In your system, you cannot make money by exposing an overvalued project, because there is no position against it. The belief remains, the counter-belief disappears.

Plus a remark on the essence of your same goal: “the minus is the same as in capitalism” is not a defense. You are building a system that is supposed to be better; here it is structurally worse on a specific measurable parameter.

# VIII. You have already accepted coercion - and this is a movement forward, not a concession

Pay attention to your own wording: “the affected areas need to be freed, and compensation is part of the process, no one will be left without housing.”

This is a description of seizure with reimbursement. That is:

- the decision of the majority is **compulsorily executed** in relation to those who disagree;
- there is **mandatory compensation**, and mandatory compensation is financed by mandatory deductions - voluntary fees “to compensate those who were evicted for the sake of the storage facility” will be collected not by everyone, but by those who are not indifferent;
- there is a body that assesses that the compensation is sufficient and carries out the liberation of the territory.

There is nothing wrong with this: any working system is designed this way. But this means that **Section 11, as currently drafted, no longer describes your own position.** You allow organized coercion where it is indispensable - and rightly so.

Practical conclusion: Section 11 should be rewritten from “no coercion” to a list: what coercion is allowed, by whom, by what procedure, with what appeal. This is not abandoning an idea, it is bringing it to a state in which it cannot be analyzed in two paragraphs.

And the Finnish example then ceases to be “passing”: it shows a working combination - the consent of the host community **plus** a national framework obliging the problem to be solved, **plus** mandatory funding from operators. Three elements, and your version contains the first one, but requires adding the other two.

---

#IX. Bought experts: without institutions the channel is wider, not narrower

The argument is correct: expertise is bought. Industry research, a revolving door, trial design for the desired result - all this is real.

But look what happens when institutions are removed. Today, a purchased expert faces: competing experts with institutional support, peer review, a requirement for reproducibility, professional reputation, responsibility for the conclusion, a regulator who can revoke the right to practice.

In your design, experts are deprived of the right to decide - but not deprived of influence on the information on which the majority decides. At the same time, all the listed counterbalances disappear, because they are all permanent professional institutions.

**Removing the expert's power to decide and leaving the power to inform is to preserve the channel of influence and remove his control.** This is a deterioration, not an improvement, and it does not require anyone's bad faith.
