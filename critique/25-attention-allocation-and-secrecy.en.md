# Part 25. How many passionate people do you need - and the question of secrecy

---

#1. What is really strong about your argument?

An interested person works better than a forced person - this is not just your opinion, this is a confirmed result in the psychology of motivation (self-determination according to Deci and Ryan, if you need a link). And the Finnish experiment with an unconditional income in 2017–2018 (two thousand unemployed people received 560 euros per month for two years) showed: people did not stop working, but their well-being and initiative grew. So “give a man freedom and he will do more and better” are not empty words, this is a direction that has actually been observed.

The objection about my examples is also partially correct: OpenSSL and xz are people who still live in a world where they have to pay rent. Accusing them of being lazy when they don't have time is unfair.

But I have an objection that remains valid even after this concession, and it is not about “laziness”, but about where interest is directed when there is more time.

---

# 2. Even those who are no longer bothered by anything have the same imbalance

An important detail that you might not know: not everyone who fixes open source projects does it instead of sleeping instead of working. Many maintainers are people with normal, paid jobs who already have evenings and weekends that they voluntarily spend on code because they are really interested. That is, for them personally, the need to earn money during these specific hours no longer applies - this is their free time, spent for the love of their work, as in your scenario with 8–9 hours.

And even this group has the same picture: they are more willing to write a new feature than to sort out other people’s old bugs. There is a well-known analysis of this topic - Nadia Eghbal’s report “Roads and Bridges” (2016), written precisely about the fact that open source rests on an infrastructure that is systemically undervalued, and maintainers burn out not from a lack of hours, but from the fact that boring work does not give recognition, but interesting work does.

So it's not just about the watch. It's also a matter of where people put their watches when the choice is theirs, not that there aren't enough watches.

---

#3. Your own, most honest question - that’s where it’s at.

You yourself wrote at the end: “will this amount be enough to ensure growth?” This is a valid question, I will answer it directly and not in general terms.

Imagine: how many pipes, servers, machines, wiring, bridges there are in society - growing along with the society itself. The larger and more complex the economy, the more things need constant care. This is not a fixed number, it grows with the scale.

And how many people are personally passionate about fixing this particular pipe or this particular old piece of code does not grow with the scale of the economy. Interest is an uneven thing: many are drawn to the new and noticeable, few are drawn to the old and unnoticed, and this does not depend on how much free time a person has in general.

Hence the gap that I spoke about earlier in terms of numbers: just to maintain what they already have, a normal country spends about 15–16% of everything that is produced. This is not because people are lazy, but because this is exactly the amount of work that is needed simply so that nothing falls apart. The question is not “will we give people time”, but “will there be exactly enough people who are personally interested in doing just this boring piece, and not something more exciting” - and these are two different questions.

---

#4: Interest vs. duress is not quite the same thing as interesting vs. boring work.

You are right that an interested person works better than a forced one. But this is true **for the work that interests him**. The problem with maintenance is not that people are forced, but that the task itself—by nature repetitive and unobtrusive—is difficult to make interesting to almost anyone, whether the person is free or not.

Previously, before any talk about income, this problem had a simple solution: pay more for unpleasant but necessary work - an allowance for harmfulness, for night shifts, for fiddling with someone else's old code. It's not a perfect solution, but it worked precisely because it didn't require personal passion. There is no such leveling in your system: everyone has the same income, which means the only remaining motive is personal interest, and by its nature it is unevenly distributed between the “interesting new” and the “boring old”.

---

# 5. About hackers - I partially agree, and that’s the limit

Money is the real motive for hacking, you are right here. But not the only one. Hackers working for the intelligence services of other countries break systems not for the sake of making money, but for the sake of the country that hired them or to which they are loyal - and this is a huge, separate category that is not explained by money at all. The same goes for ideological hackers who just want to prove something to someone.

And that leads right into your next question.

---

# 6. Secret projects - there is a direct contradiction here in the text of the concept itself, and it cannot be resolved with the words “at later stages”

See what happens when you put two sections of your own concept together.

One section says: any decision is verifiable, from the decision of citizens to the issuance of resources; there should be no closed bodies.

Another section says: an army is needed to protect against external attack.

And now the question is: other countries’ intelligence services will not become transparent simply because you decide to be open. As long as there is at least one country nearby that hides its military developments, your country will either have to hide its own (violating the principle of full transparency), or remain completely open and show the enemy everything you have in advance.

This is not a matter of good will, it is a matter of structure: **the side that plays openly against the side that plays secretly loses not because it is worse, but because the enemy sees its moves in advance, but it does not.** Unilaterally disarming, giving up secrecy before others have canceled it, is a strategically losing move, regardless of intentions.

Your answer “it’s already at a later stage when everyone can do it together” is honest, but this is not a solution, but a hope that the rest of the world will agree with you at the same time. Your own system cannot guarantee this: it governs your country, not the decisions of everyone else. Until the whole world agrees at once, the question “what to do with closed military and intelligence projects under the principle of full transparency” remains unanswered - not because the decision is difficult, but because it is a direct logical contradiction of your two sections, which disappears only under a condition that you yourself do not control.

---

#7. Briefly

- It’s true about interest versus coercion, and the UBI experiments partly confirm this.
- About the fact that my examples are from a world without a basic income - partly true, but not completely: some of the same people are already spending voluntary personal time on this, and the bias “the new is more interesting than the old” persists even among them.
- The main question - your own, about the number of enthusiastic people - does not rest on free time, but on the fact that interest is unevenly distributed between the new and the old, and the required volume of service grows with the scale of the economy, and not with the number of enthusiasts.
- About hackers - money is important, but not the only reason; intelligence and ideological attackers are not going anywhere.
- About secrecy is not a side issue, this is a direct clash between the section on transparency and the section on the army, and it cannot be resolved with the phrase “someday later,” because it does not depend on your system, but on the rest of the world.
