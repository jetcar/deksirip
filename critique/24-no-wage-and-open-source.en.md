#3. Maintenance is a different task, and without a salary it is not easier, but harder.

Now without making a mistake: what makes a person notice that the machine will soon break down and take care of it - if they don’t get paid for it, and their income is the same in any case?

The only incentive left is the same as people who do something for free because they are interested in it: passion, a desire to keep it going, a desire to remain part of something that works. This is not an incentive for an employee, it is an incentive for a volunteer.

And we have a very accurate, modern example, which has nothing in common with either the USSR or capitalism, of how exactly this incentive copes with service - open source code. People write programs for free because it’s interesting, for the sake of recognition, so that the work is useful. Exactly your motivation model, one to one.

**And this is what happens with service in practice.**

**OpenSSL, 2014.** The program that powers the encryption of a huge part of the Internet - banks, mail, shops. The Heartbleed vulnerability existed undetected for two years. When they began to look into it, it turned out that this code, critical for the entire Internet, was supported by a small group of essentially unpaid volunteers. Writing something new is interesting. Checking old code for errors is boring, no one has systematically done this.

**xz utils, 2024** A data compression library built into almost every Linux server in the world. She was supported by one person, on a voluntary basis, for many years, and he burned out. This is exactly what the attacker took advantage of: for several years he ingratiated himself by offering help with routine maintenance, which the real author was not interested in doing and had no one to delegate to, and eventually gained access and built a backdoor. It was noticed almost by accident, a couple of weeks before the code would have hit most of the world’s servers.

These are not isolated cases, this is a recognized, discussed problem throughout the field - there is even a term “bus factor”: critical infrastructure rests on one or two people, and the question is not “if”, but “when” this will become a problem.

---

# 4. Why is this your case and not just a similar one?

Look at the one-to-one coincidence:

| Open source | Your factory |
|---|---|
| Write for free, out of interest | They work for general income, not for wages from the factory |
| New feature - prestigious, interesting | New project - prestigious, interesting (section 12 of your concept) |
| Fixing an old vulnerability - boring, invisible | Replacing a worn-out part is boring, invisible |
| Works until it breaks in public | Same thing, literally your wording |
| Remains on one passionate person | Exactly what you yourself described about partnerships in the last conversation - one pulls through, the rest don’t |

This is not an analogy drawn up for the sake of argument. This is the same structure of incentives: **income does not depend on whether something is served or not, which means that service rests only on personal interest, and personal interest systematically prefers creation to service.**

#5. Bottom line

Expansion based on demand works, I admit, I don’t see any mistakes here.

Service—without a salary—does not work better, but worse than what I mistakenly wrote, because there is no longer any personal financial interest at all, only passion remains, and passion, according to everything that we know from open source—an area that has nothing to do with ideology—is systematically failing precisely routine service, not because the people are bad, but because it is uninteresting and invisible until it explodes.

This is the same C3 blocker that was there before, it’s just now justified correctly: not “workers will be lazy because of the salary,” but “income is not tied to the result at all, which means everything rests on personal interest, and personal interest predictably bypasses the boring.”
