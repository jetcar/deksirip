# Part 18. Not pathology, but the exchange of legitimacy for legal capacity

Objection: where is the pathology? The majority decides, not a couple of people with money. The world should work for the majority, and not for those who have managed to accumulate, including through illegal methods. This is not a pathology, but an ideal solution to the current problem.

---

# I. Claim to the word accepted

“Pathology” is the wrong word, and you have caught a substitution in it. It presupposes a standard (the protection of the minority is more important than the decision of the majority), which I did not justify, but simply introduced. If the standard of legitimacy is the preference of the majority, then a dump in an area with fewer votes is not a failure, but the mechanism working as intended.

Moreover: **in terms of legitimacy, the majority distribution of land is indeed stronger than the capital-weighted distribution** on most theories of legitimacy, not just yours. I admitted this in file 17 and I will repeat it directly, and not in small print.

This means that we should not argue about legitimacy. Below are three objections that **do not rely** on minority rights and work within your own standard.

---

#II. Objection one: the landfill must be somewhere

Your goal is to keep the system running. Then let's check the mechanism on the maintenance problem.

Waste exists. Treatment facilities are needed. The spent nuclear fuel storage facility must be located in a specific location. The question is not “whether to place”, but “where exactly”.

If each location is determined by the majority of those affected, then each specific decision will be negative - because for any individual area the costs are local and large, and the benefits are dispersed throughout the country. They vote for each place separately, and in each place the majority is against.

**The result is not “fairer placement”, but no placement.**

**Yucca Mountain.** The US Congress designated a nuclear waste repository site in 1987. Nevada constantly resisted, funding was stopped around 2010, and about ten-plus billion dollars were spent. The result: spent fuel remains in temporary pools at dozens of reactor sites across the country. By any technical criterion - safety, cost, risk - this is **worse** than any of the options discussed. No majority wanted this outcome; it arose as the sum of individual majoritarian decisions.

Note that this is not an argument about minority rights. Everyone suffered, including the majority, by his own criteria.

**Honest counterexample, and it's yours.** Finland built Onkalo - the only operating deep storage facility. The mechanism involved municipal consent rather than coercion. That is, your model is working.

But look at what it paid for: about forty years of process, a national legal framework obliging the problem to be solved, and compensation to the host community. **Compensation is the transfer of resources from everyone to one group for agreeing to accept a common burden, and it must be mandatory**, otherwise not everyone will pay, but only those who care. Section 11 excludes mandatory deductions.

The wording of the objection: **not “the majority decides unfairly”, but “a series of local majorities are unable to make a decision that is necessary for everyone”**. This is a deficiency of capacity, not justice.

---

#III. Objection two: “many decide” and “those affected decide” are different rules

Here, in my opinion, is the most important thing.

Your argument is based on the image of the many versus the rich few. He is faithful when the whole society votes.

But conflicts about use are local. And in a local conflict, the local majority is itself a **few** in relation to society. A village blocking a vault is not “many”; it is a small group that passes the costs on to everyone else.

From here:

- **rule “many decide”** → the decision on the site is made by the whole society → those living nearby are always in the minority on issues that concern only them, and their yard is determined by people who will not be in it;
- **rule “those affected decide”** → the local community decides → a small group determines the outcome of the common cause and extracts rent from this situation.

**These are different rules that give opposite answers, and you can’t have both at the same time.** Your wording “those who are outnumbered wins” does not indicate which of the two applies, but it is this choice that decides the matter - just as the choice of wording of the question decides it.

By the way, this is not an argument against capital: the same uncertainty exists today, but today it is at least resolved by the explicit distribution of powers between levels of government. You don't have a distribution of levels, because there are no levels.

---

#IV. Objection three: what is a majority empirically?

Your claim that “many people decide, rather than a few people with money” is arithmetically correct with high turnout. Let us check the baseline rates.

Token governance in distributed organizations: typically 1-10% participation. Swiss referendums: about 45%. Local elections in the USA: 15–20%. Residents' meetings: units of percent.

With 5% participation, “the majority decides” means that **the majority of the 5% who were mobilized most effectively decides.** The comparison is not “many versus a few rich people,” but “the best-mobilized few versus the rich few.” Both sides are few.

And one technical difference is not in your favor: capital can be measured and disclosed; there are declarations, registers and limits for it. Mobilization ability is not measurable and therefore not regulated.

**What would make your argument strong:** turnout of about 70-80% with real immersion in the issue. Then “many against few” ceases to be a figure of speech. I don’t know of any examples of sustainable retention of such a turnout on dozens of technical issues at the same time, and this is precisely the second most important empirical request for the entire project after the maintenance rate.

---

# V. “Accumulated, including through illegal methods” - true, but proves something else

It is true, and I do not intend to soften it: a significant portion of large fortunes have origins that do not stand up to scrutiny, and the legitimacy of the current distribution is a real, not an imaginary, problem.

But pay attention to the structure of the argument. “The current holders are illegitimate” is an argument in favor of **redistribution**. It says nothing about what **mechanism** to use after redistribution.

These are two separate tasks:
- who has the right to dispose (the issue of legitimacy) - here your position is strong;
- by what procedure to resolve competing claims (a question of mechanism) - here the illegitimacy of past accumulations does not provide any answer.

Blending allows one argument to do the work of two conclusions. Critics will immediately share this, so it is better to divide it yourself: first, justify why exclusive claims are unfounded, and separately justify why the majoritarian procedure is better than others.

---

#VI. Correct frame

I remove the word “pathology” and replace the wording.

> Majoritarian resolution of conflicts about use is **higher in legitimacy** and **lower in capacity** than the current mixture of market and procedures. It reliably provides a solution that the majority considers its own, and systematically does not provide a solution where the benefits are dispersed and the costs are local - that is, exactly in the tasks of maintaining a common infrastructure.

This is an exchange, not a defect. And it is yours to make it: if legitimacy is more important than capacity, the position is consistent, and it can be defended.

But it must be done consciously, because the price is specific: the class of problems that cannot be solved by a series of local majorities is waste, treatment facilities, storage facilities, main networks, landfills, emergency reserve facilities. Everything that everyone needs and no one needs next to them.

**And one more thing for publication.** From the entire discussion, two questions have the form that can be empirically verified, and both decide the fate of the structure:

1. Is there a known case where the voluntary non-refundable allocation of funds consistently provided at least 8–10% of GDP (15–16% is needed)?
2. Is there a known case of sustained participation of about 70% of the population in decision-making on dozens of technical issues simultaneously?

If both answers are negative, the design rests on two unproven assumptions. If at least one is positive, the corresponding blocker falls. This is the best question that can be brought to the platforms.
