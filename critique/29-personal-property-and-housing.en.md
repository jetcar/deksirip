# Personal Property, Housing, and Dachas

## The problem

Banning private ownership of means of production does not by itself answer questions about houses, apartments, and dachas. Housing is simultaneously personal space, a limited resource, the product of labour, and a potential source of rent.

## Working boundary

### Personal belongings

Clothing, furniture, household appliances, tools, privately used cars, and hobby equipment may be personal property. They do not give anyone authority over another person's labour and are not sources of rent.

### Primary housing

Primary housing provides a protected right of residence without becoming a speculative asset:

- the resident does not own land as a source of rent;
- no one may occupy several scarce properties and let them for income;
- no income may be obtained merely from an increase in land value;
- ordinary upkeep of the user's part is the user's responsibility;
- residents participate in managing shared building infrastructure;
- housing may be exchanged for a more suitable option when household composition changes;
- if another user receives the housing by public decision, that user pays the previous resident's reasonable relocation costs.

The right of residence may be transferred to household members, exchanged for comparable housing, or returned to the common pool. When vacated, the property does not become inheritable capital.

### Dachas

A dacha is a seasonal right to use a plot and building. Land remains common; the building and personal improvements may be personal within the rules of use.

If a dacha is rarely used, temporary shared use or a public schedule may be introduced. Otherwise a limited resource becomes an idle reserve.

### Review of excess space

Every six months, an automated system publishes data on housing use. Any citizen may initiate a vote to transfer a property to another user. The decision considers household size, available alternatives, and urgency.

For example, four people living in a two-room apartment have an obviously stronger need for a four-room apartment than one person. After a transfer decision, the new user pays the former resident's relocation costs and provides suitable housing. If society considers keeping the larger home with one person important, it may support that use with its own resources.

After a resident's death, the right to clearly excessive housing may be reviewed, for example after five years, taking into account remaining household members and other people's needs.

### Multi-unit buildings

An apartment depends on the roof, foundation, pipes, elevator, and land. Residents therefore:

- elect building management;
- see the technical condition and the repair reserve;
- jointly fund emergency and capital work;
- can replace poor management.

## Allocation of scarce housing

When suitable apartments are fewer than the number of applicants, public criteria apply: household size, accessibility, proximity to care and education, personal preferences, waiting time, and random selection among comparable options.

The accumulated social dividend must not allow someone to buy several scarce apartments. Otherwise the right to direct one's share becomes a housing market and recreates a property hierarchy.

## What is already defined

- land always remains common;
- buildings and personal improvements may be personally held;
- exchange is allowed when housing or production conditions are met;
- letting is allowed only to recover costs, without profit;
- construction starts with sufficient support from future users or other citizens;
- capital work is funded by residents, the resource user, or the community supporting the project.

## Remaining questions

1. Is personal holding of a building limited ownership, or a long-term use right with ownership of improvements?
2. How should the reasonable cost of letting part of a home and of repairs be calculated?
3. Who enforces a housing-transfer decision if the former user refuses to move?
4. How should the system handle free housing in another city or housing in unsuitable condition?
5. How exactly should the review period after death or temporary absence be calculated?
