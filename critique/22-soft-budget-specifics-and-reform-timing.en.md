# Part 22. Which factories, who provided them, and what broke first

Three specific questions, I answer each honestly - including where I don’t have accurate data.

---

# 1. “Which factories” - an honest answer: I don’t have one specific factory, and I won’t invent it

After the Yucca Mountain story, I don't want to repeat the mistake of making an example fit a thought. I'll tell you exactly what I can confirm and what I can't.

**The State Bank gave money to the factories** - not like an ordinary bank assessing risk, but fulfilling the directives of the plan. A plant that did not fulfill the plan usually did not go bankrupt and did not fire people - this was ideologically prohibited (“the right to work” was enshrined in the constitution). This is not my theory, it is documented in detail in Joseph Berliner's study "Factory and Manager in the USSR" (1957) - he interviewed directors who left Soviet enterprises and described the system from the inside: the accumulation of excess resources and workers in reserve, storming at the end of the month, informal deals for the necessary parts.

I won’t tell you the name of one specific plant - I don’t have such data, and I won’t make it up. But there is a documented systemic phenomenon: **priority of group “A” (heavy industry, defense) over group “B” (goods for people)**. This has been an open, officially stated policy since the first five-year plans of the late 1920s—not a secret or a matter of dispute.

---

# 2. “The factories that were seized and they are still working for the capitalists” is true, and here’s what it proves and what it doesn’t

Truth: Some Soviet industrial assets—metallurgical plants, nickel and oil enterprises—survived the privatization of the 1990s and are still operating, some profitably, under private owners. These are real, well-known examples.

But this does not refute my thought, but clarifies it. Steel and oil were needed in almost any system - their value did not depend on who controlled them. What has survived is mainly raw materials and heavy industries with global demand.

And now the second half of the same picture, which you did not name: **industrial production in Russia in the 1990s fell by about 40–50%**. A huge share of enterprises - mechanical engineering, light industry, civilian factories without military orders - simply closed when the subsidies stopped.

This is a fairly direct test of what I was talking about: if almost half of the industry did not survive the cancellation of replenishment, it means that a considerable part was supported by it. Not 100%, but also not “huge scales that never happened” - it’s just huge scales.

---

# 3. “The collapse was not because of money to the factories, but because of a change in the course towards profit” - here I have the exact date that breaks this version

This is an interesting and serious argument, and I will answer it precisely, by date, and not in general terms.

**Kornai's book on chronic deficits was published in 1980.** Gorbachev's reform, which gave enterprises autonomy and tied bonuses to profits, was a 1987 law that came into force in 1988. **Seven years between them.**

That is, chronic shortages - queues, coupons, the inability to buy what you need - were described in detail and recognized as a systemic, stable feature of the economy **seven years before** the element of profit was even introduced into the system. Scarcity was the norm under Brezhnev in the 1970s, long before any market - this is not a propaganda memory, it is something that was studied precisely because it was noticeable and constant.

So the version “it was fine, then they introduced profit, and it started falling” does not match the dates: the problem I’m talking about was diagnosed before the reason you name.

---

#4. What the reform actually was - and it works against your argument, not in its favor.

Here I will say more precisely what really happened in 1987–1991, because this is important.

The reform did not replace the plan with a normal market. She did the worst thing possible: **removed policy control of the plan, but did not replace it with workable prices and real risk of bankruptcy.**

What happened in practice:
- factories received the right to decide for themselves what to produce, and began to move away from unprofitable but necessary things (bread, simple goods) to what is more profitable to sell;
- prices remained largely fixed by the state - that is, the market, which should have said “make more bread, because it is getting more expensive,” did not work;
- legalized cooperatives (1988) could buy goods at the old state price and resell them at the market price - this pumped goods out of state stores, rather than adding them;
- at the same time, no one fired the plant directors and no one went bankrupt - the old softness remained, and new freedom was added from above.

**This is exactly the same combination that I warned about in file 12 in relation to your scheme: they removed strict coercion (the plan) and did not replace it with strict discipline (real prices, real risk), but left only voluntary and semi-free decisions of people within the system.** The result is not an improvement in supply, but its sharp deterioration over three or four years.

This is not an isolated opinion, but a standard explanation of transition in the economics literature: half-hearted reform is worse than a full plan or a full market because it gives freedom to act in one's own interests, but does not provide punishment for bad results.
