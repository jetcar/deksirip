## Title

**A market-socialist alternative I want criticised from a parecon perspective: individual dividends directing investment**

## Body

I've been developing a sketch that sits somewhere between market socialism and participatory planning, and I would like the parecon community's criticism specifically — not because I expect you to like it, but because I expect your objections to be the ones I can't generate myself.

**The sketch.** Productive assets are socially owned and cannot be privately held. There is no central planning body. Each citizen receives a recurring individual share of social product. What is not consumed accumulates in a personal balance. That balance confers no ownership of productive assets, but the holder directs it: anyone can propose a project, and it is funded if enough citizens choose to send their balances to it. Citizens can also join with their labour. Long-horizon projects can receive multi-year pledges that remain revocable. Strategic reserves are decided by citizens with expert and AI input that is advisory only. No permanent body sets priorities.

**Where I expect Albert and Hahnel's framework to hit hardest, and where I want you to press:**

1. **This retains market logic without market discipline.** Parecon's objection to markets isn't only about efficiency; it's that markets systematically misprice social and ecological externalities and reward antisocial behaviour. My scheme leaves that intact for ordinary goods while removing the one thing markets do well (allocating capital under residual risk). Is that the worst of both, and if so, what is the precise mechanism?

2. **Remuneration.** Parecon remunerates effort and sacrifice, and balances job complexes precisely so that unpleasant and empowering work are shared. My scheme has no such mechanism. What happens to the sewer worker and the night nurse when basic needs are unconditional, status accrues to project founders, and the design has no compensating differential? I suspect this is fatal and I'd like it stated properly.

3. **The coordinator class.** This is the objection I most want developed. My scheme forbids a planning body, but the people running funded projects control assets, hiring, specifications and information — while formally owning nothing. Is this not exactly the coordinator class you argue emerged in centrally planned economies, arriving by a different route? What in your framework predicts about how fast that class consolidates?

4. **Consumption vs. investment.** Parecon's iteration procedure reconciles consumer and worker proposals through facilitation boards and indicative prices. My scheme has no reconciliation step at all: the aggregate savings rate is an uncoordinated sum. Does your literature have a worked account of what goes wrong when that reconciliation is simply absent?

5. **Self-management at the workplace.** My sketch says nothing about who decides at an existing enterprise. Parecon's answer is decision-making power in proportion to how much you're affected. What are the concrete consequences of leaving this blank?

6. **Publicity and manipulation.** In my scheme, advertising competes directly for capital, not merely for consumer demand. Does parecon's critique of markets predict a specific magnitude or dynamic here?

I'm not looking for encouragement. If the honest reading is "this reproduces the pathologies of both markets and coordinatorism", I'd rather hear that with the mechanism spelled out. Pointers to specific chapters of *The Political Economy of Participatory Economics* or Hahnel's later work are welcome.
