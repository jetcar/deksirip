## Comment draft

Fiat question, and I'm looking to have it demolished rather than validated.

Consider an arrangement: productive assets are socially owned and can't be privately held; there's no planning agency; every citizen gets a recurring equal share of social product; unspent share accumulates as a non-transferable personal balance; any project gets funded if enough citizens direct their balances to it; long-gestation projects get multi-year but revocable pledges. Essentially Roemer's coupon socialism with the main banks deleted and a UBI bolted on.

Three things I think are decisive, and I want to know if I'm framing them right or if there's a standard result I'm missing:

**1. Is a replenishing flow a hard budget constraint?** My claim: no. The capitalist stakes a stock that isn't replaced if lost; the citizen stakes a flow that arrives again next period. Expected cost of misallocation ≈ deferred consumption over N periods, which goes to zero as N shrinks relative to the dividend. So investor selectivity collapses and you get Kornai-style shortage, but generated by income structure rather than by a bailing-out state. Is there literature that models softness this way — arising from the funder's income process rather than from ex post rescue? Or am I abusing the concept?

**2. Does the consumer-side dividend do anything at all for the calculation problem?** My reading is that it addresses the wrong half. Mises 1920 is about allocating capital goods among competing techniques, which requires prices formed by owners bearing residual risk. Dispersed non-transferable funding claims don't generate those. Is the standard modern view that Lange's answer was already dead by Hayek 1940 on precisely this point, or is there a live market-socialist response?

**3. What reconciles the aggregate savings rate with the composition of productive capacity?** If millions of people independently choose their own consumption/investment split, the economy-wide investment rate is an emergent number with nothing making it consistent with the capacity to produce investment goods versus consumption goods. In a market economy the interest rate and profits do some of this work. Here nothing does. Is this just Kalecki/Domar with extra steps, or is there a cleaner way to state the failure?

Bonus, if anyone has it: empirical work comparing crowd-directed capital allocation (crowdfunding, ICOs, quadratic-funding rounds) against intermediated finance on ex post project outcomes. I keep seeing descriptive stats and no clean comparison.
