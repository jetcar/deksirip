## Title

**Is a citizen-directed investment fund just capitalism with a distributed shareholder? Attack this market-socialist sketch.**

## Body

Upfront: this is a market-socialist proposal, not an anarchist or communist one, and I'm posting it here because I expect your objections to be the ones I can't produce myself. I'm not defending it. I want it broken.

**The sketch.** Productive assets are socially owned and cannot be privately held. There is no planning agency. Every citizen receives a recurring individual share of social product. Unspent share accumulates as a personal, non-transferable balance that confers no ownership of productive assets. Any person or group can propose a project; it gets funded if enough citizens direct their balances into it. People can also join with their labour. Long-gestation projects get multi-year, revocable pledges. Strategic reserves are set by citizens; experts and AI advise but don't decide. No permanent body sets priorities. Rules change only by citizen decision.

**The objections I think you'll make, and want made properly:**

1. **"You've abolished the title and kept the relation."** Whoever runs a funded project controls the assets, hires, sets specifications, and holds the information. Legal ownership is gone; the social relation of command over other people's labour isn't. Is there a name for this configuration in your tradition beyond "state capitalism" or "coordinator class", and what does that framework predict about how fast it consolidates?

2. **"Wage labour survives."** A person joining a project works for a project they don't control, funded by strangers. What is the concrete difference from employment, other than the identity of the funder?

3. **"The dividend individualises what should be common."** Turning the social product into per-capita private balances arguably reproduces the commodity form and makes social decisions into aggregated private purchases. I'd like the strongest version of this argument, ideally with the mechanism rather than the vocabulary.

4. **"No workplace democracy at all."** The sketch says nothing about who decides at an existing enterprise: capital replacement, hiring, suppliers, pace of work. Anarchist critique of exactly this blank is what I'm after. Note also that Yugoslav self-management, the closest historical attempt, produced perverse supply responses (Ward–Domar–Vanek) and chronic underinvestment in long-lived assets (Furubotn–Pejovich horizon problem) — is that a critique of self-management, or of the market it was embedded in?

5. **"Organised minorities eat it."** Olson's asymmetry says a coordinated 2% beats an uncoordinated 60%, and here they don't need to lobby anyone — they just coordinate their own dividends. Anarchist theory has a lot to say about informal hierarchy in supposedly structureless settings (Freeman's "The Tyranny of Structurelessness" is the obvious reference). Does that argument apply directly here, and how?

6. **"Ostrom's boundaries."** Successful commons governance in Ostrom's work requires clearly defined resource and group boundaries. This sketch makes an entire society one unbounded commons, violating the first design principle. Is that as fatal as it looks?

7. **The unpleasant-work problem.** Basic needs unconditional, status accruing to project founders, no compensating differential for sewer work or night shifts. I don't have an answer and I don't think "people will want to contribute" is one.

What I'd most value: a mechanism in the form "X happens → people get an incentive to do Y → Z follows → the thing collapses", and any historical case where something structurally similar was tried. I'm aware of Yugoslavia, the kibbutzim, Mondragon, Porto Alegre and the Czech/Russian voucher privatisations. Cases I don't know are more useful than cases I do.
