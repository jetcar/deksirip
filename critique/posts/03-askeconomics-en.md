## Title

**What does the literature say about "coupon socialism" schemes where citizens individually direct investment, and why did Roemer and Schweickart both add professional financial intermediaries?**

## Body

I've been reading about market-socialist proposals in which productive assets are socially owned, there is no central plan, and investment is steered by individual citizens directing a per-capita share of social product into projects they choose — a kind of dispersed venture funding, with multi-year revocable pledges for long-horizon projects and citizen-set strategic reserves. The explicit design goal in the version I'm looking at is that no permanent body sets priorities.

My questions:

1. **Monitoring.** Roemer's coupon socialism (*A Future for Socialism*, 1994) adds Japanese-style main banks as monitors, and Schweickart's economic democracy routes investment through regional public banks rather than individual choice. Is the standard reading that fully dispersed, non-tradable claims cannot solve the agency problem at all — i.e. that a Berle–Means outcome is unavoidable without either concentrated stakes or a delegated monitor? Is there any model where dispersed non-tradable claims do produce adequate monitoring?

2. **Capital-goods prices.** How does the modern literature treat the Mises calculation argument specifically as applied to schemes like this? A per-capita dividend gives consumer-side signals, but the allocation of capital goods among competing techniques seems untouched. Is there a market-socialist response that economists take seriously here, beyond Lange's original one and Hayek's 1940 reply?

3. **Budget constraints.** Is there work formalising the difference between staking a replenishing income flow versus a non-replenishing stock, in terms of investor selectivity? Intuitively this looks like a soft budget constraint in Kornai's sense, but generated by the income structure rather than by state rescue. Has anyone modelled it that way?

4. **Aggregate savings composition.** If the split between consumption and investment is the uncoordinated sum of millions of individual choices, what reconciles it with the economy's physical capacity to produce investment goods versus consumption goods? Is there a literature on this beyond the standard Kalecki/Domar treatment?

5. **Empirics on dispersed funding.** Is there any empirical work on crowd-directed allocation of capital at meaningful scale — crowdfunding, ICOs, quadratic-funding rounds, participatory budgeting — that speaks to whether dispersed funders select projects better or worse than intermediated finance?

6. **Long horizons.** Is there a mechanism-design result on whether revocable funding commitments can support long-gestation projects, or is the time-inconsistency problem (Kydland–Prescott) considered decisive here?

Pointers to specific papers or chapters would be more useful to me than general answers. I'm particularly interested in results that are robust to parameter choices rather than critiques of particular designs.
