## Title

**Coupon socialism without monitors: what breaks first?**

## Body

I want this idea attacked, not improved. Below is a mechanism sketch; underneath it are the failure modes I already believe in, and the specific claims I think are most likely wrong. What I want from you is the strongest failure mechanism I have not listed — ideally one that survives any setting of the free parameters.

**The mechanism.** Assume high automation and that basic needs are met unconditionally. Means of production cannot be privately owned. Every citizen receives a recurring share of social product. Unspent share accumulates as a personal balance that confers no ownership rights over productive assets, but the holder directs it: any person or group can propose a project ("a new engine type, 10bn units"), and it is funded if enough citizens choose to direct their balances into it. Ordinary goods production tracks where people direct resources and labour. Long-horizon projects can receive multi-year pledges, revocable as information arrives. Strategic reserves are set by citizens, informed by experts and AI advisors, neither of whom may decide. No standing body sets priorities; all rules are changeable by citizens; the army is professional and may act without a vote under direct attack.

**Prior art I am aware of.** This is close to Roemer's coupon socialism (*A Future for Socialism*, 1994) plus a basic income plus participatory budgeting for public goods; also Lange–Lerner market socialism, Schweickart's economic democracy, Yunker, Meade's *Agathotopia*. Notably, Roemer added Japanese-style main banks as monitors, and Schweickart routed investment through regional public banks with professional staff. Both added the institution this sketch forbids. That is the first thing I want to understand.

**Failure modes I already accept:**

1. *No capital-goods prices.* Informative prices for capital goods require someone staking a non-replenishing endowment against their own estimate. Remove the residual claimant and you get either an administrator (the banned body) or noise. Mises/Hayek, unmodified by the consumer-side dividend.
2. *Soft budget constraint.* The citizen risks a **flow** that replenishes; the capitalist risks a **stock** that does not. Expected cost of a bad allocation ≈ delayed consumption. Selection pressure on projects collapses. Kornai, generalised.
3. *Zero monitoring.* Each citizen holds ~1/N of the oversight burden and all of the cost. Rational ignorance ⇒ insider control ⇒ Djilas' new class without any legal ownership.
4. *Delegation concentrates.* Attention is scarce; delegation is inevitable; returns to attention-specialisation accumulate; delegate shares go power-law. LiquidFeedback superdelegates, DAO delegate concentration, index-fund voting power.
5. *Beauty contest.* Threshold funding turns the question from "is this good" into "will others fund it". Cascades, fads, fat tails. And Grossman–Stiglitz: nobody can profit from debunking an overvalued project (no shorting, no resale), so no professional sceptic exists. Marketing becomes the highest-return activity in the economy.
6. *McKelvey.* "Citizens set strategic priorities" over a multidimensional space has, generically, an empty core; the agenda setter drives the outcome. "No body sets priorities" is therefore not a design choice but a refusal to make the agenda setter accountable.
7. *Time inconsistency.* Revocable pledges cannot support 20-year horizons — such projects look like failures at year 7 by construction. Irrevocable endowments would work and are exactly the unaccountable fund the design forbids.
8. *Correlated advisors.* Condorcet needs error independence. A shared AI advisor injects a common error component, so the epistemic case for mass decision-making weakens precisely as the advisor gets good and widely used.

**Claims I most expect to be wrong, and would like attacked directly:**

- (A) That the dividend, being a replenishing flow, *necessarily* softens the budget constraint. Is there a mechanism that makes a flow behave like a stock without reintroducing tradable claims?
- (B) That delegation concentration is unavoidable. Is there any delegation design with a proven bound on concentration that does not collapse into "you may not publish recommendations"?
- (C) That McKelvey applies with full force here rather than being tamed by structure-induced equilibrium (Shepsle) — but note that structure-inducing institutions are the banned bodies.
- (D) That correlated AI advice is a real problem at realistic correlation levels, rather than a theoretical one.

**What would change my mind about the whole thing:** a worked mechanism in which (i) capital-goods allocation is disciplined by someone bearing non-replenishing downside, (ii) that someone is not a permanent unaccountable body, and (iii) monitoring costs are borne by whoever captures the monitoring benefit. I do not think all three are jointly satisfiable, and I would like to be shown otherwise or shown a clean impossibility.
