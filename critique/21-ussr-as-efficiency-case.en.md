# Part 21. USSR as an example of efficiency

The two criteria you propose—who the system serves and how effective it is overall—are the right framework, I won’t argue with it. I analyze the example of the USSR according to both criteria, as factually as possible.

---

#0. Short output first

Growth in the USSR was real. But it was obtained using exactly those two tools that your concept deliberately rejects: **centralized plan** and **organized coercion**. This means that the USSR is not an example of how a scheme without a plan and without coercion can be effective. This is an example of what can be achieved *with* a plan and *with* coercion - and even then, after half a century, it ran into exactly the same problems that I described in files 01, 03 and 12: shortages, lack of prices for means of production, nomenclature instead of control.

Further - in detail and with numbers, where they are.

# I. The growth was real and obtained in a concrete way

Since 1928, five-year plans gave rapid industrialization: heavy industry, metallurgy, then the atomic and space program - Sputnik in 1957, Gagarin in 1961. This is a real achievement, there is no point in denying it.

The way this was achieved:
- **directive plan** - The State Planning Committee determined what and how much to produce, this is the very “centralized body” that your concept directly rejects in the first section;
- **forced collectivization** of agriculture in the early 1930s, which caused mass famine with millions of deaths;
- **GULAG** - forced labor was used in mining, canal construction, logging - a documented contribution to specific industries of the period.

This is “organized coercion, organized by people” - what Section 11 of your concept rejects as a principle.

**Direct conclusion:** if the source of effectiveness is plan and coercion, and your scheme removes both, then the USSR does not prove that the scheme without them will be just as effective. He proves the opposite: that these two tools were historically required for this rate of growth.

---

#II. “There was no post-war devastation in the West” - inaccurate

The USSR really suffered the worst losses among all the countries at war - about 27 million dead, western territories destroyed. This should be admitted frankly and cannot be downplayed.

But the “West” is not only the United States. The US territory was not damaged, this is true. But Germany and Japan were destroyed no less, often more severely, than the European part of the USSR, and were restored **without a central plan and without the Gulag**, with a market economy (in Japan - with strong state sectoral regulation, but not with the Soviet plan).

By the 1960s and 70s, both countries were among the largest economies in the world, and in terms of living standards, quality and variety of consumer goods, and civil technologies, they were noticeably ahead of the USSR. This restoration took comparable or less time than Soviet industrialization, and without the two tools that the USSR used.

**A separate fact worth knowing:** The USSR itself abandoned the Marshall Plan in 1947 and forced the Eastern Bloc countries to abandon it. The post-war restoration of the USSR actually took place without external help - this is true and it is impressive. But during the war itself, the USSR received significant assistance under Lend-Lease from the United States - trucks, food, raw materials, estimates of tens of billions of then dollars. So “all alone, without any help” is also not entirely accurate when applied to the war years, although it certainly applies to the recovery after 1945.

---

#III. What happened next - and this was a direct hit on blocker C1

By the 1970s, growth had slowed sharply—a period called “stagnation.” There are several reasons: the aging of fixed assets, falling returns on investment, the growing military burden, and, since the mid-1980s, the collapse of oil prices, the most important source of currency for the USSR.

But the most interesting thing is what happened to ordinary people, and not to steel and rockets - this is the test of your first criterion, “who does the system serve.”

**The life expectancy of men in the USSR decreased from the mid-1960s to the mid-1980s.** This is a rare case in peacetime for an industrial country - this has not happened almost anywhere else. This is not propaganda, these are demographic statistics, known and not disputed.

**The shortage of consumer goods was chronic throughout the history of the USSR**, and not an episodic failure. Queues, coupons, cronyism as a way to get what you need is a systemic feature, not an exception.

This is exactly what I called blocker C1 in File 15: **maintaining and supplying the population is systematically underfunded** because the plan and status prioritized heavy industry and defense over the everyday needs of the people. The USSR had a plan - and the plan also did not solve this problem. Your concept of a plan has no plan at all, only a voluntary direction of attention, and this, as shown in file 12, is even weaker.

---

#IV. “There weren’t enough technical means” is a well-known position, and this is where its weak point is

This is not your invention, this idea has authors: Paul Cockshott and Allyn Cottrell, in a 1993 book, directly argue that Soviet planning failed due to a lack of computing power, and that modern computers could solve the problem of calculation in physical terms. I mentioned them in file 04 as one of the poles of the discussion.

But most economists who have studied the collapse of the USSR cite other reasons, and this is exactly what we have been discussing throughout this conversation:

- **lack of prices for means of production** (file 01, item M1) is not a computational problem. A computer can do the math quickly, but there is no substitute for a person putting their own money at risk so that the price reflects real value. This is an issue of incentives, not processor power;
- **soft budget constraint** (file 01, item M2, Kornai) - the plant was given money regardless of the result. The computer does not cure this habit;
- **nomenclature instead of control** (file 01, item M3, and analysis in file 12) - management of resources without real responsibility. This is a question about who answers, not about who counts.

These are all problems about information and incentives, not about arithmetic. Hayek wrote about it in 1945 in exactly these terms: part of the knowledge needed for a solution is dispersed, local, inexpressible in the form of data that can be loaded into a computer in advance. Faster calculations don't create this information—they just calculate what's already there faster.

# V. Summary: what exactly does the example prove?

| Statement | Confirmed? |
|---|---|
| Rapid industrial growth out of devastation is possible | Yes, it's a fact |
| It was obtained without plan and without coercion | **No.** It was obtained precisely by plan and coercion - two tools that your concept rejects |
| The West recovered more easily because there was no devastation | **Partly no.** Germany and Japan were no less destroyed and recovered without a plan and without a Gulag in a comparable period of time |
| The system served ordinary people well | **No.** The life expectancy of men had been declining for twenty years, the shortage was chronic - that is, by your first criterion, the system served not the 99%, but the military-industrial complex |
| The collapse happened due to a lack of technology and involvement, but now there is enough technology | The position exists (Cockshott-Cottrell), but most of the explanations are about incentives and information, not about computing power, and this is what blockers M1–M3 insist on |

**The main conclusion to keep in mind:** The USSR is an example of a system **with** a central plan and **with** coercion, which still could not feed and provide for its people at the level of comparable countries that recovered without these tools. Your concept removes both tools and preserves the goal. This does not make the task easier - judging by the Soviet experience, it was the plan and coercion that gave at least some (albeit crooked) discipline in the distribution of resources. To remove them and expect a better result is a statement that requires a separate explanation, and does not follow from the example of the USSR.

About “capitalism is also not the first time” - I agree as a general argument that a young system needs time. But here it is worth distinguishing between two different types of problems. The economic crises of capitalism are something that over time was partially smoothed out by new institutions (deposit insurance, countercyclical policies). And some of the blockers that I cited earlier - the theorem about the empty core in multidimensional voting (C6), Olson's logic about organized minorities, free-rider mathematics in voluntary funding (C1) - these are not implementation bugs that will go away with experience, but results that are correct in any attempt, as long as the rules scheme itself is preserved. It is not training that fixes them, but a change in principle.
