# Part 20. Testing with the same yardstick

In simple language, without a clutter of terms. I answer each point separately: where you are right, where I made a mistake with the figure, and where I will not take a position because the topic is too hot and inappropriate.

#0. What I change

You are right about the main thing: several times I said “now institute X is deciding this” and did not check whether institute X itself was bought with money. This is unfair to your scheme - I criticized it for its lack of control, and praised the current system for control, which in practice often does not work.

Then I go through all such places and count them honestly.

---

#1. “One VC fund owns 60% of everything.”

The number is wrong, but the thought behind it is correct - I’ll say both.

The three largest management companies (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) together vote about 20-25% of the shares in companies in the S&P 500. That's not 60%, and it's not a venture capital fund, but managers of regular people's pensions and index money. But 20–25% in one hand is already enough to decide almost any shareholder vote, because the remaining votes are scattered and not coordinated.

So the figure is inaccurate, but the conclusion - the capital market is no longer “a million independent investors evaluate the project” - is correct. I already wrote this before (file 09), I just confirm now with an exact number instead of your rounding.

---

#2. “The rich save too, this is capitalism”

Here you have caught the weak point in my example. I'll explain it simpler than last time.

I said: the rich are not afraid of need, and still do not give much - which means it is not a matter of fear for the future. Your objection: they save not out of fear, but because in capitalism money = power, and the more money you have, the more you decide. This is a separate, independent motive to save, which should not be in your scheme - there the balance does not give power over the plant.

I agree, this weakens my billionaire example. But there remains another, separate reason because of which people will still save, even without any power:

If I alone spend my share on a common cause, and a million others do not, the business will not gain or lose noticeably from this. And if I just leave the money in the account, I don’t risk anything and won’t look stupid if the project fails. This is not greed or a desire for power. It’s simple: why bother if nothing depends on me alone.

So I’m removing the example of billionaires, but the reason itself – “it’s easier and safer to save than to participate” – remains.

---

# 3. Purchased experts and vaccination

Here I will not examine the issue itself about Covid and vaccines - this is a live, controversial topic, and I have no reason to act as a judge of who was right. This is not an evasion, but a fair line: I don’t understand this better than any of us, and turning a document about the economic system into a debate about vaccines is pointless.

But the general mechanism that you describe is real and well-documented without any covid: studies paid for by a pharmaceutical company are statistically more likely to give a result beneficial to this company than independent studies on the same topic. This has been tested many times on different medications, it is not a conspiracy theory, it is a measurable effect.

What does this mean for our argument? In file 19 I wrote: today a purchased expert has counterweights - other experts, verification of results, responsibility for mistakes. Now I will be more honest: these counterweights work weakly and slowly, and often do not work on time at all. This is true.

But the difference with your scheme still remains, just less than I said. Your expert is deprived of the right to decide, but retains the right to speak - namely, based on what he says, the majority decides. All institutions that at least somehow (even poorly) check experts today are absent from your scheme. Weak verification is not the same as no verification.

---

#4. Responsibility and money

You say: now supposedly “a specific official is responsible,” but in reality no one is responsible, because power is protected by money. The example of 2008 shows this well: after the crisis, where fraud was documented by investigative commissions, almost none of the leaders of large banks went to prison.

This is true, and I have presented this argument incorrectly before. I'll be more precise:

- in a system where one designated person is responsible, punishment is possible, but often does not occur, because he has money and connections to defend himself;
- in your scheme, where forty million people made the decision, punishment is impossible in principle - there is simply no one to punish.

That is, I was wrong when I said that the first “works”. It works poorly and every now and then. But there is still a difference between “bad and every other time” and “never and in no way.” The first is a broken mechanism that is sometimes repaired. The second is not a mechanism at all.

#5. Ukraine

I will not analyze the topic itself - this is a living war with real people, and I will not undertake to assess who is right, whether it was lost or not, and what the government should do. This is not the place for this document and is not something I should pass judgment on.

But the general idea behind the example - that a government that has emergency powers and external support can act against the wishes of people and without a real opportunity to stop it - I analyzed in the very first file (item M16) and later (item U11). This is a long-known mechanism: a state of emergency almost never ends completely when the acute phase ends.

And here’s what’s important: your circuit has exactly the same hole, and without repair. The section about the army says directly that when attacked, the army acts without voting. Who decides that an attack occurred and when it ended? This is not indicated in the text. This means that the same mechanism that you are afraid of in the example with Ukraine is built into your system - it’s just that no one has tested it for strength yet.

So this example does not hit my criticism of the current system - it hits an unfinished place in yours.

#7. What follows from this - and what does not follow

This is where it is important not to confuse the two different conclusions.

**Follows:** the gap between your scheme and today's system on the question of “where the control mechanism works better” is less than I showed in files 07, 08, 13, 18, 19. Today's control mechanisms are weaker than stated, because money often bypasses them.

**It does not follow:** that this makes eight blockers disappear from file 15. They were not built on the statement “everything is fine today.” They are built on your schema itself:

- fifteen percent for maintenance versus two percent for voluntary donations - this is not a comparison with capitalism, this is a calculation of how much is needed and how much people actually give voluntarily;
- concentration through delegation - this follows from the fact that a person has little time, and not from the fact that capitalism is bad;
- lack of gender in the hidden infrastructure - Surfside, where money was not a problem at all, for no one;
- the hole in the question about the army is in your text, not in someone else’s.

Money in the current system does not work well as an instrument of power - I agree, I admit this honestly. But in your scheme, power does not disappear - it simply becomes the power of the one who is better at collecting attention, status and the majority. And this is also bought, only with a different currency, and this was what files 17 and 12 were about: reciprocity, status, the ability to mobilize people - this is not money, but it works in a similar way.

**In short: the debate “who has it worse - money or the majority” I partially concede to you. The debate “does your scheme work without specially built mechanisms” - no, I do not concede, because it does not depend on how bad the current system is.**
