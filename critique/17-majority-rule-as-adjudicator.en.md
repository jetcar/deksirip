# Part 17. The majority as the allocation mechanism

The proposed answer to conflicts over use is that the majority decides. This is more specific than leaving the adjudicator unnamed, and it may be fairer than a system where capital buys influence. But majority rule is complete only after two prior questions are answered.

## I. Who constructs the majority?

The wording of the question changes the result. “Give the site to project A?” differs from “A or B?” and from “A, B, or leave it undeveloped?” Every wording can be honest while producing a different majority. This is the agenda-setting problem.

The boundaries of the voting community also change the result. Who votes on a river site: the whole country, the region, or nearby residents? The boundary cannot be determined by that vote, because the electorate must already be known before the vote begins.

Thus “the majority decides” is not a complete specification. The person who formulates the question and defines the electorate has substantial power.

## II. Local majorities can become group ownership

If only local residents decide, they form a limited group with exclusive control over a common resource. The land was not produced by that village either. If everyone votes, the people directly affected may have little influence over their own surroundings. There is no cost-free boundary; the model must choose a scale and accept the trade-off.

Commons research treats clear boundaries, monitoring, and sanctions as necessary conditions for successful collective management. That means some form of bounded group authority must be acknowledged rather than assumed away.

## III. Two predictable majority pathologies

First, unwanted facilities are often placed where fewer voters live: waste sites, incinerators, treatment plants, or hazardous storage. A majority can work exactly as designed while systematically imposing costs on a weaker locality. Financial independence does not change the number of votes.

Second, existing residents can block new construction through local land-use decisions. Each vote is locally rational and democratic; the aggregate outcome can be a housing shortage. Real systems often respond by moving some land-use decisions to a higher level, narrowing the area governed by the immediate local majority.

This does not prove that majority rule is unacceptable. It shows that the model must specify compensation, appeals, higher-level rules, or some other correction instead of assuming that a bare majority automatically produces a fair outcome.

## IV. Independence reduces bribery, not influence

Material security can reduce direct vote buying. It does not remove influence; it changes its currency. If status, recognition, access to interesting projects, and mutual support are important incentives, they can be exchanged without appearing in a financial ledger.

“Support my project and I will support yours” may be sincere cooperation rather than bribery, yet it can still form durable blocs. A transparent balance does not automatically reveal social or reputational influence.

## V. Honest comparison

Current land-use decisions are not pure capital rule. They combine markets with zoning, public hearings, environmental review, compensation, and judicial appeal. Removing capital while also removing procedural limits is not simply “majority rule versus capital”; it is pure majority rule versus capital constrained by procedures.

Majority allocation may be fairer in many cases, especially where capital currently dominates. It can also be worse for minorities, invisible needs, and people who bear local costs. The model should therefore distinguish majority authority over resource allocation from unlimited permission to dispossess or silence those who lose a vote.

## Conclusion

Naming the majority as adjudicator is progress, not a complete mechanism. The agenda setter, electorate boundary, enforcement, compensation, and appeal procedure remain part of the power structure. Majority rule can be the final decision rule, but it cannot by itself define who votes, what is voted on, or how a decision is implemented.
