# Part 16. The foundation of ownership: no exclusive claim

The objection is not that a factory can be physically forbidden. One person cannot build it alone; if other people helped, on what basis does it become one person's property? The same applies to land: it is common, yet a piece suddenly becomes someone's permanently. Knowledge is also cumulative: no important work is created by one person in isolation.

## I. A serious normative tradition

This is not an amateur position. Herbert Simon estimated that roughly 90% of income in a rich country reflects social inheritance — institutions, infrastructure, and accumulated knowledge. Rawls argued that the distribution of natural abilities is morally arbitrary. Locke's proviso allows appropriation from the common only while enough remains for others; this condition is difficult to satisfy for land. Henry George built a continuing tradition around that problem. The history of simultaneous discoveries — Newton and Leibniz, Darwin and Wallace, Bell and Gray — also supports the claim that individual contributions depend on a large common stock.

As a normative argument against exclusive claims to means of production and land, this position is substantial.

## II. What the argument establishes

It establishes that nobody has an automatically justified moral claim to exclusive ownership of a factory or plot of land. It does **not** establish that nobody will attempt exclusive control, or that disputes over use can be avoided.

Suppose two hundred people build and operate a factory and decide that they alone control it. If society does nothing, exclusive control exists in fact. If society prevents it, society uses organized coercion. Ownership is therefore not only a moral description; it is also a description of whose claim prevails when uses conflict.

The precise objection is:

> A system needs organized coercion not merely because it bans something, but because it must settle conflicting claims to use resources, and settlement requires an executor. This is an engineering requirement of every economic system.

## III. The symmetry problem

“You did not create this alone” also applies to the individual's accumulated dividend. The dividend is produced by society, infrastructure, knowledge, and other people's work just as a factory is. Yet directing that share is the central decentralizing mechanism.

The argument therefore does not distinguish exclusive factory ownership from exclusive direction of one's personal share. A better boundary is not the origin of the good, but what control over it enables.

Personal property is consumed or used for one's own purposes. Private ownership of means of production gives command over other people's labour. A personal share lets its holder choose consumption and projects without commanding another person; factory ownership determines the conditions under which others work. This distinction protects the proposed model more effectively.

## IV. Land and the unavoidable allocation problem

The argument is strongest for land: nobody produced it, and land is finite and non-substitutable. But common land creates an allocation duty. Two projects may want the same river site; citizens must decide, and a decision must be implemented.

Common ownership is not itself a management solution. Working commons require boundaries, monitoring, rules, and sanctions. If land rent belongs to everyone, collecting it also requires a compulsory charge or another compulsory allocation rule. This consequence must be accepted explicitly.

## V. Average versus marginal contribution

The claim that an individual's contribution is less than one percent can be true for the **average** value of a product: most of a medicine rests on accumulated science, tools, institutions, and infrastructure. But decisions concern the **marginal** event. The last team may add little to average value while determining whether this particular medicine exists at all.

The two claims are compatible only if the distinction is explicit: an individual's share of created value may be small while their role in making the event happen is decisive.

## Conclusion

The anti-exclusive-ownership argument is serious and useful, especially for land. It does not solve disputes over use or eliminate the need for an accountable executor. The normative basis and the operating mechanism are separate questions: one may reject exclusive ownership while still needing a procedure that decides who may use a resource.
